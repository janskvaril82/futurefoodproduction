Accessibility statement

Future Food Production should work for every student — with a mouse or a keyboard, a screen reader or a magnifier, on a large screen or a phone, in light or dark mode, and on paper. This statement describes the standard the site aims for, how you can use it, what we know does not yet work well, the alternatives that exist, and how to tell us about a problem.

Standard and legal framework

The site aims to meet level AA of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2, the W3C Recommendation published in October 2023 (current edition dated 12 December 2024). WCAG organises its requirements under four principles — content must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust — which Figure 1 relates to this course.

In the European Union, Directive (EU) 2016/2102 on the accessibility of the websites and mobile applications of public sector bodies requires such bodies to make their websites perceivable, operable, understandable and robust, and to publish an accessibility statement that explains any inaccessible content, offers a feedback mechanism and links to an enforcement procedure (Article 7). Compliance is presumed through the harmonised European standard EN 301 549, which builds on WCAG 2.1. In Sweden the directive is implemented by the Act (2018:1937) on accessibility to digital public services (the DOS Act), in force since 1 January 2019 and supervised by the Agency for Digital Government (Digg). Where this course is offered by a public university, these rules apply to it; the site aims at the newer WCAG 2.2, which includes the requirements of WCAG 2.1 except the parsing criterion 4.1.1 that WCAG 2.2 removed. This statement follows the structure of the EU model accessibility statement (Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2018/1523).

Motion, zoom, themes and print

  • Reduced motion. If you turn on “reduce motion” in your operating system, the page styles switch off transitions, animations and smooth scrolling, and animated changes such as the rearrangement of the frontiers radar happen instantly. 3D scenes stop rendering when they are scrolled out of view.
  • Zoom and small screens. The layout reflows for narrow windows and browser zoom; side menus fold away and wide tables scroll inside their own frame instead of widening the page.
  • Light and dark themes. The site follows your system setting; the moon and sun button in the header switches theme and remembers your choice. The dark theme has the higher contrast for secondary text (see Known limitations).
  • Print. “Print lesson” opens every hidden derivation and solution before printing, so that a lesson can be studied entirely on paper.
  • No time limits. Nothing on the site is timed; the optional exam mode of the practice generator only reports how long you took, afterwards.

Colour and contrast

Colour is used to support meaning, not to carry it alone: quiz feedback says “Correct” or “Not quite”, chart series have legends and labels, and every blip on the frontiers radar carries a number and a name as well as its sector colour. The colours of the radar’s sectors were checked with a colour-vision-deficiency simulation so that neighbouring sectors stay distinguishable. Heat maps use perceptually uniform colour maps such as viridis by default; the red–yellow–green maps that are conventional for vegetation indices are hard to read with red–green colour blindness, so read them with their colour bar and numbers (see conventions). Body text meets the 4.5:1 contrast ratio in both themes; the exceptions for secondary text in the light theme are listed under Known limitations.

Screen readers and other assistive technology

  • Structure. Every page has landmarks (header, navigation, main content, footer), one main heading and a logical heading hierarchy; lessons and guides have an automatic table of contents.
  • Equations are typeset with KaTeX, which places MathML alongside the visual formula for screen readers. In addition, every equation box contains the equation “read aloud” in words and a table explaining each symbol and its unit.
  • Diagrams drawn for the course are inline SVG images written with a text description and a numbered caption; photographs carry alternative text and a credit line.
  • Controls are native buttons, links, sliders and form fields with visible labels; each slider shows its current value with the unit next to it (screen readers announce the number; the unit is in the label or the visible value).
  • Quizzes use ordinary radio buttons, check boxes and text fields; feedback appears directly below the question.
  • Status messages — such as “Saved to your notebook” — are announced through a live region.

3D and interactive content: the alternatives

The 3D laboratories are drawn with WebGL on a canvas, which a screen reader cannot describe and which needs a reasonably capable graphics processor. The course is designed so that no learning outcome depends on seeing a 3D scene. Every laboratory presents the same model in several other forms:

  1. Readouts give the key results as numbers with units, next to the scene.
  2. Charts show how the results change; their values are also given in readouts, tables or the text, and several laboratories offer a CSV download of the data.
  3. The Explain and Derive tabs describe the system and derive every model equation in text, with 2D diagrams that follow the course conventions.
  4. The Investigate tab poses the tasks in words, and the quiz can be answered from the readouts.
  5. The Assumptions & sources tab lists what the model leaves out.

If a 3D scene is slow or does not display on your computer, you can still complete the laboratory from the controls, readouts, charts and tabs. If you need the content in another format — for example as a printed task sheet or as tables of results — ask your teacher.

Known limitations

The following parts of the site do not yet fully meet WCAG 2.2 level AA. They are listed so that you know what to expect and what alternatives exist; they are reviewed each term.

  • 3D scenes and canvas charts (WCAG 1.1.1) are not described in detail to screen readers. Alternatives: readouts, text and tables as described above.
  • Animated simulations (2.2.2) keep running when the “reduce motion” setting is on, because they are the content itself. Use the pause control of the simulation clock, or the Space key where a laboratory defines it; 3D scenes pause when scrolled out of view.
  • Contrast of secondary text in the light theme (1.4.3): amber text, used for some diagram labels and warning badges, reaches about 3.7:1 on the page background, below the required 4.5:1 for small text (grey captions and metadata reach about 5.6:1). The dark theme exceeds 6:1 for the same elements; switch themes with the button in the header.
  • Tabs in the laboratories are ordinary buttons reached with Tab; the arrow-key navigation between tabs that some screen-reader users expect is not yet provided.
  • Slider values are announced by screen readers as bare numbers; the unit is given in the visible label and value but not yet in the announcement (a value text for assistive technology is planned).
  • Small secondary buttons on equation boxes are below the 24-pixel target size (2.5.8).
  • Dragging (2.5.7): in some 2D laboratories a point on a chart can be dragged; not every such value may yet have a slider or number field as an alternative.
  • Focus under the sticky header (2.4.11): see Table 1.
  • External content — journal articles, PDFs and websites linked as sources — is outside the control of the course and may not be accessible.
  • Assistive-technology testing: the site has not yet been tested systematically with screen readers such as NVDA, JAWS or VoiceOver, and no independent audit has been made.

How accessibility is checked

This statement is based on a self-assessment by the course author. During development every page is loaded in a headless browser that reports script errors, broken links and images, and captures the layout at desktop and phone widths in both light and dark themes. Components are built from native HTML elements with visible labels, keyboard support is designed into each component, and the colour palettes of charts are checked with a colour-vision-deficiency simulation. The contrast figures above were calculated from the theme colours with the WCAG formula. The statement was prepared on 27 September 2026 and is reviewed each term, together with the frontiers radar.

Report a problem or ask for another format

If something on the site does not work for you, or you need material in another format, please tell us — every report helps the next student as well.

  1. Describe the problem: the page address (copy it from the address bar), what you tried to do and what happened, and your device, browser and any assistive technology you use.
  2. Send it to your course teacher through your university’s learning platform or course room, or raise it in a seminar or laboratory session. Your notebook (press N) is a convenient place to draft the description.
  3. We will reply, fix the problem where we can, and meanwhile provide the content in an accessible alternative form.
If you are not satisfied with the response

For websites of Swedish public bodies, the Agency for Digital Government (Digg) supervises compliance with the Act on accessibility to digital public services. You can contact Digg about accessibility shortcomings that have not been resolved — see Digg: the DOS Act and Digg’s regulations. If you have a disability, your university’s study-support service can also arrange support such as alternative formats or adapted examinations; contact it early in the course.
