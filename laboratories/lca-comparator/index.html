LCA comparator

Which kilogram of lettuce on a shelf in Västerås or Stockholm has the smallest footprint — one trucked from a field in southern Spain, one from a Swedish field, one from a heated Swedish greenhouse, or one from a vertical farm down the road? Build the life-cycle inventory yourself, switch grids and heat sources, and watch five supply chains, four impact indicators, the break-even grid intensity and a Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis respond in real time.

A guided tour of five supply chains

The stage shows five ways to put one kilogram of lettuce on a retailer's shelf in the Mälardalen region. Lettuce crates (green dots) travel from each farm through cultivation and packing to the retailer. The size of each node is its contribution to the chosen indicator; the puffs rising from it are proportional to that contribution. Hover over a lane for its full breakdown.

  1. A knife-edge in January. With the default inventory, imported Spanish lettuce comes to about 0.68 kg CO₂e per kg delivered, of which the 3,500 km truck journey is more than half. The vertical farm on the Swedish production mix (35 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹) lands at about 0.69 — almost exactly the same. The break-even chart shows why: the break-even intensity EF* is about 34 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹, and the Swedish mix sits right on it.
  2. Change the accounting of the grid. Choose the Swedish consumption mix (49 g, used in the IVL study of a Stockholm vertical farm) or the EU-27 average: the vertical farm rises to 0.86 and 2.9 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹. Choose a renewable power-purchase agreement: it falls to about 0.39 and wins. Nothing about the farm changed — only the electricity dataset.
  3. Look at the coal-grid farm. The same farm on the Polish mix (608 g) emits about 7.8 kg CO₂e per kg — more than ten times the import. The lane is identical; only the power station behind the ⚡ line differs.
  4. Heat the greenhouse differently. With a natural-gas boiler and 8 kWh of heat per kg (a January value), the Swedish greenhouse emits about 2.6 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ — heat is 84 % of it. Switch to wood chips (≈ 0.60) or Västerås district heating (≈ 0.79): the same greenhouse becomes competitive with the import. This is the pattern Stoessel et al. (2012) and Hospido et al. (2009) found: local only wins without fossil heat.
  5. Go to July. Swedish field lettuce appears (≈ 0.27 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹) and wins clearly; the greenhouse needs little heat or light. Seasonal local produce is the robust low-carbon choice.
  6. Change the indicator. Switch to Water and Land: the vertical farm now wins by one to two orders of magnitude (about 2 L and 0.002 m²·yr per kg against 56 L and 0.28 m²·yr for Spanish field lettuce). Switch to Energy: it loses by almost an order of magnitude (≈ 88 MJ against ≈ 10 MJ). The trade-off chart at the bottom shows all four at once.
  7. Ask how sure you are. The Monte Carlo bar chart turns the knife-edge of step 1 into a probability: in January the import, the vertical farm and — with renewable heat — the greenhouse each win a substantial share of plausible states of the world. Raise the uncertainty width and watch the probabilities converge; narrow it and they diverge.
Key idea

A comparative LCA answers a precise question — here: which way of delivering 1 kg of lettuce to a retailer in Västerås/Stockholm causes the least impact, under stated assumptions? The answer depends on three things far more than on “local versus imported”: the electricity dataset of energy-intensive systems, the heat source of greenhouses, and the season. Distance matters mainly when the mode is air freight.

Goal, scope and functional unit

The functional unit is 1 kg of lettuce delivered to a retailer in Västerås or Stockholm. The system boundary runs from the cradle (fertiliser, fuel and electricity production) through cultivation, packing and transport to the retailer's door; retail storage, the household and the end-of-life of the lettuce are outside it. Why the grid decides the vertical farm

A vertical farm replaces free sunlight with purchased electricity — here 12 kWh per kilogram, within the 8–12.5 kWh kg⁻¹ of a recent review (Pennisi et al., 2025) and a little below the ≈ 15 kWh kg⁻¹ of Casey et al. (2022). Multiply by the grid emission factor and electricity is between 34 % (wind) and 97 % (coal) of the footprint. The emission factors in this lab are life-cycle intensities of generation for 2024 from Ember via Our World in Data: Sweden 35, Spain 146, EU-27 211 and Poland 608 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹; the Swedish consumption mix of 49 g (including imports) is the value used by Molin and Martin (2018); a wind power-purchase agreement is represented by the IPCC median life-cycle intensity of onshore wind, 11 g (Schlömer et al., 2014). Which one is right is an accounting decision (location-based average, market-based contract or consequential marginal mix — see Lesson 15.1), and the model lets you see its consequence directly.

Energy is not the same as carbon

Switch the indicator to Energy and compare the Swedish and the coal-grid vertical farm: 88 against 110 MJ per kg — only 24 % apart, although their GWP differs elevenfold. Nuclear power has a high primary-energy factor (three units of heat per unit of electricity) but a low carbon intensity, so a nuclear-and-hydro grid can use a lot of primary energy with few emissions. CED measures resource use; GWP measures climate impact. They answer different questions.

Heat: the hidden giant of Nordic greenhouses

A glass greenhouse at 59° N loses heat through its cover for most of the year. For year-round production Swedish greenhouses need roughly 120–388 kWh of heat per m² and year (Nilsson & Nimmermark, as summarised by Molin & Martin, 2018); per kilogram of winter lettuce this becomes several kWh. The comparator uses 8 kWh kg⁻¹ in January and 1 kWh kg⁻¹ in July, and three heat sources per kWh of delivered heat: a wood-chip boiler, 0.023 kg CO₂e (biogenic CO₂ treated as neutral); the Västerås district-heating network, 0.045 kg CO₂e (Mälarenergi's 2025 value, fossil share 0.5 %); and a natural-gas boiler, 0.262 kg CO₂e. The factor of eleven between gas and wood chips is larger than any difference in transport.

Losses multiply everything

Lettuce is perishable. If a fraction ℓ of the harvest is lost before it reaches the retailer, every kilogram delivered has to carry the burden of 1/(1 − ℓ) kg grown, packed and transported. With 10 % losses on the long Spanish chain every impact rises by 11 %; with 3 % on the short vertical-farm chain by 3 %. Globally about 14 % of food is lost between harvest and retail (FAO, 2019).

Uncertainty: why triangular distributions?

For most inventory items we know a plausible minimum, a most likely value and a plausible maximum — for instance electricity use of vertical farms from 8 to 15 kWh kg⁻¹ with a mode near 12. A triangular distribution encodes exactly these three numbers and nothing more, which makes it the standard choice when data are too scarce for a fitted distribution. The Monte Carlo analysis draws every uncertain value, recomputes all systems and records which one is lowest. Factors that several systems share — the Swedish grid factor, the truck factor, the packaging factor, the N₂O emission factor — are drawn once per run for all systems. This paired sampling removes noise that would otherwise blur a real difference (Heijungs, 2020).

Common misconception

“Local food has a lower footprint because it travels less.” In this model the 3,500 km truck journey adds about 0.4 kg CO₂e per kg — a lot for a vegetable, yet less than heating a greenhouse with gas in January (2.1 kg CO₂e per kg) or running a vertical farm on an average EU grid (2.5). Distance becomes decisive only with air freight (≈ 2.7 kg CO₂e per kg over 3,000 km).

Frontier 2024–2026

Newer LCAs of controlled-environment agriculture use hourly electricity factors, because a farm that lights its crop at night, when wind supplies a larger share of the Nordic grid, has a different footprint from one that lights by day. Combined with demand response, the farm becomes a flexible load that can soak up surplus renewable power — a consequential benefit that an annual average factor cannot show.

The equations behind the comparator

The model is a linear LCA: nine short equations. All impacts are per kilogram delivered to the retailer; inventory amounts \(a_i\) are per kilogram harvested.

Footprint per kilogram delivered(A1)
\[ I_c = \frac{1}{1-\ell}\sum_{i} a_i\,\text{CF}_{i,c} \]

the impact in indicator c per kilogram delivered is the sum over all inventory items of the amount needed per kilogram harvested times its characterisation factor, divided by the fraction of the harvest that survives to the retailer.

\(I_c\)impact in indicator \(c\) (GWP, CED, water, land) per kg deliveredkg CO₂e, MJ, L or m²·yr per kg
\(a_i\)amount of inventory item \(i\) per kg harvested (kWh, g N, L, t·km …)item unit kg⁻¹
\(\text{CF}_{i,c}\)characterisation or emission factor of item \(i\) for indicator \(c\)impact per item unit
\(\ell\)fraction of the harvest lost between the field or farm and the retailer–
Follow the derivation
  1. LCA models are linear: each unit process scales with its output, so impacts are sums of activity × factor (Lesson 15.1, Eq. 15.1.1).
  2. To deliver 1 kg when a fraction \(\ell\) is lost, the farm must harvest \(H\) with \((1-\ell)H = 1\), so \(H = 1/(1-\ell)\).
  3. Every harvested kilogram carries \(\sum_i a_i\text{CF}_{i,c}\); multiplying by \(H\) gives Eq. A1. The contribution chart shows the terms \(a_i\text{CF}_{i,c}\) and, separately, the loss surcharge \(\big(\tfrac{1}{1-\ell}-1\big)\sum_i a_i\text{CF}_{i,c}\).
  4. Example (Spanish field, GWP): the items sum to 0.613 kg CO₂e per kg harvested; with \(\ell = 0.10\) the footprint per kg delivered is \(0.613/0.9 = 0.681\).
Assumptions and validity

All lost lettuce is assumed to have been grown, packed and transported (a conservative choice for transport). Losses are not credited with any end-of-life benefit (composting or biogas). Linearity means no economies of scale inside the model.

\(\dfrac{1}{(-)}\cdot\dfrac{\text{item}}{\text{kg}_{\text{harvested}}}\cdot\dfrac{\text{impact}}{\text{item}}\) = impact per kg delivered, because \((1-\ell)\) has units kg delivered per kg harvested ✓
Electricity: carbon and primary energy of a grid mix(A2)
\[ G_{\text{el}} = e_{\text{el}}\,\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}, \qquad \text{CED}_{\text{el}} = e_{\text{el}}\,\text{PEF}, \qquad \text{PEF} = f_{\text{loss}}\cdot 3.6\sum_{k}\frac{s_k}{\eta_k} \]

the climate impact of electricity is the electricity used per kilogram times the grid emission factor; its primary energy is the electricity times the primary-energy factor, which is 3.6 MJ per kWh times the sum over generation technologies of their share divided by their conversion efficiency, raised for grid losses and fuel supply.

Follow the derivation
  1. One kWh is 3.6 MJ. Producing it from a fuel with efficiency \(\eta\) requires \(3.6/\eta\) MJ of primary energy.
  2. For hydro, wind and solar the physical-energy-content convention counts the electricity itself as primary energy (\(\eta = 1\)); for nuclear the heat released in the reactor is counted, with \(\eta = 0.33\).
  3. A mix is the share-weighted sum: \(3.6\sum_k s_k/\eta_k\). Sweden 2024 (37.5 % hydro, 29.4 % nuclear, 23.5 % wind, 5.9 % biomass …): \(3.6\times1.757 = 6.33\) MJ kWh⁻¹, and 6.83 with \(f_{\text{loss}}\). Poland (54 % coal): 8.56 MJ kWh⁻¹. A wind contract: 3.89 MJ kWh⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

The energy-harvested approach of Frischknecht et al. (2015) counts slightly more for hydro and wind (the kinetic or potential energy converted), and efficiencies differ between plants. Emission factors are annual averages of generation; consumption mixes with trade, residual mixes and marginal factors are different accounting choices (Lesson 15.1).

Assumptions and validity

Biogenic CO₂ from wood is treated as climate-neutral, which assumes regrowth within the time horizon. Primary energy of district heat (2.4 MJ kWh⁻¹) is a model assumption that treats waste-derived heat as burden-free.

Break-even grid intensity, with losses(A8)
\[ \text{EF}^{*} = \frac{(1-\ell_{\text{VF}})\,G_{\text{imp}} - g_0}{e_{\text{el}}} \]

the grid intensity below which the vertical farm beats the imported lettuce equals the import's footprint per kilogram delivered, converted to a per-kilogram-harvested basis for the farm, minus the farm's non-electric footprint, divided by its electricity use.

Monte Carlo with triangular distributions(A9)
\[ x = \begin{cases} a + \sqrt{u\,(b-a)(c-a)} & u < \dfrac{c-a}{b-a}\\[4pt] b - \sqrt{(1-u)(b-a)(b-c)} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad \hat P_s = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\!\left[G_s^{(j)} = \min_k G_k^{(j)}\right] \]

a uniform random number u between 0 and 1 is turned into a triangular random value between the minimum a and maximum b with mode c by inverting the cumulative distribution; the probability that system s is lowest is the fraction of the N runs in which its footprint is the smallest of all systems.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a, c, b\)minimum, mode and maximum of an uncertain input (the mode is the default value)input unit
\(u\)uniform random number on (0, 1)–
\(\hat P_s\)estimated probability that system \(s\) has the lowest footprint–
\(N\)number of Monte Carlo runs–
Follow the derivation
  1. The triangular density rises linearly from \(a\) to \(c\) and falls to \(b\); its area is 1, so its peak height is \(2/(b-a)\).
  2. The cumulative probability up to \(x \le c\) is the area of a triangle: \(F(x) = (x-a)^2/[(b-a)(c-a)]\). At \(x=c\), \(F = (c-a)/(b-a)\).
  3. Set \(F(x) = u\) and solve: \(x = a + \sqrt{u(b-a)(c-a)}\). The upper branch follows in the same way from \(1-F(x) = (b-x)^2/[(b-a)(b-c)]\). This inverse-transform method turns any uniform random number into the desired distribution.
  4. Each run is a Bernoulli trial for “system \(s\) is lowest”, so the standard error of \(\hat P_s\) is \(\sqrt{\hat P_s(1-\hat P_s)/N}\) — the formula of Lesson 15.1 (Eq. 15.1.9).
Assumptions and validity

Inputs are drawn independently except for shared factors, which are drawn once per run (paired comparison). Scenario choices — the grid basis, the heat source, the season — are not sampled: they are separate experiments that you choose with the controls.

\(\hat P\) and its standard error are dimensionless ✓

Model assumptions

  • Functional unit and boundary: 1 kg of lettuce delivered to a retailer in Västerås/Stockholm; cradle to retailer; attributional, average data. Retail, household and end-of-life of the lettuce are excluded.
  • Inventory defaults (per kg harvested) are teaching values chosen within published ranges and calibrated so that the totals reproduce the teaching model of Lesson 15.1 (import ≈ 0.62 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ before losses; vertical-farm non-electric footprint ≈ 0.25). Yields: Spanish field 3.9 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (arid-region winter lettuce, Barbosa et al., 2015); Swedish field 3.3 (UK field, Gargaro et al., 2025); greenhouse 41 (hydroponic greenhouse, Barbosa et al., 2015); vertical farm 115 kg per m² of cultivation area (Zhuang et al., 2022) with five growing layers per m² of land (assumption). Electricity: vertical farm 12 kWh kg⁻¹ (Pennisi et al., 2025; Casey et al., 2022; Graamans et al., 2018); greenhouse 3.5 (January) and 0.8 (July) kWh kg⁻¹; field 0.2–0.3 kWh kg⁻¹ (order of magnitude of the 1,100 kJ kg⁻¹ of conventional lettuce in Barbosa et al., 2015). Heat: 8 kWh kg⁻¹ in January (Lesson 15.1) and 1 kWh kg⁻¹ in July (assumption). Nitrogen: field 3–3.8 g N kg⁻¹ (≈ 100–150 kg N ha⁻¹), soilless 2.2–2.5 g N kg⁻¹ (close to crop uptake). Diesel 0.005–0.006 L kg⁻¹ (≈ 200 L ha⁻¹). Substrate, seeds, CO₂ and infrastructure are a lumped GWP term (0.08–0.19 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹). Packaging 10 g of plastic film per kg. Water: see Eq. A7. Losses: 10 % (Spain), 7 % (Swedish field), 5 % (greenhouse), 3 % (vertical farm) — scenario assumptions.
  • Emission factors: grid intensities and generation shares for 2024 from Ember via Our World in Data (accessed September 2026): Sweden 34.9, Spain 146.2, EU-27 211.2, Poland 608.2 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹; Swedish consumption mix 49 g (IVL, used by Molin & Martin, 2018); onshore wind 11 g and coal 820 g (IPCC medians, Schlömer et al., 2014). Heat: natural gas and wood chips from DESNZ (2025) via Lesson 15.1; district heating 44.9 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹ delivered (Mälarenergi, Västerås, 2025). Truck 0.110, rail 0.028, ship 0.013, air 0.90 (with radiative forcing) kg CO₂e t⁻¹ km⁻¹ (DESNZ 2025 via Lesson 15.1). Nitrogen fertiliser production 4 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ N (illustrative, Lesson 1.3); field N₂O by IPCC Tier 1 (2019 Refinement). Diesel 3.3 kg CO₂e L⁻¹ (2.68 combustion, Lesson 1.3, plus 24 % for supply, the same proportion as the DESNZ truck factors). Plastic film 5.0 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ (≈ 1.9 production + 3.1 from incineration of its fossil carbon: 0.857 × 44/12).
  • Cumulative energy demand uses primary-energy factors computed from the 2024 generation shares with efficiencies of 1 (hydro, wind, solar), 0.33 (nuclear), 0.37 (coal), 0.50 (gas), 0.35 (oil) and 0.30 (biomass electricity), plus 8 % for losses and fuel supply; heat 4.4 (gas), 4.45 (wood chips) and 2.4 (district heat, waste heat counted as burden-free) MJ kWh⁻¹; nitrogen 40 MJ kg⁻¹ N; diesel 43 MJ L⁻¹; plastic 80 MJ kg⁻¹; other inputs 15 MJ per kg CO₂e. These are approximations for teaching; a database LCA (e.g. ecoinvent) would give different decimals.
  • Water and land are direct, on-site quantities only (no water or land embodied in electricity, heat, fertiliser, packaging or buildings).
  • The coal-grid vertical farm is a counterfactual: the identical farm and delivery distance, powered by a coal-heavy mix. It isolates the effect of the grid.
  • Monte Carlo: triangular distributions with the default as mode and relative half-widths: yield 15 %, layers 25 %, electricity 25 %, heat 30 %, nitrogen 20 %, diesel 30 %, other inputs 40 %, packaging 30 %, water 30 %, distance 10 %, losses 50 %; shared factors: Swedish grid ×0.7–1.5, Spanish grid ×0.8–1.25, coal grid ×0.85–1.15, N₂O factor ×0.3–2, nitrogen production ±25 %, packaging ±30 %, transport modes ±20 %, heat factor ±8 % (gas), ±30 % (district heat), ±50 % (wood chips). All widths are scaled by the uncertainty-width slider.
