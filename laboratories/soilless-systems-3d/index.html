A guided tour

  1. Start with all six. The crop is shown on the day selected under Crop and climate (24 days after transplanting, when an unstressed butterhead weighs about 150 g). The readouts summarise one critical number for each system and colour it green, amber or red. Press ▶ Grow the crop and watch the Supply ÷ demand chart: every line falls as the plants grow, because demand grows and some supplies shrink.
  2. NFT. Click the NFT system. Blue particles leave the submersible pump in the black reservoir, climb to the feed manifold and run down the two gullies as a film about a millimetre deep; the front gully has a clear lid and no front wall, so you can see the root mats lying in the film. The film is fast (≈ 0.13 m s⁻¹) and the gully short, so oxygen hardly falls — the NFT channel simulator shows what happens in 10–30 m commercial gullies.
  3. Deep-water culture. The raft floats on 44 litres of solution; bubbles rise from two air stones fed by the air pump through a check valve. Set the air flow to 0.2 L min⁻¹ and read the DO probe; then increase the solution temperature to 28 °C. Note how many hours the crop would survive if the air pump stopped.
  4. Ebb-and-flow. The tray floods for a few minutes (30 × real time): the pump fills it through the fitting in the floor, the overflow standpipe limits the depth, and when the timer switches the pump off the water drains back through the pump. Reduce the floods per day to 2 and watch the water margin between floods.
  5. Drip on rock wool. Pressure-compensating emitters deliver short pulses through drip stakes into stone-wool blocks; the surplus drains out of the slab into the gutter and a measuring bucket. Compare the drain fraction early in the crop and at harvest.
  6. Wick. Cotton wicks lift solution by capillarity from the reservoir into the substrate. As the plants grow and the reservoir level falls, the lift grows and the wick delivers less: the upper part of the wick turns pale and the supply ÷ demand label turns red in the last days of the crop. Try four wicks, or a fuller reservoir.
  7. Kratky. No pump at all. The level falls as the plants drink, and the growing air gap under the lid fills with a mat of “oxygen roots”, while the “water roots” follow the solution down. Halve the initial solution depth and see when the tote runs dry.
Key idea

Every soilless system is a set of buffers and fluxes (Lesson 5.1). Water, nutrients and oxygen reach the roots from a buffer — a reservoir, a film, a slab, a wick, the air — at a rate the hardware must sustain as the crop grows. Pumped systems buy control and compactness with electricity and with short survival times when the power fails; passive systems (wick, Kratky) are silent and robust but can only deliver what capillarity or a stored volume allows.

Six answers to the same three questions

Every soilless system must answer three questions: how does water with nutrients reach the roots, how do the roots get oxygen, and what happens when something stops? The six systems in this gallery answer them differently (Lesson 5.1, Figure 2; Raviv et al., 2019, give the full engineering treatment).

Pumped solution: NFT and deep-water culture

In NFT a film a millimetre or two deep flows down a sloping gully; its thickness follows Nusselt's falling-film law and grows only with the cube root of the flow (Lesson 5.4). Commercial guidance is about 1 L min⁻¹ per gully on slopes of 1:30 to 1:100 (Graves, 1983). The film is replaced within seconds, so oxygen and nutrients change little along a short gully — but it holds only about 40 mL of water per plant, so a stopped pump is an emergency. In deep-water culture the roots hang in tens of litres of solution. The buffer of water is enormous; the limiting resource is oxygen. An aquarium pump that pushes 2 L of air per minute through a stone dissolves only about 2 % of the oxygen it carries, yet that is enough to keep a lettuce crop near saturation (Lesson 5.3). The Cornell raft system keeps about 7–8 mg L⁻¹ and notes stress below 3–4 mg L⁻¹ (Brechner & Both, 2013).

Periodic wetting: ebb-and-flow and drip

Substrate systems store water in the pores of the growing medium and refill them periodically. An ebb-and-flow tray is flooded by the pump until the overflow standpipe limits the depth, then drains back by gravity; while the water leaves, air is sucked into the substrate. The drain time follows Torricelli's law for an orifice, so a small drain opening can keep roots submerged much longer than intended. In drip systems each plant receives short pulses from an emitter; growers apply more than the crop uses so that a drain fraction of 10–40 % leaches accumulating salts (Lesson 5.1). A fixed schedule is therefore wasteful early in the crop, when small plants transpire little, and may be insufficient on hot days near harvest — the reason commercial growers control irrigation by accumulated radiation or by drain measurements.

Passive systems: wick and Kratky

A wick lifts solution by the same capillary forces that set the height of water in a thin tube (Jurin's law, Eq. 5.1.1). But the equilibrium rise is reached only when nothing flows. To carry a flux the wick needs a gradient of suction along its length, and the wetter it must stay to conduct water, the smaller that gradient can be. Knutson and Selker (1994) measured this for fibreglass wicks: their unsaturated conductivity falls exponentially with suction. For such a material, Gardner's (1958) steady-state solution gives the flux that can be lifted over a height Z (Eq. S6) — high when the lift is small compared with the wick's capillary length, and collapsing when the lift approaches it. That is the physical reason wick systems suit herbs and seedlings but struggle with a lettuce close to harvest, whose transpiration is several times larger.

The Kratky method avoids every moving part (Kratky, 2009). The tote is filled once; as the plants transpire, the level falls and a humid air space opens under the lid, where roots grow that take oxygen from the air. The only design question is volume: the tote must hold the water the crop will use, with a margin (Eq. 5.1.2). Kratky reports 4–8 L of solution per lettuce, of which a 150–250 g head uses 3–6 L.

Common misconception

“Hydroponics always saves water.” Only the water that passes through the plants is productive. Open drip systems deliberately drain 10–40 %; small systems throw away their reservoir at the end of each crop; a Kratky tote may be emptied with a quarter of its solution still in it. Switch Count solution discarded at harvest on and off and compare the water-use chart with the 20 L kg⁻¹ modelled for a hydroponic greenhouse by Barbosa et al. (2015) and the 11–20 L kg⁻¹ Kratky (2009) measured.

Energy and risk

Electricity use is set not by the laws of hydraulics — the hydraulic power of a small pump is only a few watts — but by how long the pump runs and how many plants share it. A 20 W pump running continuously for twelve NFT plants costs far more per kilogram than the same pump running a few minutes a day for an ebb-and-flow tray. Risk follows the same “buffer over rate” logic as in Lesson 5.1 (Eq. 5.1.8): the time a crop survives a power cut is the water or oxygen stored per plant divided by the rate at which the plant consumes it. At harvest a lettuce transpires about 20 mL an hour: the 40 mL left in an NFT gully lasts two hours, whereas the 17 L still in the Kratky tote at harvest last about six days — without any electricity at all.

The models behind the six systems

All six systems grow the same lettuce crop (S8). Each system then limits the supply of water or oxygen in its own way (S1–S7), and the comparison uses three system-level indicators (S9). Numbers in the tables are the gallery defaults at harvest.

NFT: film thickness, velocity and residence time(S1)
\[ h=\left(\frac{3\,\mu\,Q}{\rho\,g\,W\sin\theta}\right)^{1/3},\qquad \bar u=\frac{Q}{W h},\qquad t_{\text{res}}=\frac{L}{\bar u} \]

the depth of the film is the cube root of three times the viscosity times the flow per unit width, divided by density, gravity and the sine of the slope; the mean velocity is the flow divided by the film cross-section, and the residence time is the gully length divided by the velocity.

\[ \begin{gathered} k_La=\frac{E_{O_2}\,\rho_{O_2,air}\,Q_{air}}{V\,C^*}\,\theta^{T-20} \\[6pt] C_{ss}=C^*-\frac{n\,R_{O_2}/V}{k_La},\qquad \tau_{fail}=\frac{C_{ss}-C_{crit}}{n\,R_{O_2}/V} \end{gathered} \]

the volumetric transfer coefficient is the oxygen actually dissolved from the air stream divided by the tank volume and the saturation concentration, corrected for temperature; the steady oxygen concentration lies below saturation by the uptake rate per litre divided by that coefficient; and after an air-pump failure the dissolved oxygen falls to the critical level after the stored surplus divided by the uptake rate.

Worked example 1How long does the DWC crop survive a power cut?

The gallery DWC tote holds 44 L of solution at 22 °C (\(C^*\) = 8.74 mg L⁻¹) for six 150 g lettuces that each respire 8.2 mg O₂ h⁻¹. An aquarium pump pushes 2 L min⁻¹ of air through the stones, of which 2 % of the oxygen dissolves. (a) What is \(k_La\)? (b) What DO does the tank settle at? (c) How long after the pump stops does DO fall to 3 mg L⁻¹?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Oxygen dissolved into O₂-free water: \(\text{OTR}_{\max} = 0.02\times279\ \text{mg L}^{-1}\times120\ \text{L h}^{-1} = 670\) mg h⁻¹.
  2. (a) \(k_La(20) = 670/(44\times8.74) = 1.74\) h⁻¹; at 22 °C \(1.74\times1.024^{2} = 1.82\) h⁻¹.
  3. (b) \(\text{OUR} = 6\times8.2/44 = 1.12\) mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹; \(C_{ss} = 8.74 - 1.12/1.82 = 8.13\) mg L⁻¹ — the air pump has a large reserve.
  4. (c) With \(k_La\approx0\): \(\tau = (8.13-3)/1.12 = 4.6\) h.
  5. Interpretation. Capacity is not the problem, reliability is: a power cut in the evening is found in the morning with a hypoxic crop. Doubling the solution depth doubles \(\tau\); a battery-backed air pump removes the risk. Compare the DWC readouts of the gallery.
(a) kLa ≈ 1.8 h⁻¹; (b) Css ≈ 8.1 mg L⁻¹; (c) ≈ 4.6 h
Ebb-and-flow: filling, Torricelli drainage and water between floods(S4)
\[ \begin{gathered} t_{fill}=\frac{A\,d}{Q_p},\qquad t_{drain}=\frac{A}{C_d\,a}\sqrt{\frac{2d}{g}} \\[6pt] M=\frac{V_{EAW}}{E\,\Delta t_{flood}} \end{gathered} \]

the fill time is the flooded water volume divided by the pump flow; the drain time through an opening of area a follows from Torricelli's law; and the water margin is the easily available water stored in a pot divided by the water the plant uses between two floods.

Follow the derivation
  1. Filling: the pump delivers \(Q_p\) until the tray holds \(Ad\), so \(t_{fill}=Ad/Q_p\) (the drain-back path is closed by the running pump).
  2. Draining: with depth \(h\) the outflow is \(C_da\sqrt{2gh}\) (Bernoulli/Torricelli), so \(A\,\mathrm{d}h/\mathrm{d}t=-C_da\sqrt{2gh}\).
  3. Separate variables: \(\mathrm{d}h/\sqrt h=-(C_da\sqrt{2g}/A)\,\mathrm{d}t\); integrating from \(d\) to 0 gives \(2\sqrt d=(C_da\sqrt{2g}/A)\,t_{drain}\), i.e. the boxed result. The depth falls as \(h=d(1-t/t_{drain})^2\).
  4. Margin: between floods the plant draws on the easily available water of its pot; if \(M \lt 1\) the substrate dries before the next flood and growth slows in proportion.
Assumptions and validity

The drain-back path through the pump is represented by an equivalent sharp-edged orifice discharging freely; friction in the return pipe would lengthen the drain time. Water absorbed by the substrate during the flood and the water held in the saucers of the pots are ignored in \(t_{fill}\).

\(Ad/Q_p\): m³ ÷ m³ s⁻¹ = s; \((A/a)\sqrt{d/g}\): \(\sqrt{\text{m}/(\text{m s}^{-2})}=\text{s}\) ✓
Worked example 2Setting the ebb-and-flow timer

The flood tray has a free water area of 0.433 m² and an overflow standpipe 4 cm high. The pump delivers 8 L min⁻¹; when it stops, the tray drains back through the pump, which behaves like a 13 mm sharp-edged orifice (\(C_d\) = 0.61). The timer holds each flood for 5 minutes. Each 0.58 L pot stores 25 % of its volume as easily available water, and a 150 g lettuce transpires 0.45 L d⁻¹. (a) Calculate the fill and drain times. (b) Are four floods a day enough? (c) What is the minimum number of floods, and on a hot day (0.75 L d⁻¹)?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Flood volume and filling: \(V = 0.433\times0.04 = 0.0173\) m³ = 17.3 L; \(t_{fill} = 17.3/8 = 2.2\) min.
  2. Draining (Torricelli): \(a = \pi(0.0065)^2 = 1.33\times10^{-4}\) m²; \(A/(C_da) = 0.433/(0.61\times1.33\times10^{-4}) = 5348\); \(\sqrt{2\times0.04/9.81} = 0.0903\) s; \(t_{drain} = 483\) s = 8.0 min.
  3. Submergence: 2.2 + 5 + 8.0 = 15 min per flood, i.e. 1.0 h per day with four floods — short enough for the pores to refill with air.
  4. (b) Water margin: stored \(0.58\times0.25 = 0.145\) L; used between floods \(0.45/4 = 0.113\) L; \(M = 1.29\) ✓.
  5. (c) Minimum floods: \(0.45/0.145 = 3.1\) → at least 4 floods a day; on a hot day \(0.75/0.145 = 5.2\) → at least 6. Floods at night are unnecessary because transpiration is small without light.
tfill ≈ 2.2 min, tdrain ≈ 8.0 min; four floods give M ≈ 1.3; at least 4 floods a day (6 on hot days)
Drip: supply, drain fraction and emitter uniformity(S5)
\[ \begin{gathered} S=q_e\,n_{pulse}\,t_{pulse},\qquad \text{DF}=1-\frac{E}{S} \\[6pt] \frac{q_{last}}{q_{first}}=\left(1-\frac{\Delta H}{H}\right)^{x},\qquad E_N=(S-E)\,c_N \end{gathered} \]

Wick: steady capillary flux against gravity (Darcy with exponential conductivity)(S6)
\[ \begin{gathered} K(\psi)=K_s\,e^{\alpha\psi},\qquad \alpha=\frac{1}{h_c} \\[6pt] q_{\max}=K_s\,\frac{Z}{L}\;\frac{1-e^{-\alpha(h_{top}-Z)}}{e^{\alpha Z}-1},\qquad Q_{wick}=n_w\,\frac{\pi d_w^2}{4}\,q_{\max} \end{gathered} \]

the conductivity of the wick falls exponentially as its suction increases, with a capillary length h_c; solving Darcy's law along a wick that rises Z over a path length L, the largest flux it can deliver while the substrate end stays wetter than the suction h_top is the boxed expression, and the capacity of the wick set is that flux times the wick cross-section and the number of wicks.

\(K_s\)saturated hydraulic conductivity of the wick; measured 220–1380 cm h⁻¹ (6 × 10⁻⁴–4 × 10⁻³ m s⁻¹) for fibreglass wicks (Knutson & Selker, 1994); lower for cotton (assumed)m s⁻¹1 × 10⁻⁴

Follow the derivation
  1. Darcy's law for unsaturated flow along a wick inclined by \(\beta\) (\(\sin\beta=Z/L\)), with pressure head \(\psi\le0\) and upward flux \(q\): \(q=-K(\psi)\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}s}+\sin\beta\right)\).
  2. Rearrange: \(\mathrm{d}s=-\dfrac{K(\psi)\,\mathrm{d}\psi}{q+K(\psi)\sin\beta}\). With \(K=K_se^{\alpha\psi}\) the integral is elementary: \(L=\dfrac{1}{\alpha\sin\beta}\ln\dfrac{q+K_s\sin\beta}{q+K_s\sin\beta\,e^{\alpha\psi_{top}}}\), taking \(\psi=0\) at the water surface and \(\psi_{top}\) at the substrate end.
  3. Solve for \(q\) with \(\psi_{top}=-h_{top}\) and \(L\sin\beta=Z\): \(q=K_s\sin\beta\,\dfrac{1-e^{\alpha(Z-h_{top})}}{e^{\alpha Z}-1}\) — the boxed result. For a vertical column and \(h_{top}\to\infty\) it reduces to Gardner's (1958) classic limit \(q=K_s/(e^{\alpha Z}-1)\) for evaporation from a water table.
  4. For small lifts \(q\approx K_s(1-e^{-\alpha h_{top}})/(\alpha L)\): the wick behaves like a horizontal Darcy conductor. When \(Z\) approaches \(h_c\) the denominator grows exponentially and the capacity collapses.
Assumptions and validity

The exponential \(K(\psi)\) was observed for fibreglass wicks (Knutson & Selker, 1994); cotton ropes and felts swell, age and clog with salts, so their parameters are uncertain — measure your own wick's capillary rise (it gives \(h_c\)) and the time it takes to deliver a known volume. The contact between wick and substrate and the conductivity of the substrate itself add further resistance, so the model is an upper limit.

\(K_s\,(Z/L)\) is m s⁻¹; the fraction is dimensionless because \(\alpha Z\) and \(\alpha h_{top}\) are dimensionless; m s⁻¹ × m² = m³ s⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 3Will two wicks keep up?

The gallery wick system feeds two lettuces through two 8 mm cotton ropes with \(K_s\) = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ and a capillary length \(h_c\) = 15 cm. At harvest the reservoir level has fallen so far that the lift from the water surface to 3 cm inside the substrate is \(Z\) = 12.1 cm (wick path \(L = Z\) + 6 cm = 18.1 cm); the substrate still supplies easily available water up to a suction of 50 cm. Each 150 g head transpires 0.45 L d⁻¹. (a) What can the wicks deliver? (b) How many 10 mm wicks are needed for a margin of 1.2?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Dimensionless groups: \(\alpha = 1/0.15 = 6.67\) m⁻¹; \(\alpha Z = 0.807\); \(\alpha(h_{top}-Z) = 6.67\times0.379 = 2.53\).
  2. Flux (S6): \(q = 10^{-4}\times\dfrac{0.121}{0.181}\times\dfrac{1-e^{-2.53}}{e^{0.807}-1} = 10^{-4}\times0.669\times\dfrac{0.920}{1.241} = 4.96\times10^{-5}\) m s⁻¹.
  3. (a) Capacity: each wick has \(A = \pi(0.004)^2 = 5.03\times10^{-5}\) m²; two wicks carry \(2\times5.03\times10^{-5}\times4.96\times10^{-5} = 4.99\times10^{-9}\) m³ s⁻¹ = 0.43 L d⁻¹ — less than half of the 0.90 L d⁻¹ the two heads need. In the gallery the plants therefore slow down in the last days and reach 134 g instead of 150 g.
  4. (b) Thicker wicks: a 10 mm wick has 1.56 times the area and carries 0.34 L d⁻¹; \(1.2\times0.90/0.34 = 3.2\) → four 10 mm wicks (1.35 L d⁻¹, margin 1.5).

Model assumptions

  • One crop for all. Every system grows the same butterhead lettuce (logistic, 150 g at 24 days after transplanting; Brechner & Both, 2013), with transpiration proportional to fresh mass. Light, CO₂, air humidity and nutrient composition are assumed adequate everywhere; only the supply of water (and oxygen for NFT and DWC) differs between systems.
  • Supply limitation. When a system delivers less water than the plant demands, growth and transpiration are reduced in the same proportion; oxygen limitation uses the illustrative response of the NFT simulator (no effect above 4 mg L⁻¹). Recovery after a stress is immediate, and wilting, root death and disease are not modelled.
  • NFT. Nusselt film and a constant-coefficient oxygen balance for a 1.2 m gully (surface renewal length 1 m, inlet 8 mg L⁻¹). The pump runs continuously.
  • DWC. Well-mixed tank; aeration capacity from a fixed transfer efficiency of the air stones (Lesson 5.3); surface re-aeration and microbial oxygen demand neglected. The raft covers the surface.
  • Ebb-and-flow. Linear filling at the pump flow, overflow at the standpipe height, and drainage back through the pump as a free orifice (Torricelli, Cd = 0.61); each 0.58 L pot stores 25 % of its volume as easily available water. Water taken up by the substrate during the flood is ignored.
  • Drip. A fixed irrigation schedule; drain water carries nitrogen at the supply concentration (a lower bound, as drains are usually more concentrated); emitter uniformity from the emitter law only (no clogging).
  • Wick. Gardner's exponential conductivity with parameters that you set; the default \(K_s\) = 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ and \(h_c\) = 15 cm are order-of-magnitude assumptions for a cotton rope (measured fibreglass wicks conduct 6–40 times better; Knutson & Selker, 1994). Wick–substrate contact resistance and the substrate's own conductivity are ignored, so the capacity is an upper limit.
  • Kratky. A vertical-walled tote; only transpiration removes water; growth stops if the tote runs dry.
  • Energy. Only pumps are counted (no lighting or climate control); the electrical power ratings are inputs, with defaults taken from Lessons 5.3–5.4. Water discarded at harvest is the reservoir or the solution left over.
  • 3D scene. Plant sizes, root lengths, water levels, flow speeds, bubble rates, drip rates, the flood cycle and wick wetness follow the models; the hardware is built at realistic scale but is illustrative.
