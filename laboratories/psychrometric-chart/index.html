Interactive psychrometric chart

The psychrometric chart is the map on which every heating, cooling, ventilating and humidifying process of a greenhouse or plant factory is a line. This one is computed live from the equations of Lesson 8.2 at whatever altitude you choose. Drag air states across it and read all their properties; then send the air through a heater, a cooling coil, a wet pad, a mixing box or a sealed room full of transpiring plants, and see the energy and water that each process costs or recovers.

Process results

Process sweep

How the selected process responds to its main setting; the magenta dot marks your current setting. For the closed room: humidity against time without dehumidification.

VPD made visible

Saturation vapour pressure against temperature. Each point's vertical bar runs from its actual vapour pressure up to saturation: the length of the bar is its vapour pressure deficit. Dashed lines bound the plant comfort zone.

Energy of the air

Sensible and latent parts of the enthalpy change of the process (or of the enthalpy of the active point when no process is selected), per kg of dry air.

Reading the map

  1. Find your way around. Dry-bulb temperature runs along the bottom, the humidity ratio up the right-hand side. The thick blue curve is saturation (100 % RH); the grey curves are 10–90 % RH; the thin amber lines slope down to the right at constant enthalpy; the dashed blue lines are wet-bulb temperatures; the dotted magenta lines are specific volumes. Hover anywhere to read the state under the cursor.
  2. Drag point A (24 °C, 70 %). Its dew point (18.2 °C) is where a horizontal line from A meets saturation; its wet-bulb temperature (20.1 °C) is where the dashed line through A meets saturation. Drag A to the right: RH falls, the dew point stays — heating never changes the water content.
  3. Choose “Cooling coil”. The air runs from A towards the coil's apparatus dew point on the saturation curve. With the coil at 10 °C and 10 000 m³ h⁻¹, the coil condenses about 54 kg of water per hour: this is where a plant factory recovers its crop's transpiration. Lower the coil temperature and watch the condensate grow in the sweep chart.
  4. Try “Winter vent: fog?”. Humid greenhouse air mixes with frosty outside air along a straight line. Because the saturation curve bends upwards, the straight line crosses it: above about 27 % outside air the mixture is supersaturated and fog forms — the plume you see above a greenhouse vent on a cold morning.
  5. Seal a growth room. With the dehumidifier off, 100 m² of lettuce transpiring 0.15 L m⁻² h⁻¹ push a 3-m-high room from 60 % to 90 % RH in about 7 minutes. The latent load (≈ 10 kW) is as large as the lamps' sensible heat: the sensible heat ratio is about 0.5.
  6. Climb a mountain. Load “Highland greenhouse (2400 m)”. The same 25 °C and 60 % RH now correspond to 16.0 instead of 11.9 g of water per kg of dry air, because the air itself is thinner. Every curve moves; only the dew point and the VPD, which depend on vapour pressure alone, stay put.
Key idea

Two independent properties fix the state of moist air; everything else can be read off the chart. Processes are lines: sensible heating is horizontal, evaporative cooling follows a wet-bulb line, mixing is a straight chord, dehumidification heads for the coil's point on the saturation curve, and a transpiring crop in a sealed room climbs vertically.

Why a chart, and how this one is built

Before computers, engineers designed air-conditioning with a ruler on the psychrometric chart: the equations of moist air (Lesson 8.2) are all there, drawn once and for all at one pressure. The chart is still the best way to see a process — whether a coil will dehumidify, whether mixing makes fog, how close a pad can cool towards the wet bulb. This chart is not a picture but a live computation: every curve is recalculated from Eqs. P1–P7 when you change the altitude or switch to saturation over ice.

The families of lines

  • Relative humidity curves are the saturation curve scaled down: \(W = 0.622\,\phi e_s/(P-\phi e_s)\). They crowd together at low temperature and fan out at high temperature because \(e_s\) grows exponentially.
  • Enthalpy lines are nearly straight and slope down to the right: warming the air at constant enthalpy requires removing water, and vice versa.
  • Wet-bulb lines are almost parallel to the enthalpy lines. They are not identical: along a wet-bulb line the enthalpy rises slightly by the enthalpy of the evaporated liquid water (Eq. P5).
  • Specific volume lines are steep: warm, humid air takes more space per kilogram of dry air, which matters when fans are sized in cubic metres per hour but loads are computed per kilogram.

Why altitude changes the chart

Relative humidity, dew point and VPD depend only on the vapour pressure \(e\) and the temperature. The humidity ratio, however, compares the mass of vapour with the mass of dry air, and at 2400 m there is a quarter less dry air in each cubic metre: the same \(e\) means a larger \(W\) (Eq. P1). Highland greenhouses — in Kenya, Colombia or the Alps — therefore need charts drawn for their pressure, and ventilation removes more water per cubic metre there than at sea level.

Plant comfort and disease on the chart

The green band marks a leaf-to-air vapour pressure deficit of 0.8–1.2 kPa, a common grower target for fruiting crops such as tomato: Iraqi et al. (1995) found higher photosynthesis and yield of greenhouse tomato at 0.8 kPa than at 0.5 kPa, and the ranges compiled by Shamshiri et al. (2018) span roughly 0.4–1.3 kPa between sources. Treat the band as a rule of thumb that you can move with the sliders, not as a physiological constant (Lesson 8.2). The amber and red bands mark relative humidities above 85 % and 90 %, where grey mould (Botrytis cinerea) risk rises because surfaces stay wet or close to saturation; keeping wetting periods short by ventilation and heating reduces epidemics (Yunis et al., 1994; Elad et al., 2016).

Common misconception

“Evaporative cooling removes heat from the air.” A wet pad removes no energy at all: the enthalpy of the air leaving the pad is the same as that entering (plus the tiny enthalpy of the water). Sensible heat is converted into latent heat. That is why a pad cannot cool below the wet-bulb temperature, and why it works well in dry Almería (wet bulb 21 °C at 34 °C and 30 %) but hardly in humid air.

Which saturation formula?

The course uses the Tetens (FAO-56) equation throughout, and so does this chart by default. ASHRAE charts use the Hyland–Wexler equations and, below 0 °C, saturation over ice. The differences are below 0.3 % above freezing; below freezing, switch on “saturation over ice” to see the frost curve (Murray, 1967).

The equations behind the chart

Temperatures \(T\) in °C (absolute temperatures in K where marked), pressures in kPa, humidity ratio \(W\) in kg of water per kg of dry air. Each equation is implemented in psy.js; the lesson numbers refer to Lesson 8.2.

Humidity ratio from vapour pressure(P1)
\[ W = \frac{M_w}{M_a}\,\frac{e}{P-e} = 0.62198\,\frac{e}{P-e}, \qquad e = \frac{W\,P}{0.62198 + W} \]

the humidity ratio is the mass ratio of vapour to dry air: the molar-mass ratio times the partial pressure of vapour divided by the partial pressure of dry air (Dalton's law). Inverting gives the vapour pressure of air with a given humidity ratio.

Follow the derivation
  1. Ideal-gas law for each component in the same volume and temperature: \(m_v = eVM_w/(RT)\), \(m_a = (P-e)VM_a/(RT)\).
  2. Divide: \(W = (M_w/M_a)\,e/(P-e)\); \(V\), \(R\), \(T\) cancel (Lesson 8.2, Eq. 8.2.2).
  3. Solve for \(e\): \(W(P-e) = 0.622e\) → \(e = WP/(0.622+W)\).

Ideal gases; the enhancement factor (≈ 1.004) of real moist air is neglected, as in all agricultural calculations.

Saturation vapour pressure over water and over ice(P2)
\[ e_s(T) = 0.6108\,\exp\!\left(\frac{17.27\,T}{T+237.3}\right) \ \ \text{(water)}, \qquad e_{s,i}(T) = 0.6108\,\exp\!\left(\frac{21.875\,T}{T+265.5}\right) \ \ \text{(ice, } T<0) \]

the saturation vapour pressure rises roughly exponentially with temperature; over ice it is lower than over supercooled water because molecules escape a crystal less easily.

  3. Over ice the latent heat is that of sublimation (≈ 2.83 MJ kg⁻¹), so the curve is steeper and the constants larger (21.875, 265.5).

Thermodynamic wet-bulb temperature(P5)
\[ W = \frac{(2501 - 2.326\,T_{wb})\,W_s(T_{wb}) - 1.006\,(T - T_{wb})}{2501 + 1.86\,T - 4.186\,T_{wb}} \qquad (T_{wb} \ge 0) \]

air of dry-bulb temperature \(T\) and humidity ratio \(W\) has the wet-bulb temperature \(T_{wb}\) at which adiabatically evaporating water into it would saturate it: the enthalpy of the air plus the enthalpy of the added liquid water equals the enthalpy of saturated air at \(T_{wb}\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(T_{wb}\)thermodynamic wet-bulb temperature°C
\(W_s(T_{wb})\)saturation humidity ratio at \(T_{wb}\) and pressure \(P\)kg kg⁻¹
2.326, 4.186from the enthalpies of vapour and liquid waterkJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Adiabatic saturator: air enters at (\(T, W\)), leaves saturated at \(T_{wb}\); liquid water is supplied at \(T_{wb}\). Energy balance per kg dry air: \(h(T,W) + (W_s - W)\,4.186\,T_{wb} = h(T_{wb}, W_s)\).
  2. Insert \(h = 1.006T + W(2501 + 1.86T)\) on both sides and solve for \(W\): the boxed ASHRAE (2021) equation. Below 0 °C the liquid term is replaced by ice (2830 − 0.24 \(T_{wb}\), 2.1 \(T_{wb}\)).
  3. The simulator solves for \(T_{wb}\) by bisection, at any pressure. The empirical Stull (2011) formula in physics.js is shown for comparison in the readout note; it is valid at sea level only.
Assumptions and validity

The thermodynamic wet bulb equals the reading of a well-ventilated psychrometer to within a few tenths of a kelvin (Lewis relation ≈ 1).

Specific enthalpy of moist air(P6)
\[ h = 1.006\,T + W\,(2501 + 1.86\,T) \quad \text{kJ per kg dry air}, \qquad T = \frac{h - 2501\,W}{1.006 + 1.86\,W} \]

\[ v = \frac{R_a\,(T+273.15)\,(1 + 1.607858\,W)}{P}, \qquad \rho = \frac{1+W}{v}, \qquad \rho_v = \frac{W}{v} \]

Sensible heating and cooling(P8)
\[ \dot Q = \dot m_a\,(h_2 - h_1) + \dot m_w h_{w}, \qquad \dot m_a = \frac{\dot V}{3600\,v_1}, \qquad W_2 = W_1 \ \ \text{if } T_2 \ge T_{d,1} \]

the heat added to (or removed from) an air stream is its dry-air mass flow times the enthalpy change; above the dew point the humidity ratio does not change. If the air is cooled below its dew point it follows the saturation curve and the condensate \(\dot m_w\) leaves with enthalpy \(h_w = 4.186\,T_2\).

Cooling coil: apparatus dew point, bypass factor and condensate(P9)
\[ T_2 = T_{\text{adp}} + \text{BF}\,(T_1 - T_{\text{adp}}), \quad W_2 = W_s(T_{\text{adp}}) + \text{BF}\,\big(W_1 - W_s(T_{\text{adp}})\big), \quad \dot m_w = \dot m_a (W_1 - W_2), \quad \text{SHR} = \frac{\dot Q_s}{\dot Q} \]

Investigation tasks

  1. Heat to dry?

    Load “Greenhouse winter night”. By how much must you heat the 18 °C, 85 % air to bring it to 70 % RH? How much heat does that take for 20 000 m³ h⁻¹, and why is the dew point — the real condensation risk on cold leaves — unchanged? Compare with the minimum-pipe strategy in the greenhouse climate simulator.

  2. Design a plant-factory coil

    Load “Plant-factory cooling coil”. Find the coil temperature and bypass factor that return the air at 16–18 °C and about 90 % RH while removing at least 60 kg of water per hour. Record the load and the SHR. Then express the condensate as litres per m² of cultivation area per day if the coil serves 400 m² and runs 16 h a day.

  3. How far can a pad cool?

    With “Pad-and-fan in Almería”, move point A along constant temperature (34 °C) from 20 % to 70 % RH. Plot the leaving temperature and the water use against inlet RH. At what inlet humidity is a pad no longer worth running (say, less than 5 K of cooling)?

  4. When does the vent plume appear?

    With “Winter vent: fog?”, find the share of outside air at which fog first forms for inside air at 85 % RH, and repeat for 75 % and 90 %. Explain with Eq. P11 why drier inside air tolerates more outside air before fog forms.

  5. The latent load of a crop

    With “Sealed growth room”, vary the transpiration rate and the lamp heat. Plot the SHR against lamp heat for 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 L m⁻² h⁻¹. What does an SHR near 0.5 mean for the choice of air-conditioning equipment designed for offices (SHR ≈ 0.7–0.8)?

Assumptions

  • Moist air is a mixture of ideal gases; the enhancement factor of real moist air (≈ 0.4 %) is neglected.
  • Saturation vapour pressure: Tetens / FAO-56 over water (as everywhere in the course); optionally Tetens with Murray's constants over ice below 0 °C. ASHRAE's Hyland–Wexler formulation differs by less than 0.3 % above 0 °C.
  • Enthalpy, specific volume and the thermodynamic wet bulb follow ASHRAE (2021); the pressure at altitude follows the FAO-56 standard atmosphere.
  • Relative humidity below 0 °C is taken relative to the saturation curve in use (water or ice).
  • Processes are steady and adiabatic except for the stated heat flows; condensate leaves at the coil (or final) temperature; fans add no heat.
  • The coil is the ADP–bypass-factor model; pad cooling follows the wet-bulb line; the closed room is held at constant temperature with transpiration independent of humidity.
  • The VPD comfort band and the RH risk thresholds are adjustable rules of thumb from the literature, not physiological constants or a disease model.
