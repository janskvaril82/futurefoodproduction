Sun path and daily light integral

Put a Venlo glasshouse full of lettuce at any latitude — at one of seven real sites from the Equator to beyond the Arctic Circle, or at a custom site of your choice — and follow the sun across its sky. The sun in the 3D scene moves along its true path for the chosen latitude, date and time; the golden arc and hour markers show today's path, the orange and blue arcs the solstices. Behind the scene a radiation model — solar geometry, 20 years of NASA satellite climatology, glazing transmission — turns the sunlight into photons on the crop, day by day through the year, and tells you how many hours of lamp light, kilowatt-hours and euros it takes to keep a crop at its target daily light integral.

A guided tour through the seasons

  1. Västerås, midsummer. Press Play the day. The sun rises in the north-east long before 04:00 clock time, climbs to 54° above the southern horizon at solar noon and sets in the north-west: the golden arc spans more than 18 hours. The natural DLI inside the glasshouse is well above the 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ lettuce target — no lamps are needed.
  2. Västerås, midwinter. The arc shrinks to a low, short bow just above the southern horizon: at noon the sun is only 7° high and the day lasts 5.6 hours. Clouds make it worse — the clearness index in December is only 0.27. The inside DLI drops below 1 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, and the lamps must supply more than 90 % of the crop's photons.
  3. Kiruna. Above the Arctic Circle the sun never sets at midsummer (play the day at Kiruna midnight sun) and never rises in December. Note the flat-topped day-length curve.
  4. Almería and Singapore. In southern Spain the mid-January DLI inside a glasshouse still exceeds 12 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ — one reason why Europe's winter vegetables come from there. In Singapore, day length hardly changes over the year and the sun passes almost overhead, but frequent clouds keep the DLI moderate.
  5. Look at the diamonds in the year chart: they are NASA's own monthly photosynthetic radiation estimates, completely independent of our within-day model. The model's outside DLI should run through them.
  6. Change the lamps. Switch the photon efficacy from 2.8 (LED) to 1.7 µmol J⁻¹ (high-pressure sodium, the fixtures change to HPS luminaires) and watch the annual electricity rise by 65 %.
Key idea

The light a crop receives in a day is set by geometry (how high and how long the sun is up — latitude and date), atmosphere (clouds, captured by the clearness index) and the greenhouse (transmission). At high latitudes these combine into a hundred-fold range in DLI between summer and winter — which is why Nordic year-round production depends on lamps and on cheap, low-carbon electricity.

Where is the sun?

Because the Earth's axis is tilted by 23.44° to its orbit, the latitude at which the sun stands overhead at noon — the solar declination δ — swings between +23.44° (June solstice) and −23.44° (December solstice). At a place of latitude φ the sun at solar noon is therefore \(90^\circ-|\varphi-\delta|\) above the horizon: 54° in Västerås in June but only 7° in December. During the day the sun turns with the Earth at 15° per hour (the hour angle ω); spherical trigonometry turns δ, φ and ω into the sun's elevation and azimuth (Derive, D1–D4).

Solar time and clock time differ. Solar noon is when the sun crosses the meridian. Clock time follows a time zone (CET = UTC+1 is centred on 15° E), so in Västerås (16.55° E) the sun culminates about 6 minutes before 12:00 CET — or at about 12:54 CEST in summer time. The Earth's elliptical orbit and tilt add the equation of time, up to ±16 minutes. The laboratory runs on solar time and shows the clock time in the scene.

How much solar energy arrives?

Above the atmosphere, a horizontal square metre receives the extraterrestrial radiation \(H_0\): about 41 MJ m⁻² per day at Västerås in June and only 2.3 MJ in December — geometry alone makes an 18-fold difference. The atmosphere then removes part of it. The clearness index \(K_t = H/H_0\) summarises how much of the extraterrestrial radiation reaches the ground on average; it is about 0.7 on a perfectly clear day and 0.1–0.2 under thick overcast. We take monthly mean \(K_t\) values from NASA's POWER project, a satellite-derived climatology averaged over 2001–2020. Swedish winters are doubly dark: low \(H_0\) and low \(K_t\) (0.27 in December in Västerås, compared with 0.56 in Almería).

Within the day, the model spreads the daily total over the hours in proportion to a clear-sky irradiance curve (the Haurwitz clear-sky model), so that the day integral matches \(K_t H_0\) exactly. Real days are not averages: a sunny and an overcast day in the same month can differ by a factor of five. The DLI curve therefore shows the climatological mean, which is what a lighting engineer uses to size an installation and estimate annual energy.

From watts to photons, and through the glass

About 45 % of the energy of global solar radiation lies in the PAR band (400–700 nm), and each joule of that sunlight PAR carries about 4.57 µmol of photons (McCree, 1972). So 1 J of total solar radiation delivers roughly 2.0 µmol of PAR photons: the course-wide conversion \(\text{PPFD}\approx 2.02\,G\) (see Lesson 4.1). A greenhouse then transmits only part of the light: clean glass passes about 90 % at normal incidence, but the frame, gutters, screens, lamps and dirt shade more, and light arriving at low angles is partly reflected. The hemispherical PAR transmission τ of a whole glasshouse is best measured with two quantum sensors, one inside and one outside; the default here, 0.65, is a typical assumed value.

How much light does the crop need?

Crop growth is closely proportional to the daily light integral. Hydroponic lettuce reached a 150 g market weight in 24 days after transplanting at an average of 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (Both et al., 1997), a value widely used as the lettuce target; fruiting crops such as tomato need more, microgreens less. Albright, Both and Chiu (2000) showed that controlling supplementary lamps (and shade screens) so that the crop receives a constant DLI every day gives predictable, schedulable production. This laboratory uses the same idea: each day the lamps are switched on for exactly as many hours as needed to top the natural DLI up to the target, up to a maximum photoperiod — first early and late in the day, when the sun contributes least.

Swedish and EU context

Graamans et al. (2018) compared lettuce production in plant factories and greenhouses at 24–68° N. The Swedish greenhouse with artificial lighting was the most energy-efficient greenhouse they modelled (1699 MJ per kg dry weight), yet its purchased electricity was 182 kWhe per kg dry weight (211 without lamps, because of the low winter yield), against 70 kWhe in the Netherlands and 247 in a plant factory. Use the laboratory to see where this comes from: compare the annual lighting electricity for Västerås, Kiruna and Amsterdam at the same target.

Common misconception

“Long summer days at high latitude compensate for the dark winter.” They do in June — the natural DLI in Västerås in June is similar to Almería's — but the annual light sum is much lower, and the winter deficit cannot be stored. A year-round crop needs its target DLI every single day.

The equations behind the simulation

Solar geometry follows FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 (Allen et al., 1998) and Spencer (1971), as implemented in the course library physics.js, so this laboratory and the lessons use identical numbers.

Solar declination(D1)
\[ \delta = 0.409\,\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi J}{365}-1.39\right) \]

the declination of the sun, in radians, oscillates once a year with an amplitude of 0.409 rad (23.4°), reaching its maximum near day 172 (21 June).

Where it comes from
  1. The Earth's axis is tilted by ε = 23.44° to the normal of its orbit.
  2. Over a year the angle between the Sun–Earth line and the equatorial plane varies as \(\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon\,\sin\lambda_\odot\), where \(\lambda_\odot\) is the Sun's ecliptic longitude.
  3. For a nearly circular orbit \(\lambda_\odot\) grows almost uniformly with the day number; the phase −1.39 rad places the March equinox (δ = 0) near day 80.
Assumptions and validity

Accuracy ≈ 0.5° — ample for agronomy. Astronomical algorithms (e.g. NOAA's) reach 0.01°.

Solar elevation and azimuth(D3)
\[ \sin\alpha=\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos\omega, \qquad \cos A=\frac{\sin\delta-\sin\alpha\sin\varphi}{\cos\alpha\cos\varphi} \]

the sine of the sun's elevation combines latitude, declination and hour angle; the azimuth follows from the same spherical triangle and is taken as \(360^\circ-A\) in the afternoon.

Follow the derivation
  1. Draw the spherical triangle zenith – celestial pole – sun. Its sides are \(90^\circ-\varphi\), \(90^\circ-\delta\) and \(90^\circ-\alpha\); the angle at the pole is the hour angle ω.
  2. The spherical law of cosines for the side opposite ω gives \(\cos(90^\circ-\alpha)=\cos(90^\circ-\varphi)\cos(90^\circ-\delta)+\sin(90^\circ-\varphi)\sin(90^\circ-\delta)\cos\omega\), i.e. the first equation.
  3. At solar noon (ω = 0): \(\sin\alpha=\cos(\varphi-\delta)\), so \(\alpha_{\text{noon}}=90^\circ-|\varphi-\delta|\).
  4. Applying the law of cosines to the side opposite the azimuth angle gives the second equation.
Assumptions and validity

Atmospheric refraction (which lifts the apparent sun by ≈ 0.5° at the horizon) and the size of the solar disc are ignored, so sunrise and sunset are a few minutes earlier/later in reality.

all angles; sin and cos dimensionless ✓
Sunset hour angle and day length(D4)
\[ \omega_s=\arccos\!\left(-\tan\varphi\,\tan\delta\right), \qquad N=\frac{24}{\pi}\,\omega_s \]

the sun sets when its elevation is zero, at an hour angle whose cosine is minus the product of the tangents of latitude and declination; the day length is twice that angle converted to hours.

Follow the derivation

\[ H_0=\frac{24\cdot60}{\pi}\,G_{sc}\,d_r\left[\omega_s\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\sin\omega_s\right],\qquad d_r=1+0.033\cos\frac{2\pi J}{365} \]

Clear-sky irradiance within the day(D6)
\[ G_{cs}=1098\;\sin\alpha\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{0.059}{\sin\alpha}\right) \]

the clear-sky global irradiance on a horizontal surface is a maximum value times the projection factor sin α, reduced by an exponential attenuation whose exponent grows with the length of the sun's path through the atmosphere, which is roughly 1/sin α air masses (Haurwitz, 1945).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(G_{cs}\)clear-sky global (beam + diffuse) irradiance on a horizontal surfaceW m⁻²
\(\alpha\)solar elevationrad
1098, 0.059empirical constants fitted to clear-sky measurements (as implemented in the open-source pvlib library)W m⁻², –
Why this model?
  1. For a plane-parallel atmosphere the relative air mass is \(m\approx 1/\sin\alpha\): at 30° elevation sunlight crosses two atmospheres, at 7° about eight.
  2. The exponential term therefore behaves like a Beer–Lambert attenuation \(e^{-0.059\,m}\) of the whole global radiation, while \(\sin\alpha\) projects the beam on the horizontal plane.
  3. Reno, Hansen and Stein (2012) compared published clear-sky models against measured irradiance; among the models that need only the solar elevation, Haurwitz's had the smallest average monthly error.
  4. Its daily integral gives a clear-sky clearness index of 0.72–0.76 around midsummer at the laboratory's sites, and 0.4–0.7 at midwinter, when the long air path at low sun matters. It is therefore always brighter than the all-sky climatology — as a clear sky must be.
Assumptions and validity

An empirical model for a typical clean atmosphere at low altitude, with no inputs for water vapour, aerosols or altitude, so hazy or high-altitude sites deviate by 10–20 %. It is used for the shape of the day; the daily total comes from the clearness index (D7). Below about 2° elevation all simple models are unreliable.

\[ H = K_t\,H_0,\qquad G(t)=H\,\frac{G_{cs}(t)}{\int_{\text{day}}G_{cs}\,\mathrm{d}t} \]

the daily global radiation is the clearness index times the extraterrestrial radiation; within the day it is distributed with the same shape as a clear-sky day, scaled so that the integral is exactly \(H\).

Assumptions and validity

Clouds are treated as a uniform, time-independent dimmer. Real cloudy days are more diffuse and flatter; broken clouds cause minute-scale fluctuations. For daily and annual integrals — what DLI and energy calculations need — the approach is exact by construction; for instantaneous PPFD it is an average expectation. Classical alternatives are the hourly-to-daily ratios of Collares-Pereira and Rabl (1979).

J m⁻² d⁻¹ × (W m⁻²)/(J m⁻²) = W m⁻² (per day-shape) ✓
Photons and daily light integral inside the greenhouse(D8)
\[ \text{PPFD}_{\text{in}}(t)=\tau\cdot2.02\,G(t),\qquad \text{DLI}_{\text{in}}=\frac{1}{10^{6}}\int_{\text{day}}\text{PPFD}_{\text{in}}\,\mathrm{d}t=2.02\,\tau\,K_t\,H_0\big[\text{MJ}\big] \]

inside PPFD is the greenhouse transmission times about two micromoles of PAR photons per joule of global radiation; integrating over the day gives the inside DLI, which in convenient units is simply 2.02 × τ × Kt × H₀ in MJ.

Follow the derivation
  1. \(\int G\,\mathrm{d}t = H\) (J m⁻² d⁻¹) by D7.
  2. \(\int 2.02\,\tau\,G\,\mathrm{d}t = 2.02\,\tau H\) µmol m⁻² d⁻¹; dividing by 10⁶ gives mol.

\[ h=\min\!\left(h_{\max},\ \frac{\max(0,\ \text{DLI}_T-\text{DLI}_{\text{in}})\cdot10^{6}}{3600\ \text{PPFD}_L}\right),\qquad E_d=\frac{\text{PPFD}_L}{\eta_{\text{ph}}}\cdot\frac{h}{1000},\qquad C=p\sum_{J=1}^{365}E_{d,J} \]

All lamp photons are assumed to reach the canopy (so PPFDL/ηph is the electrical power density; use a slightly lower ηph to account for photons lost to walls and paths). Worked exampleLettuce in Västerås at midwinter

A glasshouse in Västerås (59.6° N, τ = 0.65) grows lettuce at a target of 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹. On 21 December the mean clearness index is 0.27 and H₀ = 2.3 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹. LED top-lighting gives 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 2.8 µmol J⁻¹. How many lamp hours are needed, and what does that day's light cost at 0.12 € kWh⁻¹ for 1000 m² of crop?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Natural DLI inside (D8): 2.02 × 0.65 × 0.27 × 2.3 = 0.82 mol m⁻² d⁻¹.
  2. Missing: 17 − 0.82 = 16.2 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ = 16.2 × 10⁶ µmol m⁻².
  3. Hours at 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹: 16.2 × 10⁶ / (250 × 3600) = 18.0 h — just within an 18 h maximum.
  4. Power density: 250 / 2.8 = 89 W m⁻²; energy: 89 × 18.0 / 1000 = 1.60 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹.
  5. For 1000 m²: 1600 kWh × 0.12 € = 192 € for that single day. Sanity check: 95 % of the crop's photons come from the lamps.
≈ 18 h of lamps, ≈ 1.6 kWh m⁻², ≈ 190 € per 1000 m² per day

Model assumptions

  • Solar geometry uses the FAO-56 declination and extraterrestrial-radiation formulas with the Spencer (1971) equation of time; refraction and the solar disc are ignored. Time is local solar time; clock time uses standard time zones and EU summer time of the 2026 calendar.
  • Cloudiness is the monthly mean clearness index Kt from NASA POWER (20-year climatology 2001–2020, satellite-derived, ≈ 1° grid cells), interpolated linearly between mid-month days. Day-to-day weather variability is not simulated: the DLI curve is a climatological mean.
  • The within-day distribution follows the Haurwitz clear-sky model scaled to the daily total. In the clear-sky mode the clear-sky model is used directly. (The course library's simpler beam + 10 % diffuse model is not used here because at low winter sun it gives less light than the all-sky climatology.)
  • The custom site lies on the 15° E meridian (clock time UTC+1, no summer time). No satellite climatology exists for it, so its cloudiness is one clearness index for the whole year (the Custom Kt slider) or the clear-sky model.
  • PAR photons per joule of global radiation are taken as 2.02 µmol J⁻¹ for all sky conditions (overcast light is somewhat richer in PAR, so winter DLIs may be slightly underestimated).
  • Greenhouse transmission τ is constant (daily hemispherical mean). In reality it falls for direct light at low sun elevations, so high-latitude winter DLIs inside are likely somewhat overestimated.
  • Lamps top up the DLI to the target each day, operating symmetrically at both ends of a maximum photoperiod centred on solar noon; all lamp photons reach the crop; heat from lamps is not credited.
  • The 3D scene is driven by the model (sun position, beam/diffuse light via Erbs et al. (1982), cloud cover from Kt, lamp status, roof vents opening with irradiance); plants, trees and houses are illustrative.
