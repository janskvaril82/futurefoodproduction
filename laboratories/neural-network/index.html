Neural network playground for yield

A greenhouse grower wants to predict how many grams of lettuce a cycle will deliver from its light, temperature, CO₂ and humidity. Here you train a real multilayer perceptron — written from scratch in JavaScript, running in your browser — on a synthetic but mechanistically generated greenhouse data set whose true response you know exactly. Watch every weight change as the network learns, see the training and validation losses separate when it starts to memorise noise, compare it with plain linear regression, open the black box with partial-dependence plots, and step through back-propagation one number at a time.

A guided tour in seven steps

  1. Watch it learn. The network starts with random weights: thin and thick, blue (positive) and orange (negative) connections. As the epochs run, the learning curves fall and the pattern of weights settles. The dots racing along the connections are the strongest signals for the probed greenhouse cycle.
  2. Compare with a straight line. The amber line in Learning curves is multiple linear regression on the same four inputs. Within a few hundred epochs the network's validation error drops well below it and approaches the grey noise floor — the error of the true, noise-free function, which no model can beat on average.
  3. Open the black box. In the partial-dependence plots the network (magenta) has discovered the temperature optimum near 22 °C, the saturating response to light and CO₂ and the humidity optimum — while linear regression can only draw straight lines. The dashed curves are the truth.
  4. Break it: few data, big network. Click the preset Few data, big network (40 cycles, 3 × 32 ReLU neurons, 2 000+ parameters). The training error plunges towards zero while the validation error stops falling and climbs: the network memorises noise. The readout Training samples per parameter turns red.
  5. Fix it. Press Early stopping to restore the weights of the best validation epoch, or click … now regularised (L2 penalty λ = 0.02) and compare the partial-dependence plots — smoother, and closer to the truth.
  6. Correlated inputs. Choose Correlated greenhouse data. In a real greenhouse, bright days are also warm, dry and ventilated (low CO₂). The network still predicts well within that pattern, but its partial dependence away from it — dark and hot, bright and cold — is guesswork, and the importance is shared between correlated inputs.
  7. Look inside one step. Switch the mode to Backprop step-through. A tiny 2–3–1 network processes one greenhouse cycle: forward pass, loss, the output error, the hidden errors and every gradient — with a numerical check — and finally the update that lowers the loss.
Key idea

A neural network is a flexible curve-fitter with thousands of adjustable weights. That flexibility lets it learn non-linear responses and interactions that a linear model misses — and, with too little data, to learn the noise as well. Judging it on data it has never seen is not optional; it is the only way to know whether it has learned anything.

Synthetic data

The data in this lab come from a documented crop-response function (Derive tab, Eqs. NN1–NN4) chosen to be plausible for lettuce. They are not measurements. Real greenhouse data are noisier, have missing values and drift, and contain effects (cultivar, pests, operator) that no input column records.

A greenhouse you can know completely

With real data you never know the true relationship between climate and yield, so you cannot tell whether a model has learned the truth or just fitted the noise. This lab therefore generates its own data from a crop-response function built from standard crop-physiology components (Figure 1). Light and CO₂ drive canopy photosynthesis through a rectangular hyperbola, the form used in Van Henten's (1994) lettuce model: at low light the canopy is light-limited, at high light it saturates at a rate that itself increases with CO₂. The CO₂ parameters are chosen so that doubling the concentration raises yield by a third — the average response of Kimball's (1983) review of 430 enrichment experiments. Temperature acts through a cardinal-temperature curve (Yan & Hunt, 1999) with an optimum at 22 °C. High vapour-pressure deficit partly closes the stomata (Leuning, 1995); low VPD lets fast-growing heads develop tipburn, which makes leaves unmarketable (Collier & Tibbitts, 1984; Saure, 1998). Log-normal noise represents everything else that differs between greenhouse cycles.

What the network is

A multilayer perceptron passes the four standardised inputs through one to three hidden layers. Every neuron computes a weighted sum of the previous layer plus a bias and applies a non-linear activation function — tanh, ReLU or sigmoid (Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.5). The output neuron is linear and predicts the standardised yield. A 4–12–1 network has 73 adjustable parameters; 4–32–32–32–1 has 2 305. With enough hidden neurons, such a network can approximate any smooth function of its inputs (Hornik et al., 1989) — which is both its power and its danger.

How it learns

Training minimises the mean squared error on the training samples plus an optional L2 penalty on the weights. The optimiser repeatedly takes a small mini-batch of samples, computes the gradient of the loss by back-propagation, and moves every weight a small step downhill. Plain stochastic gradient descent uses the same step size η for all weights; momentum accumulates past steps so that progress along long shallow valleys accelerates; Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) scales each weight's step by running estimates of the mean and variance of its gradient, which makes it forgiving of badly chosen learning rates. One pass through all training samples is an epoch. Weights start as small random numbers scaled to the number of connections (Glorot & Bengio, 2010; He et al., 2015): too large and tanh neurons saturate, too small and signals fade.

Over-fitting, validation and regularisation

The training error always keeps falling: with enough parameters the network can thread its prediction through every noisy sample. The validation error tells the real story. It falls while the network learns the true response, flattens near the noise floor and rises once the network starts to learn the noise of the training samples. Three remedies are in the lab: more data (the most effective), early stopping — keep the weights of the epoch with the lowest validation error (Prechelt, 1998) — and L2 regularisation, which penalises large weights and hence wiggly functions (Eq. NN8). A useful rule of thumb is the number of training samples per parameter: below one the network can fit almost anything.

Opening the black box — and its limits

A network's weights are not interpretable one by one. Two model-agnostic tools help. Partial dependence (Friedman, 2001) averages the prediction while one input is swept across its range, showing the learned response; permutation importance measures how much worse the predictions become when one input is scrambled (Breiman, 2001). Both assume that the inputs can be varied independently. When inputs are correlated, as in a real greenhouse where light, temperature, VPD and ventilation move together, partial dependence evaluates the network in combinations it has never seen (bright but cold, dark but hot) — extrapolation — and importance is shared unpredictably between the correlated inputs. The network may still predict well for the conditions that actually occur. It has learned a correlation, not a mechanism.

Common misconception

“The network predicts yield well, so it tells me how to change the climate.” A model trained on observational data describes associations inside the range of the data. To predict the effect of an intervention — raising CO₂ on a hot, sunny day, for example — you need either experiments that vary the inputs independently (the Designed experiment setting here, and the logic of experimental design) or a mechanistic model such as the Crop growth model.

Frontier

Hybrid models combine both worlds: a mechanistic crop model supplies the structure and a neural network learns the residual errors or the parameters from sensor data — the building blocks of the digital twins in Lesson 10.5 and of the Lettuce digital twin lab.

The data generator (NN1–NN4)

Canopy production: light, CO₂ and stomata(NN1)
\[ P = \frac{A\,B}{A+B},\qquad A = \frac{\text{DLI}}{17}\,\frac{g_a(C)}{g_a(800)},\qquad B = \frac{g_m(C)}{g_m(800)}\,f_s(D),\qquad g_a = \frac{C-\Gamma}{C+2\Gamma},\quad g_m = \frac{C-\Gamma}{C-\Gamma+K_C} \]

relative canopy production is a rectangular hyperbola combining a light-limited rate \(A\), proportional to the daily light integral and slightly increased by CO₂ (less photorespiration), and a light-saturated rate \(B\) that increases with CO₂ with Michaelis–Menten-like kinetics and decreases with VPD through stomatal closure; both are scaled to 1 at DLI 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, 800 µmol mol⁻¹ and 0.8 kPa.

Where the form and the numbers come from
  1. The rectangular hyperbola \(AB/(A+B)\) is the canopy form of Acock-type models used in Van Henten's (1994) lettuce model: it approaches \(A\) when light limits and \(B\) when it saturates.
  2. The factor \((C-\Gamma)/(C+2\Gamma)\) is the CO₂ dependence of the light-use efficiency in the same model family (photorespiration lowers the quantum yield at low CO₂).
  3. \(K_C = 350\) µmol mol⁻¹ was chosen so that \(P(17, 660)/P(17, 330) = 1.33\): doubling CO₂ raises production by 33 %, the mean yield response of Kimball's (1983) review. Because \(B\) matters most at high light, the relative CO₂ response rises from +30 % at DLI 8 to +36 % at DLI 28 — a realistic light × CO₂ interaction.
Assumptions and validity

A static response: the whole cycle is summarised by constant inputs. Acclimation to high CO₂, nutrient limitation and night conditions are ignored. The values are illustrative for a lettuce crop, not calibrated to a cultivar.

Cardinal-temperature response(NN2)
\[ f_T(T) = \frac{T_{\max}-T}{T_{\max}-T_{\text{opt}}}\left(\frac{T-T_{\min}}{T_{\text{opt}}-T_{\min}}\right)^{\frac{T_{\text{opt}}-T_{\min}}{T_{\max}-T_{\text{opt}}}}, \qquad T_{\min}<T<T_{\max} \]

the growth factor is zero at the minimum and maximum cardinal temperatures, exactly one at the optimum, and rises more slowly than it falls — the asymmetric shape of most temperature responses.

  1. At \(T = T_{\text{opt}}\) both brackets equal 1, so \(f_T = 1\).
  2. With \(T_{\min} = 4\), \(T_{\text{opt}} = 22\), \(T_{\max} = 34\) the exponent is 18/12 = 1.5; at 14 °C: \((20/12)(10/18)^{1.5} = 0.69\); at 30 °C: \((4/12)(26/18)^{1.5} = 0.58\).

VPD: stomatal closure and tipburn(NN3)
\[ f_s(D) = \frac{1+D_{\text{ref}}/D_0}{1+D/D_0}, \qquad L_{\text{tb}} = \min\!\left[0.6,\; 0.25\,\frac{Y_p}{250\ \text{g}}\;e^{-(D-0.2)/0.25}\right] \]

stomatal conductance, and with it the light-saturated rate, falls hyperbolically with VPD (a Lohammar/Leuning-type response, equal to 1 at 0.8 kPa); the fraction of the potential yield lost to tipburn is largest for large, fast-growing heads at very low day-time VPD and fades above about 1 kPa.

Biological background

Tipburn is a calcium-deficiency disorder of young, enclosed leaves: calcium moves with the transpiration stream, and rapidly expanding inner leaves that transpire little receive too little (Saure, 1998). In growth-chamber experiments with lettuce, lowering the day-time relative humidity (raising VPD) delayed tipburn and increased leaf calcium, but also slowed growth (Collier & Tibbitts, 1984); very fast growth makes tipburn more likely (Frantz et al., 2004). The generator encodes these directions; the magnitudes are illustrative.

\(D/D_0\) and \((D-0.2)/0.25\) are kPa/kPa → dimensionless ✓
Observed yield: the complete generator(NN4)
\[ Y_p = 250\ \text{g}\;\frac{P}{P_{\text{ref}}}\;f_T(T), \qquad Y = Y_p\,(1-L_{\text{tb}})\;e^{\sigma\varepsilon-\sigma^2/2}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \ln(1+\text{CV}^2),\ \ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1) \]

the potential yield is 250 g per head at the reference conditions, scaled by canopy production and the temperature factor; tipburn removes part of it; multiplicative log-normal noise with coefficient of variation CV (the Noise slider) represents everything the inputs do not capture, and its mean is exactly 1.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(Y\)marketable head fresh mass (the target)g head⁻¹
CVcoefficient of variation of the noise–
Why subtract \(\sigma^2/2\)?

For \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(E[e^{\sigma\varepsilon}] = e^{\sigma^2/2}\). Multiplying by \(e^{-\sigma^2/2}\) makes the noise factor average 1, so the expected observed yield equals the noise-free yield, and its standard deviation is \(\text{CV}\cdot Y\). The noise floor readout is the RMSE of the noise-free function on the validation samples, ≈ CV × typical yield.

\(P/P_{\text{ref}}\), \(f_T\), \(L_{\text{tb}}\) and the noise factor are dimensionless, so \(Y\) is in g head⁻¹ ✓

The network (NN5–NN11)

Standardisation (fitted on the training set only)(NN5)
\[ \tilde x_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}-\bar x_j^{\,\text{train}}}{s_j^{\,\text{train}}}, \qquad \tilde y_i = \frac{y_i-\bar y^{\,\text{train}}}{s_y^{\,\text{train}}} \]

every input and the target are shifted and scaled to mean zero and standard deviation one, using the means and SDs of the training samples only.

Why it matters

CO₂ varies by hundreds of µmol mol⁻¹ and VPD by tenths of a kPa. Without standardisation the loss surface is a long narrow valley and gradient descent crawls (Lesson 10.2, Figure 7); tanh neurons fed with large raw numbers saturate. Computing \(\bar x\) and \(s\) from the validation data as well would leak information about them into training — a small but real form of data leakage.

Training objective with L2 regularisation(NN7)
\[ J(\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac1m\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}(\hat y_i-\tilde y_i)^2 + \lambda\sum_{l}\lVert\mathbf W^{(l)}\rVert_F^2, \qquad \text{RMSE}_{\text{g}} = s_y\sqrt{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i(\hat y_i-\tilde y_i)^2} \]

the objective is the mean squared error over a mini-batch of \(m\) samples plus λ times the sum of all squared weights (biases are not penalised); errors are reported back in grams by multiplying the standardised RMSE by the SD of the training yields.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathcal B, m\)mini-batch and its size (1, 8, 32 or all)–
\(\lambda\)L2 penalty–
\(s_y\)SD of the training yieldsg head⁻¹
Why L2 smooths the fit

The gradient of \(\lambda w^2\) is \(2\lambda w\), so every step multiplies each weight by \((1-2\eta\lambda)\) before the data gradient acts — “weight decay” (Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.9). Weights that the data do not need decay towards zero, and a network with small weights computes a smoother function.

Standardised squared errors are dimensionless, as is λΣw²; multiplying the standardised RMSE by \(s_y\) restores grams ✓
Back-propagation(NN8)
\[ \delta^{(L)}_i = \frac{2(\hat y_i-\tilde y_i)}{m},\qquad \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} = \bigl(\mathbf W^{(l+1)\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\delta^{(l+1)}\bigr)\odot\varphi'\bigl(\mathbf z^{(l)}\bigr),\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial\mathbf W^{(l)}} = \sum_{i}\boldsymbol\delta^{(l)}\mathbf a^{(l-1)\mathsf T} + 2\lambda\mathbf W^{(l)},\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial\mathbf b^{(l)}} = \sum_i\boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} \]

the error signal of the output is the derivative of the loss with respect to the prediction; each earlier layer's error is the next layer's error sent back through the transposed weights and multiplied by the local slope of the activation; a weight's gradient is the error at its destination times the activation at its source, summed over the batch, plus the penalty term.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} = \partial J/\partial\mathbf z^{(l)}\)error signal of layer \(l\)–
\(\odot\)element-wise product–
Follow the chain rule
  1. \(J\) depends on \(\mathbf z^{(l)}\) only through \(\mathbf a^{(l)} = \varphi(\mathbf z^{(l)})\), which feeds \(\mathbf z^{(l+1)} = \mathbf W^{(l+1)}\mathbf a^{(l)} + \mathbf b^{(l+1)}\).
  2. Chain rule: \(\partial J/\partial z^{(l)}_k = \sum_j (\partial J/\partial z^{(l+1)}_j)\,W^{(l+1)}_{jk}\,\varphi'(z^{(l)}_k)\) — the recursion above.
  3. Since \(\partial z^{(l)}_j/\partial W^{(l)}_{jk} = a^{(l-1)}_k\), the weight gradient is \(\delta^{(l)}_j a^{(l-1)}_k\).
  4. In the step-through mode the loss of a single sample is \(L = \tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\), so \(\delta_{\text{out}} = \hat y - y\) exactly as in Lesson 10.2 (Eq. 10.2.8). The lab's code was verified against central finite differences (relative error below 10⁻⁵), and the step-through shows such a check live.
With standardised variables all quantities are dimensionless; in general \([\partial J/\partial W] = [J]/[W]\) at every step ✓
Optimisers: SGD, momentum and Adam(NN9)
\[ \text{SGD: } \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta g;\qquad \text{momentum: } v \leftarrow \mu v - \eta g,\ \theta \leftarrow \theta + v;\qquad \text{Adam: } \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon},\ \ \hat m = \frac{m}{1-\beta_1^t},\ \hat v = \frac{v}{1-\beta_2^t} \]

stochastic gradient descent steps against the mini-batch gradient \(g\); momentum keeps a running velocity; Adam keeps running averages of the gradient (\(m\)) and of its square (\(v\)), corrects them for their start at zero, and divides — so each parameter's step has roughly the size η whatever the scale of its gradient.

Why Adam forgives a poor learning rate

For plain SGD the step is proportional to the gradient: tiny in flat directions, explosive in steep ones, and the stable η is set by the steepest direction (Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.7). Adam's ratio \(\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}\) is of order one wherever the gradient is consistent, so all parameters move at a similar pace (Kingma & Ba, 2015). It is not magic: a very large η still makes the loss oscillate — try η = 0.5.

For standardised parameters η carries the units that make \(\eta g\) a parameter change; in Adam \(\hat m/\sqrt{\hat v}\) is dimensionless, so η itself has the units of θ ✓
Weight initialisation(NN10)
\[ \text{Glorot (tanh, sigmoid, output): } W\sim\mathcal U\!\left(-\sqrt{\tfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}},\ \sqrt{\tfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}}\right);\qquad \text{He (ReLU): } W\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\ \tfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}\right) \]

initial weights are random, with a spread that shrinks as the number of connections grows, so that the variance of the signals stays roughly constant from layer to layer; biases start at zero.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(n_{\text{in}}, n_{\text{out}}\)fan-in and fan-out of the layer–
Why random, and why this scale?

If all weights started equal, all hidden neurons would compute the same thing and receive the same gradient forever (symmetry). A sum of \(n_{\text{in}}\) inputs times weights of variance \(\sigma^2\) has variance \(n_{\text{in}}\sigma^2\); keeping it near 1 requires \(\sigma^2 \approx 1/n_{\text{in}}\). Glorot and Bengio (2010) balanced the forward and backward passes; ReLU zeroes half its inputs, hence the factor 2 of He et al. (2015).

Weights are dimensionless for standardised inputs ✓

Evaluation and interpretation (NN12–NN14)

Validation RMSE, R² and the noise floor(NN12)
\[ \text{RMSE}_{\text{val}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in\mathcal V}(\hat y_i-y_i)^2}, \qquad R^2_{\text{val}} = 1-\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal V}(\hat y_i-y_i)^2}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal V}(y_i-\bar y_{\mathcal V})^2}, \qquad \text{floor} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_v}\sum_{i\in\mathcal V}\bigl(f(\mathbf x_i)-y_i\bigr)^2} \]

prediction error and explained variance are computed on the validation samples \(\mathcal V\); the noise floor is the error that the true, noise-free function \(f\) itself makes on those samples.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathcal V, n_v\)validation set and its size–
\(f(\mathbf x)\)noise-free generator (Eq. NN4 without noise)g head⁻¹
Reading the three numbers together

Validation RMSE far above the floor: the model is too simple or under-trained (bias). Training RMSE far below the validation RMSE: it is over-fitted (variance). Validation RMSE close to the floor: nothing more can be learned from these inputs — further gains need better inputs or less noise, not a bigger network. A validation RMSE below the floor can happen by chance with small validation sets.

All three are in g head⁻¹; \(R^2\) is dimensionless ✓

\[ \text{PD}_j(v) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat f\bigl(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{i,j-1},\,v,\,x_{i,j+1},\ldots\bigr) \]

Assumptions and validity

Friedman (2001) introduced partial dependence for boosted trees; it works for any model. It averages over interactions and, when inputs are correlated, evaluates the model at combinations that never occur in the data. Compare the designed-experiment and real-greenhouse settings.

\[ I_j = \text{RMSE}_{\text{val}}\bigl(\text{input } j \text{ randomly permuted}\bigr) - \text{RMSE}_{\text{val}}(\text{original}) \]

shuffle the values of one input among the validation samples — destroying its relation with the yield while keeping its distribution — and record how much the prediction error grows.

Its structure follows standard crop physiology; the CO₂ response is calibrated to the mean of Kimball (1983); the cardinal temperatures, the VPD parameters and the tipburn magnitudes are illustrative. The generator is not a validated lettuce model — for that, see the Crop growth model and the Lettuce digital twin.
  • Each sample is one cycle with constant inputs; day–night differences, seasonal trends, cultivars and management are absent. In the “real greenhouse” design the inputs are linked through a latent sunniness variable with illustrative coefficients.
  • Noise is multiplicative and log-normal with a constant coefficient of variation, independent between samples. Real residuals are often correlated in time and space, which makes random train/validation splits optimistic (Roberts et al., 2017).
  • The network is a plain fully connected perceptron trained in double precision with the equations shown; the train/validation split is random with a fixed seed. There is no separate test set: when you tune hyperparameters by looking at the validation curve, the validation error itself becomes slightly optimistic.
  • Partial dependence averages over at most 160 samples; permutation importance uses a single random permutation. Both are therefore noisy estimates.
