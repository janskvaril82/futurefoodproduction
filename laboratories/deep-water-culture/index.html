Deep-water culture and dissolved oxygen

In deep-water culture (DWC) the roots of every plant hang in the same tank of nutrient solution — so the whole crop breathes from one shared, invisible reservoir of dissolved oxygen. This laboratory puts a working DWC raft system on your bench, cut open so that you can watch the roots, the air stones and the bubble plumes. A physical model computes the oxygen balance minute by minute through day and night: how much oxygen the water can hold at its temperature, how fast the bubbles put it back, and how fast the roots take it out. Switch the air pump off, heat the room, add a chiller or simulate a power cut, and see the roots turn from white to brown when the oxygen runs out.

  1. Run a normal day. Press Run (or the space bar with the stage selected). The clock starts at 06:00, when the LED fixture switches on. Watch the solution temperature on the DO chart creep upwards during the 16-hour photoperiod and drift back at night: 80 litres of water respond over many hours, not minutes. Dissolved oxygen follows the saturation line a few tenths of a milligram below it — that small gap is the price of pushing oxygen into the water fast enough to feed the roots.
  2. Look into the cut-away. Orbit to the front of the tank (or press Zoom into the root zone). The front wall has been cut away so that you see the solution in section: white root masses hanging from black net pots, air stones on the bottom and bubble plumes rising to the underside of the polystyrene raft. The hand-held meter on the stand shows what an optical DO probe in the tank would read.

Key idea

Dissolved oxygen is a race between supply — the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient \(k_La\) times the distance from saturation — and demand — root respiration, which grows with root mass and temperature. Aeration sets the supply, the crop and the temperature set the demand, and the volume of solution per plant sets how long the crop survives when supply fails.

An oxygen budget with one reservoir

Every gram of root tissue in the tank burns sugar in its mitochondria and needs oxygen to do it; without oxygen a root cell falls back on fermentation, gains only a fifteenth of the energy per glucose and soon stops taking up water and nutrients (Drew, 1997). In soil, oxygen reaches roots through air-filled pores. In DWC it must first dissolve in the solution, then be carried to the root surface. The tank is therefore a single, well-mixed reservoir with one inflow and one outflow of oxygen (Figure 1).

How much oxygen can the water hold?

Water in equilibrium with air holds only about 9.1 mg L⁻¹ of oxygen at 20 °C and 7.6 mg L⁻¹ at 30 °C — thirty times less per litre than the air above it (Benson & Krause, 1984; Lesson 5.3). This saturation concentration \(C^*\) is the ceiling that no air pump can exceed; it falls by roughly 2 % for every degree of warming and by about 1 % per 100 m of altitude.

Why an air stone works — and why doubling the pump does not double the oxygen

Oxygen crosses a thin, stagnant liquid film around every bubble. Many small bubbles make a large interfacial area per litre of solution (\(a\)), and their rise stirs the tank so that the films stay thin (large film coefficient \(K_L\)). The product \(k_La\) (h⁻¹) is what engineers measure. In bubble-aerated vessels \(k_La\) grows with the superficial gas velocity \(u_G\) — the air flow divided by the tank footprint — roughly as \(u_G^{0.7}\) in coalescing liquids such as tap water (Heijnen & Van 't Riet, 1984). Because \(u_G = (Q_{\text{air}}/V)\cdot H\), the same specific aeration rate (litres of air per litre of solution per hour) works better in a deeper tank, and doubling the air flow raises \(k_La\) only by \(2^{0.7} = 1.62\). Aeration also has diminishing returns in a second way: the transfer rate is proportional to the distance from saturation, so once DO is close to \(C^*\), extra air buys almost nothing.

The temperature squeeze

Warming the solution from 20 to 28 °C lowers \(C^*\) from 9.1 to 7.8 mg L⁻¹ while root respiration rises by \(2^{0.8} = 1.74\) (Q₁₀ = 2; Atkin & Tjoelker, 2003). Supply shrinks and demand grows at the same time, which is why summer is the dangerous season for DWC and why Cornell researchers found that a 24 °C pond kept lettuce marketable even in 31 °C air (Thompson et al., 1998). The third chart shows the squeeze directly: when root demand exceeds what the aeration can deliver at 4 mg L⁻¹, DO cannot recover above the threshold however long you wait.

The model uses 4 mg L⁻¹ as its practical threshold. It is a design choice with a safety margin, not a physiological constant: the well-mixed bulk solution the model computes is always better oxygenated than the water trapped inside a thick root mat.

Pythium: why warm, oxygen-poor tanks get sick

Pythium root rot is the most common yield-limiting disease of hydroponic leafy greens (Amrhein et al., 2025). Its swimming zoospores are carried by the solution to every plant, and they are attracted to stressed and injured roots (Stanghellini & Rasmussen, 1994; Sutton et al., 2006). Extension guidance recommends a root-zone temperature of 20–24 °C and more than 6 mg L⁻¹ of DO, because higher temperatures favour P. aphanidermatum (Mattson, 2018). The Pythium risk index in this laboratory combines exactly these three factors — warmth above about 24 °C, low DO and hypoxic root injury — into a number from 0 to 100. It is qualitative: it ranks conditions, it does not predict infection, which also needs a source of inoculum.

What does the oxygen cost?

An air pump is a small compressor: it must push air against the water column above the stones and through the pores of the stones. The ideal work is tiny — about a third of a watt for 4 L min⁻¹ against 22 cm of water plus the resistance of a fine stone — but small diaphragm pumps convert only a small fraction of their electricity into pneumatic power, so a few watts are typical. That is 20–40 kWh per year, a few euros. A chiller is a heat pump: it has to remove all the heat that flows into a cold tank from a warm room, plus the heat from the lamps, and its electricity use in a hot room is typically ten times that of the air pump. Insulating the tank, shading it and moving heat-producing equipment out of the solution are therefore the first steps; a chiller is the last.

Frontier · aeration, tipburn and what DO meters really see (2026)

Ries, Park and Meng (2026) grew lettuce in 89-litre DWC trays at 22–23 °C with no aeration or with two air stones per tray. The measured mean DO was only 2.9 mg L⁻¹ without aeration and 3.5–4.0 mg L⁻¹ with it — yet 21 days after transplanting, the aerated plants had 98–139 % more shoot fresh mass and wetter tissue. Two lessons follow. Oxygen supply matters even in the "adequate" range. And real tanks can sit far below the well-mixed prediction of this model: microbial respiration in biofilms, root exudates that lower \(K_L\), incomplete mixing and the position of the probe all push measured DO down. Use the model to understand mechanisms and sizes, and your own DO meter to learn the truth about your tank.

The equations behind the simulation

The model has three state variables — dissolved oxygen \(C\), solution temperature \(T\) and a root-injury index \(D\) — integrated with a one-minute time step. The symbols follow Lesson 5.3.

Oxygen saturation of fresh water (Benson & Krause)(D1)
\[ \begin{aligned} \ln C^*_0 &= -139.344 + \frac{1.5757\times10^{5}}{T_K} - \frac{6.6423\times10^{7}}{T_K^{2}} \\ &\quad + \frac{1.2438\times10^{10}}{T_K^{3}} - \frac{8.6219\times10^{11}}{T_K^{4}} \\[6pt] C^* &= C^*_0\,P\,\frac{(1-p_w/P)(1-\vartheta P)}{(1-p_w)(1-\vartheta)} \end{aligned} \]

the natural logarithm of the saturation concentration at one atmosphere is a fourth-order polynomial in the reciprocal absolute temperature; the concentration at barometric pressure \(P\) (in atmospheres) is corrected for the water-vapour pressure \(p_w\) and a small non-ideality term \(\vartheta\).

Where the equation comes from
  1. Henry's law (Lesson 5.3, Eq. 5.3.3): \(C^* = k_H\,p_{\mathrm{O_2}}\), with \(p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 0.2095\,(P - p_w)\) for moist air.
  2. \(k_H\) falls as temperature rises because dissolving oxygen releases heat (van 't Hoff). Instead of a single enthalpy, Benson and Krause (1984) fitted a polynomial in \(1/T_K\) to precise gas-extraction measurements. The coefficients shown are the form adopted by Standard Methods (method 4500-O), which returns \(C^*_0\) directly in mg L⁻¹.
  3. The pressure factor rescales from 1 atm to the local pressure, removing the water vapour that dilutes the air at the interface; \(\vartheta\) accounts for the small non-ideality of oxygen gas.
  4. The course library implements the same equation as doSaturation(); the model calls it at every time step.

Volumetric mass-transfer coefficient of bubble aeration(D2)
\[ k_La_{20} = 0.32\,u_G^{\,0.7}, \qquad u_G = \frac{Q_{\text{air}}}{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{air}}}{V}\,H, \qquad k_La(T) = k_La_{20}\,\theta^{\,T-20} + k_La_{\text{s}} \]

the transfer coefficient at 20 °C, in reciprocal seconds, is 0.32 times the superficial gas velocity in metres per second raised to the power 0.7. The superficial velocity is the air flow divided by the tank footprint, which equals the specific aeration rate times the solution depth. Warmer water transfers faster by a factor θ per degree, and a small surface term remains when the pump is off.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Structure. \(k_La = K_L\cdot a\). The interfacial area per volume is \(a = 6\varepsilon_G/d_b\) for bubbles of diameter \(d_b\) occupying a gas hold-up fraction \(\varepsilon_G\) of the liquid.
  2. Hold-up grows with air flow. In bubble columns the hold-up rises less than proportionally with \(u_G\) — Heijnen and Van 't Riet (1984) give \(\varepsilon_G \approx 0.6\,u_G^{0.7}\) — because faster bubble swarms rise faster and coalesce more.
  3. \(K_L\) is roughly constant for millimetre-sized bubbles in water (about \(4\times10^{-4}\) m s⁻¹; penetration theory, Higbie 1935), so \(k_La\) inherits the 0.7 exponent: \(k_La = 0.32\,u_G^{0.7}\) (s⁻¹) for coalescing, low-viscosity liquids.
  4. Depth. Writing \(u_G = (Q_{\text{air}}/V)H\) shows that the same pump in the same volume works better in a deeper tank: the bubbles spend longer in the water.
  5. Diminishing returns. Doubling \(Q_{\text{air}}\) multiplies \(k_La\) by \(2^{0.7} = 1.62\), while pump power doubles.
  6. Temperature. Diffusivity rises and viscosity falls with temperature; the clean-water standard corrects \(k_La\) with \(\theta^{T-20}\), θ = 1.024 (ASCE, 2007).

\[ \text{OUR}(T, C) = \frac{n\,q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,m_{\text{root}}}{V}\;Q_{10}^{\,(T-20)/10}\;\frac{C}{K_O + C}, \qquad m_{\text{root}} = \frac{m_{\max}}{1+e^{-r\,(t_d - t_{50})}} \]

Follow the derivation
  1. Aerobic respiration consumes six O₂ per glucose; per plant the demand is \(q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,m_{\text{root}}\) (mg h⁻¹), and all \(n\) plants draw on the same \(V\) litres (Lesson 5.3, Eq. 5.3.1).
  2. A rise of 10 °C multiplies the enzyme-driven rate by \(Q_{10}\); for any \(\Delta T\) the factor is \(Q_{10}^{\Delta T/10}\) (Eq. 5.3.2) — about +7 % per °C for Q₁₀ = 2.
  3. When the bulk DO approaches zero, diffusion into the root can no longer sustain full respiration. A Michaelis–Menten term \(C/(K_O+C)\) captures this: it is 0.93 at 4 mg L⁻¹ and 0.5 at 0.3 mg L⁻¹. It also prevents the model from producing negative concentrations.
  4. Example: 8 plants × 5 mg g⁻¹ h⁻¹ × 1.01 g ÷ 80 L = 0.51 mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 20 °C; at 26 °C × 20.6 = 0.77 mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

Respiration acclimates to temperature within days, so a constant Q₁₀ overestimates the long-term effect of a warm week (Atkin & Tjoelker, 2003). Microbial oxygen demand of biofilms, algae and root debris is not included — in a dirty tank it can equal the root demand. The root-mass curve is a smooth assumption through the order-of-magnitude values of Lesson 5.3, not a measured growth curve.

Dissolved-oxygen balance of the tank(D4)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_La(T)\,\big(C^*(T) - C\big) - \text{OUR}(T, C), \qquad \tau_{\text{crit}} \approx \frac{C_0 - C_{\text{crit}}}{\text{OUR}} \ \ (\text{pump off}) \]

the dissolved oxygen changes at the rate of transfer into the solution minus the rate of uptake by the roots. At steady state the two are equal. If the pump fails, transfer almost stops and the concentration falls at roughly the uptake rate, so the time to reach the critical concentration is the oxygen buffer divided by the uptake rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. Control volume: the solution, volume \(V\), well mixed. Accumulation of O₂ = transfer in − consumption: \(V\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = k_La\,V(C^*-C) - V\,\text{OUR}\). Divide by \(V\).
  2. Steady state (\(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t=0\)) with the Michaelis–Menten uptake \(R\,C/(K_O+C)\): \(k_La(C^*-C)(K_O+C) = R\,C\), a quadratic \(k_La C^2 + (R + k_La K_O - k_La C^*)C - k_La C^* K_O = 0\) whose positive root is \(C_{ss}\).   3. Pump failure: with only the small surface term, \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t \approx -\text{OUR}\) while \(C \gg K_O\), a straight-line decline.   4. Numerically: explicit Euler steps of 60 s. The fastest time scale, \(1/k_La\) ≈ 20–60 min, is far longer than the step, so the integration is stable and accurate.

Why these are "indices", not models

No validated equation predicts how fast lettuce roots brown at a given DO, or the probability of Pythium infection from temperature and DO. The indices encode the qualitative knowledge summarised in Explain — injury within hours below about 2–4 mg L⁻¹ (Morard & Silvestre, 1996), recommended root-zone temperature 20–24 °C and DO above 6 mg L⁻¹, higher temperatures favouring P. aphanidermatum (Mattson, 2018), stressed roots being more susceptible (Sutton et al., 2006) — so that the 3D scene and the readouts react in the right direction and with plausible timing. Use them to compare scenarios, never as predictions of yield loss.

Model assumptions

  • Well-mixed tank. One DO and one temperature for the whole solution. Gradients inside root mats, near the bottom and away from the stones are ignored; the model is optimistic compared with a probe inside the root mat.
  • Aeration. \(k_La\) from the bubble-column correlation of Heijnen and Van 't Riet (1984) for coalescing liquids, applied to the whole tank footprint; temperature correction θ = 1.024 (ASCE, 2007); 0.03 h⁻¹ of surface re-aeration through the gaps around the raft (assumed, as in Lesson 5.3). Stone type, fouling, surfactants and the partly non-coalescing character of saline nutrient solutions are not modelled; the number of stones changes only the 3D scene.
  • Oxygen demand. Only root respiration: specific rate \(q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\) (default 5 mg g⁻¹ h⁻¹, Lesson 5.3), root dry mass from an assumed logistic curve (≈ 0.03 g at transplanting to ≈ 1.2 g at 35 days), Q₁₀ = 2 without thermal acclimation, and a Michaelis–Menten factor with \(K_O\) = 0.3 mg L⁻¹ (assumed). Microbial and algal oxygen demand is omitted.
  • Saturation for fresh water at sea-level pressure (Benson & Krause, 1984); the ≈ 1 % salting-out by the nutrient salts is neglected.
  • Heat. Lumped heat balance with a room-air schedule (day value while the lights are on), lamp heat absorbed by the tank, a wall U-value (slider) plus 1.2 W m⁻² K⁻¹ through the EPS raft, no evaporation, and a proportional chiller with capacity limit and constant COP (assumed 2.5).
  • Air-pump power from flow × (hydrostatic head + 3 kPa stone resistance) ÷ efficiency (assumed 10 %). Check your pump's rating plate.
  • Root injury and Pythium risk are illustrative indices encoding qualitative knowledge (Derive, Eq. D7); they are not validated predictions of damage or infection.
  • The 3D scene is to scale (tank, raft spacing of 0.2 m, 5 cm net pots, 4.4 cm air stones), but plant and root shapes are procedural illustrations; root length follows crop age and root colour follows the injury index.
