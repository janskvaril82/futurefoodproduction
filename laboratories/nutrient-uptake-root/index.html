Root nutrient uptake

A lettuce plant takes up nitrate, potassium and phosphate through the membranes of its root cells — but only if the ions can reach the root. Fly from the whole plant into the root-hair zone and down to the plasma membrane, where proton pumps, symporters and channels move ions at rates computed by a mechanistic uptake model. Compare soil with a stirred nutrient solution, switch root hairs on and off, and watch depletion zones grow around the roots day by day.

Key idea

Nutrient uptake is a chain of two processes in series: transport to the root surface (mass flow and diffusion through the medium) and transport across the membrane (Michaelis–Menten kinetics of carriers and channels). Whichever is slower controls the rate. In a stirred nutrient solution the membrane step limits; in soil, for immobile ions such as phosphate, the supply step dominates — which is why root hairs, root length and mycorrhizas matter so much in soil and so little in hydroponics.

How ions reach a root

Roots take up nutrients from the solution that bathes them, never directly from soil particles. Barber (1995) distinguished three ways in which ions come into contact with the root surface (Figure 1). Root interception: the growing root displaces soil and meets ions on its way — a small contribution because roots occupy only about one per cent of the topsoil volume. Mass flow: transpiration pulls water towards the root and the water carries dissolved ions with it; the supply rate is the water influx times the concentration. Diffusion: when the root absorbs ions faster than mass flow delivers them, the concentration at the root surface falls, a concentration gradient forms and ions diffuse down it towards the root.

Mobile and immobile ions

How far an ion can travel in soil depends on its effective diffusion coefficient \(D_e = D_l\theta f/b\) (Eq. U3). Diffusion happens only in the soil water (volume fraction \(\theta\)), along tortuous paths (impedance factor \(f\)), and it is slowed by adsorption: the buffer power \(b\) is the amount of ion held on the solid phase plus in solution per unit change of solution concentration. Nitrate is hardly adsorbed (\(b\approx\theta\)), so it moves almost as fast as in pure water through the water-filled pores; phosphate is held so strongly (\(b\) of the order of hundreds) that its effective diffusion coefficient is thousands of times smaller. A useful rule of thumb is that in a time \(t\) an ion travels about \(2\sqrt{D_e t}\): centimetres for nitrate in a day, a fraction of a millimetre for phosphate in a week. Use the readouts to check this.

From the root surface into the cell

At the root surface ions cross the plasma membrane of epidermal and root-hair cells (Figure 3). The membrane is energised by the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase, a P-type pump that uses the energy of ATP to move protons out of the cell (Palmgren, 2001). This creates an acidic apoplast (pH about 5.5) and an inside-negative membrane potential. Anions must enter against this potential, so they are co-transported with protons: nitrate by NRT2 symporters with two protons per nitrate (Crawford & Glass, 1998) and phosphate by PHT1 symporters with several protons (Raghothama, 1999). Potassium, a cation, is pulled in by the membrane potential through channels such as AKT1 (Hirsch et al., 1998) and, at very low concentrations, by high-affinity H⁺-coupled transporters.

Because each carrier has a limited number of binding sites and a finite turnover rate, influx saturates at high concentration. Epstein, Rains and Elzam (1963) showed that K⁺ uptake by barley roots follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics, with a high-affinity mechanism that is half-saturated at about 0.02 mM. Claassen and Barber (1974) added the threshold Cmin, the concentration below which net influx stops because efflux balances influx (Figure 2).

Soil versus hydroponics

In a stirred or flowing nutrient solution the bulk concentration reaches right up to a thin unstirred layer around the root, typically tens of micrometres thick. Supply is therefore rarely limiting, and uptake is governed by the membrane kinetics — this is why a hydroponic lettuce thrives on a compact root system. In soil the same root faces a depletion zone: for phosphate the concentration at the root surface can fall to a small fraction of the bulk within hours, and the influx is then set by how fast diffusion can deliver ions, not by Imax. Two consequences follow: (i) increasing Imax (more transporters) helps little in soil, whereas (ii) anything that enlarges the volume of soil a root can exploit — root hairs, more root length, mycorrhizal hyphae — helps a lot.

Common misconception

“A high buffer power means the soil holds on to phosphate, so plants get less.” At a given soil-solution concentration, the opposite is true: a well-buffered soil replenishes the solution as the root depletes it, so the depletion zone expands more slowly and the gradient at the root stays steep. Try it: raise b for K⁺ while keeping Cb fixed and watch the cumulative uptake. What a high buffer power does do is make it harder to raise the solution concentration with fertiliser.

Root hairs

Root hairs are tubular outgrowths of epidermal cells, about 10 µm wide and a fraction of a millimetre to more than a millimetre long. For mobile nitrate they hardly matter, because the depletion zone extends far beyond them. For immobile phosphate they are decisive: the soil between the hairs is exhausted quickly, so the whole hair zone acts like a thicker root. Jungk (2001) concluded from the literature that root hairs contribute anything from almost nothing to about 80 % of the uptake of a nutrient, mainly for nutrients of low mobility and high demand; a root-hairless barley mutant did not survive beyond 30 days in a low-phosphorus soil, whereas the wild type did (Gahoonia & Nielsen, 2003).

Research frontier

Can we breed or engineer longer root hairs for low-input agriculture? Zhang et al. (2018) tripled root-hair length in Brachypodium by over-expressing RSL transcription factors. One line improved phosphorus nutrition, but plant biomass fell — longer hairs alone were not enough. Root-hair traits are now combined with root architecture and uptake kinetics in "root ideotype" breeding (Griffiths & York, 2020).

History

The mathematical description of nutrient transport to roots was developed in the 1960s–1980s by P. H. Nye and P. B. Tinker in Oxford and by S. A. Barber and J. H. Cushman at Purdue. Their "Barber–Cushman" model — radial diffusion and mass flow to a cylindrical root with Michaelis–Menten uptake at its surface — is the model solved in this laboratory, and it is still the core of modern root-system models.

Michaelis–Menten influx with a threshold(U1)
\[ J = \frac{I_{\max}\,(C_s - C_{\min})}{K_m + C_s - C_{\min}} \quad (C_s > C_{\min}), \qquad J = 0 \ \text{otherwise} \]

the net influx of an ion per unit root surface rises with its concentration at the root surface above a threshold, reaching half of the maximum when the excess concentration equals Km and approaching Imax at high concentration.

\(I_{\max}\)maximum influx at 20 °Cpmol cm⁻² s⁻¹10 · 5 · 3.26
\(K_m\)Michaelis constantµM25 · 20 · 5.8
\(C_{\min}\)concentration at which net influx is zeroµM2 · 2 · 0.2
\(C_s\)concentration in the solution at the root surfaceµMcomputed
Where does the equation come from?
  1. A carrier T binds the ion S outside, \(T + S \rightleftharpoons TS\), and releases it inside at rate \(k_{\text{cat}}[TS]\).
  2. At steady state the fraction of occupied carriers is \([TS]/T_{\text{tot}} = C/(K_m + C)\), so the influx is \(k_{\text{cat}}T_{\text{tot}}\,C/(K_m+C) = I_{\max}C/(K_m+C)\).
  3. Ions also leak out of the root. Claassen and Barber (1974) found that net influx becomes zero at a finite concentration Cmin; shifting the curve by Cmin gives Eq. U1, the form used in the Barber–Cushman model.
Assumptions and validity

One transport system per ion is assumed. Real roots have high- and low-affinity systems (for nitrate a high-affinity system with Km of roughly 6–100 µM and a low-affinity system that does not saturate even at tens of millimolar; Griffiths & York, 2020) and regulate Imax with their nutrient status. The default nitrate and phosphate values are the maize values used by Barber (reported by York et al., 2016, and Ruiz et al., 2020); the K⁺ Km follows Epstein et al. (1963), while its Imax and Cmin are illustrative values of the right order of magnitude.

Temperature effects(U2)
\[ I_{\max}(T) = I_{\max,20}\,Q_{10}^{(T-20)/10}, \qquad D_l(T) = D_{l,25}\,\frac{T+273.15}{298.15}\,\frac{\eta(25)}{\eta(T)} \]

Where do these come from?
  1. Carrier turnover is an enzyme-like process; over a limited temperature range its rate is well described by a constant Q₁₀, which is equivalent to an Arrhenius law with a fixed activation energy.
  2. Diffusion of a small ion follows the Stokes–Einstein relation \(D = k_BT/(6\pi\eta a)\): proportional to absolute temperature and inversely proportional to viscosity. Between 5 and 35 °C this more than doubles \(D_l\).
Assumptions and validity

Measured temperature responses are ion-specific and not constant: for maize roots, Bravo-F and Uribe (1981) found activation energies for K⁺ uptake of about 3 kcal mol⁻¹ above a transition near 13 °C but about 29 kcal mol⁻¹ below it, and for phosphate about 21 kcal mol⁻¹ between 13 and 22 °C and 10 kcal mol⁻¹ above — corresponding to Q₁₀ values from about 1.2 to above 3, and much larger in the cold. Km is kept constant.

\(Q_{10}\) and the ratios are dimensionless, so \(I_{\max}\) and \(D_l\) keep their units ✓
Effective diffusion coefficient in soil(U3)
\[ D_e = \frac{D_l\,\theta\,f}{b}, \qquad f = \frac{\theta^{7/3}}{\phi^{2}}, \qquad b = \frac{\mathrm{d}C_{\text{tot}}}{\mathrm{d}C_l} \]

the effective diffusion coefficient of an ion in soil equals its diffusion coefficient in water times the water content times an impedance factor for the tortuous pore paths, divided by the buffer power — the change in total (adsorbed plus dissolved) amount per unit soil volume for a unit change of solution concentration.

Follow the derivation
  1. Fick’s law in the soil solution, corrected for the cross-section occupied by water and for path tortuosity: flux \(F = -D_l\theta f\,\partial C_l/\partial x\).
  2. Conservation of the total amount: \(\partial C_{\text{tot}}/\partial t = -\partial F/\partial x\).
  3. With the buffer power, \(\partial C_{\text{tot}}/\partial t = b\,\partial C_l/\partial t\), so \(\partial C_l/\partial t = (D_l\theta f/b)\,\partial^2 C_l/\partial x^2 = D_e\,\partial^2C_l/\partial x^2\).
  4. Worked number for phosphate: \(f = 0.3^{7/3}/0.5^2 = 0.241\); \(D_e = 0.846\times10^{-9}\times0.3\times0.241/239 = 2.6\times10^{-13}\) m² s⁻¹. In one day it spreads \(2\sqrt{D_e t}\approx 0.3\) mm. Nitrate (\(b = \theta\)): \(D_e = 4.6\times10^{-10}\) m² s⁻¹ and about 13 mm per day.
Assumptions and validity

The buffer power is taken as constant (a linear adsorption isotherm). Real phosphate isotherms are curved and partly irreversible, and b varies from about ten in sandy soils to over a thousand in oxide-rich soils. The Millington–Quirk expression is one of several empirical impedance relations; measured f values also depend on texture (Barraclough & Tinker, 1981).

Radial transport to a root (Barber–Cushman)(U4)
\[ \frac{\partial C_l}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left( r D_e \frac{\partial C_l}{\partial r} + \frac{r_0 v_0}{b}\, C_l \right), \quad r_0 < r < r_1 \] \[ D_e b\,\frac{\partial C_l}{\partial r}\Big|_{r_0} + v_0 C_l(r_0) = J\big(C_l(r_0)\big), \qquad D_e b\,\frac{\partial C_l}{\partial r}\Big|_{r_1} + \frac{r_0 v_0}{r_1} C_l(r_1) = 0 \]

the solution concentration at distance r from the root axis changes because ions diffuse and are carried by water flowing radially inwards; at the root surface the diffusive plus convective supply equals the Michaelis–Menten uptake, and half-way to the neighbouring root no ions cross.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(r\)radial distance from the root axism
\(r_0\)root radius (0.2 mm) — or the effective radius with hairs (Eq. U8)m
\(v_0\)water influx at the root surfacem s⁻¹
\(r_1\)half-distance between roots (Eq. U7)m
Follow the derivation
  1. Consider a thin cylindrical shell of soil between r and r + dr, per unit root length. Its content of the ion is \(2\pi r\,\mathrm{d}r\,bC_l\).
  2. The inward flux through a cylinder of radius r, per unit length, is \(2\pi r\,(D_e b\,\partial C_l/\partial r) + 2\pi r_0 v_0 C_l\) — diffusion plus the water flux, which is the same through every cylinder (water conservation: \(r v(r) = r_0 v_0\)).
  3. The content changes by the difference of the fluxes at r + dr and r. Dividing by \(2\pi r\,\mathrm{d}r\,b\) gives the partial differential equation above.
  4. Initially \(C_l = C_b\) everywhere. The simulator writes the equation in \(u=\ln r\), which gives fine resolution near the root, and integrates it with the implicit Euler method; the non-linear uptake boundary condition is linearised and iterated three times per step.
Assumptions and validity

Single, straight, non-growing root; soil homogeneous and isotropic; constant water content and water influx; no root exudates, microbial activity or mineralisation; linear buffering. The model predicts phosphate and potassium uptake well in many pot experiments but tends to under-predict at low supply, where root hairs, mycorrhizas and rhizosphere chemistry matter (see Samal et al., 2010, for K⁺).

Quasi-steady supply to a cylinder(U5)
\[ C_l(r) = C_s + (C_b - C_s)\,\frac{\ln(r/r_0)}{\ln(R/r_0)}, \qquad J = \frac{D_l\,\theta f\,(C_b - C_s)}{r_0 \ln(R/r_0)}, \qquad R \approx r_0 + 2\sqrt{D_e t} \]

when the depletion zone reaches out to radius R, the concentration rises logarithmically from the root surface to the bulk, and the supply to the root is proportional to the concentration difference divided by the logarithm of the ratio of radii.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(R\)outer radius of the depletion zonem
\(C_s, C_b\)surface and bulk concentrationmol m⁻³
Follow the derivation
  1. Without mass flow and at steady state, Eq. U4 becomes \(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\big(r\,\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}r}\big) = 0\), so \(r\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}r = B\) is constant and \(C = A + B\ln r\).
  2. With \(C(r_0)=C_s\) and \(C(R)=C_b\): \(B = (C_b-C_s)/\ln(R/r_0)\).
  3. The flux density at the root surface is \(D_e b\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}r = D_l\theta f\,B/r_0\). Note that b cancels: at steady state the buffer power no longer matters — it only controls how fast R grows, \(R-r_0\approx2\sqrt{D_e t}\).
  4. Setting this supply equal to U1 and solving for Cs gives the influx. Worked example (phosphate, θ = 0.3, b = 239, Cb = 5 µM, t = 1 d): R = 0.50 mm, \(\ln(R/r_0) = 0.91\), maximum supply \(D_l\theta f C_b/(r_0\ln(R/r_0)) = 0.17\) pmol cm⁻² s⁻¹; solving with U1 gives Cs ≈ 0.48 µM and J ≈ 0.15 pmol cm⁻² s⁻¹ — about a tenth of the 1.48 pmol cm⁻² s⁻¹ the same root would take up from a stirred 5 µM solution.

An approximation to the full transient solution of U4, useful for reasoning and hand calculation. It breaks down when neighbouring depletion zones overlap (R > r₁) and when mass flow is large.

Compare with the activation energies measured by Bravo-F and Uribe (1981) for maize — convert them into Q₁₀ values with \(Q_{10} = \exp\big(\tfrac{E_a}{R}\,\tfrac{10}{T_1T_2}\big)\). Would heating the nutrient solution be a sensible use of energy?

  6. What a lettuce root system must do

    A 200 g lettuce head with 5 % dry matter and 4 % N in its dry matter contains how many millimoles of N? If it takes this up in 30 days, what root length is needed in (a) a stirred solution with 15 mM nitrate and (b) the default soil (5 mM nitrate, Lv = 1 cm cm⁻³), reading the Uptake per metre of root readout on day 2 and on day 10? Why does the soil answer depend on the day you read it, and what replenishes soil nitrate in a real field that the model leaves out (mineralisation, fertiliser, mass flow from deeper layers)? Then estimate the ATP cost at the membrane (Eq. U10).

Model assumptions

  • A single root segment of radius 0.2 mm (lateral-root value used by Ruiz et al., 2020) in a cylinder of soil of radius r₁ = 1/√(πLv); the soil is homogeneous, isotropic and at constant water content; the root does not grow and its uptake properties do not change with time.
  • Influx follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics with a threshold Cmin (Eq. U1); one transport system per ion; Imax scales with temperature by a constant Q₁₀ and Km is temperature-independent. Default kinetics: nitrate and phosphate from maize (Barber’s values as reported by York et al., 2016, and Ruiz et al., 2020); K⁺ Km from Epstein et al. (1963); K⁺ Imax = 5 and Cmin = 2 are illustrative values of the correct order of magnitude, not measurements for lettuce.
  • Soil transport by diffusion and mass flow only (Barber–Cushman). Linear adsorption with a constant buffer power; impedance factor from Millington & Quirk (1961) with porosity 0.5; no mineralisation, exudates, pH changes or microbial uptake in the rhizosphere; no mycorrhizas.
  • Root hairs are represented by an effective absorbing cylinder plus their membrane area (Eq. U8), a simplification of the explicit treatment by Itoh and Barber (1983).
  • Hydroponics: bulk solution well mixed and constant (continuously replenished); an unstirred layer of 50 µm is assumed; the finite volume of a real tank and its declining concentration are not modelled.
  • The 3D scenes are illustrative. Root architecture, cell dimensions, protein shapes (cylinders for transmembrane helices) and the number of ions shown are schematic; the ion density in the root-hair scene and the transport event rates in the membrane scene are driven by the computed concentrations and fluxes, and the membrane animation is slowed down enormously. Membrane potential and pH values are typical textbook values, not computed.
