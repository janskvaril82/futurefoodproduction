Ammonia equilibrium and toxicity

Your test kit says “TAN 1.5 mg L⁻¹” — is that safe? It depends on how much of it is un-ionised ammonia, NH₃, the form that crosses fish gills, and that depends on pH and temperature.   1. Read the map. Colour shows which percentage of TAN is un-ionised NH₃. The thin white contours mark 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10 and 30 %. They run diagonally: the same share is reached at a lower pH in warm water than in cold water.

Key idea

A TAN value alone never tells you whether fish are safe. Always convert it to un-ionised ammonia with the pH and temperature at the time of day when pH is highest. The same 1 mg L⁻¹ of TAN is harmless in a cool, slightly acidic trout tank and dangerous in a warm, alkaline pond on a sunny afternoon.

Why only part of the ammonia is toxic

Dissolved ammonia exists in two forms that interconvert in microseconds: the ammonium ion NH₄⁺ and un-ionised ammonia NH₃. Their ratio is set by the acid–base equilibrium \(\text{NH}_4^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_3 + \text{H}^+\). Analyses report their sum as total ammonia nitrogen (TAN, in mg N L⁻¹). The charge makes the difference for fish: NH₄⁺ crosses membranes poorly, whereas NH₃ is a small, uncharged, lipid-soluble molecule that diffuses across the gill epithelium. Ammonia accumulating in the fish depolarises neurons and disturbs the brain's glutamate–glutamine balance; high levels cause convulsions and death (Randall & Tsui, 2002). This is why water-quality criteria are expressed as un-ionised ammonia and why the same TAN can be harmless or lethal.

The two control knobs: pH and temperature

The share of NH₃ is \(f = 1/(1 + 10^{\text{p}K_a - \text{pH}})\), with \(\text{p}K_a \approx 9.25\) at 25 °C (Eq. T1). Far below the pKa — the normal situation in fish culture — each pH unit multiplies \(f\) by ten. Temperature acts through the pKa, which falls by about 0.03 per °C (Eq. T2, Emerson et al., 1975): warming from 10 to 30 °C lowers the pKa from 9.73 to 9.10 and raises the NH₃ share about fourfold at constant pH.

Honest limits of the data

Toxicity depends on fish size, acclimation, oxygen, CO₂, exposure time and many other factors, and published values scatter widely. The chronic values above are conservative benchmarks, not sharp boundaries. For carp and perch we did not find acute values we could verify for this laboratory, so only the chronic benchmark is used.

The afternoon peak

In ponds and in sunlit tanks with algae, photosynthesis removes CO₂ during the day and respiration returns it at night. Because \(\text{pH} \approx \text{p}K_1 + \log_{10}([\text{HCO}_3^-]/[\text{CO}_2])\), removing CO₂ raises the pH — in algae-rich ponds often by one to two units between dawn and late afternoon (Boyd & Tucker, 1998). Water temperature peaks at about the same time. Both effects push the NH₃ share up, so fish meet their highest NH₃ exposure in the afternoon even when TAN is constant; measurements taken in the morning underestimate the risk. In a recirculating system without algae the swing is small and comes from the CO₂ produced after feeding.

Nitrite and the chloride shield

Nitrite, the intermediate of nitrification, is taken up across the gills by the chloride-uptake pathway, accumulates in the blood and oxidises haemoglobin to methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen; the blood turns brown (Kroupová et al., 2005). Chloride competes for the same uptake route, so the chloride concentration largely decides how toxic a given nitrite level is. EIFAC (1984) therefore recommends monitoring the weight ratio Cl⁻ : NO₂⁻-N and keeping it at or above 17 for salmonids and 8 for coarse fish (reviewed by Kroupová et al., 2005). Species with high chloride uptake, such as trout, perch and pike, are more sensitive than carp, tench and eel (Williams & Eddy, 1986, in Kroupová et al., 2005). Recirculating systems therefore often keep chloride at 100–150 mg L⁻¹ as a safeguard (Popma & Masser, 1999) — but in aquaponics the sodium that comes with the salt accumulates and can harm the plants.

Common misconception

“Lowering the pH removes the ammonia.” Acid does not remove a single molecule of TAN; it only converts NH₃ into NH₄⁺. The ammonia returns as NH₃ as soon as pH rises again, and a low pH slows the nitrifying bacteria that would really remove it (see the aquaponics system simulator).

The equations behind the laboratory

Share of TAN present as un-ionised NH₃(T1)
\[ f = \frac{[\text{NH}_3]}{[\text{NH}_3]+[\text{NH}_4^+]} = \frac{1}{1+10^{\,\text{p}K_a-\text{pH}}} \]

the fraction of total ammonia that is un-ionised equals one divided by one plus ten to the power of the difference between the pKa and the pH.

Follow the derivation
  1. Equilibrium: \(K_a = [\text{NH}_3][\text{H}^+]/[\text{NH}_4^+]\), so \([\text{NH}_4^+]/[\text{NH}_3] = [\text{H}^+]/K_a = 10^{\text{p}K_a-\text{pH}}\).
  2. Divide numerator and denominator of \(f\) by \([\text{NH}_3]\): \(f = 1/(1 + [\text{NH}_4^+]/[\text{NH}_3]) = 1/(1+10^{\text{p}K_a-\text{pH}})\).
  3. When \(\text{pH} \ll \text{p}K_a\): \(f \approx 10^{\text{pH}-\text{p}K_a}\) — a straight line of slope 1 on a log scale: ×10 per pH unit.
  4. At \(\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a\), \(f = 0.5\) (dotted line on the map).
Assumptions and validity

Activities are replaced by concentrations: valid for fresh water. In brackish and sea water the ionic strength raises the pKa slightly and the NH₃ share is a little lower.

Why the peak is so large

Because \(f\) grows exponentially with pH, the daily mean of NH₃ is dominated by the afternoon hours: with \(A = 0.9\) the peak is ≈ 8× the pH-mean value (\(10^{0.9}\)) and ≈ 60× the dawn value. A sinusoid is a simplification; real swings are asymmetric and depend on sunlight, algae and CO₂ exchange.

  1. One pH unit versus how many degrees?

    At TAN 1 mg L⁻¹, record NH₃-N at pH 7.0 and 8.0 (20 °C) and at 10 °C and 30 °C (pH 7.5). Express each change as a factor. How many degrees of warming have the same effect as one pH unit? Compare with Eq. T4.

    Use the New system start-up preset. How much salt must be added to a 6 m³ system to protect tilapia (ratio 8) and how much for perch (ratio 17)? How much sodium does each add, and why would an aquaponic grower hesitate?

  5. Species for Nordic aquaponics

    Compare tilapia, rainbow trout and Eurasian perch at the temperature each prefers (tilapia 28 °C, trout 14 °C, perch 20 °C) and pH 7.2. Which species tolerates the highest TAN at its own temperature? Combine this with energy use for heating in a Swedish winter to argue for one species.

Model assumptions

  • Fresh water at low ionic strength; activity coefficients are taken as 1 (Eq. T1). The Emerson et al. (1975) pKa fit is valid from 0 to 50 °C.
  • Toxicity is attributed to un-ionised ammonia only; ammonium is treated as non-toxic, and interactions with oxygen, CO₂ and acclimation are not modelled.
  • Thresholds (Table 2) are benchmarks from the cited sources; values reported as NH₃ are converted to NH₃-N with 14.01/17.03. Where the unit basis of a published value is not stated explicitly, it is taken as NH₃.
  • The diurnal cycle is a pair of cosines with fixed peak times (Eq. T5); TAN is held constant.
  • Nitrite protection uses the EIFAC (1984) weight ratios; applying the salmonid ratio to perch is our conservative assumption based on its high chloride uptake. The HNO₂ fraction is neglected above pH 5.
