Sensory booth

Walk into a row of ISO 8589-style tasting booths and prepare a real study. Choose a triangle, 9-point hedonic or paired-preference test; the laboratory builds a balanced serving plan (six triangle arrangements or a Williams Latin square), gives every cup a random three-digit code and serves each panellist's tray through the hatch in the right order. Switch the booth light between white, red and green and a spectral colour model tells you whether the colour of the cricket bread can still give the answer away. Then print the ballots and the serving sheet — or save the design to your project.

Camera

Position balance

How often each product is served in each position across the whole panel. In a balanced design all bars of one position are equal, so no product is favoured by being tasted first or last.

First-order carry-over

How often the row product is served immediately before the column product. White cells are at the panel average; red cells occur more often, blue cells less often. A Williams design makes all off-diagonal cells equal (all white), so every product follows every other product equally often.

Why coloured light masks colour

Reflectance spectra of the products (illustrative bread crumbs, left axis) and the spectrum of the booth light (right axis). Under narrow-band light only the reflectance inside the band can be seen.

Colour cue under each light

CIELAB colour difference ΔE*ab between the control (A) and each test product, computed from the spectra for an observer adapted to each light. Bars below the dashed line are hard to see (about one just-noticeable difference).

Serving sheet (panel leader only)

The complete plan: sequence, booth, serving round and the code in every position. Click a row to serve that panellist. Magenta codes mark the odd sample of a triangle test — the answer key. Print it from Outputs in the control panel.

A guided tour of the booth

  1. Look at the architecture. Press Row overview, then Preparation side. The assessors sit in a quiet, odour-free testing area; the samples are prepared and coded in a separate room and pass through a hatch with a sliding door. Nobody walks through the preparation area to reach the booths, and nobody in a booth can see what is being prepared. Click a hatch to open or close it.
  2. Sit down. Press Assessor's view. The side partitions reach beyond the counter so that you cannot see your neighbour's face or tray; the booth interior is a matt, neutral grey; the light panel above is uniform and shadow-free; a grille removes odours; a small sink lets you rinse and expectorate; the READY button calls the next sample. On the tray: coded cups, water and unsalted crackers, an expectoration cup. The tablet shows your ballot.
  3. Change the light. With the preset Triangle · 24 · red light, switch between white, red and green. The cups change colour, the spectra chart shows which wavelengths are available, and the colour-cue readout tells you whether the darker cricket crumb is still visible (ΔE*ab above about 2.3). Now raise the colour difference slider: red light hides a small browning difference but not a large one.
  4. Serve the panel. Press ▶ (or the space bar with the stage focused) to step through the panellists. Each booth shows the tray of the panellist sitting there in the current round; at the end of a round the hatches open and the next trays slide in. Every panellist gets a different arrangement and fresh codes — compare the sheet and the cups. Click a cup to see the panel leader's key.
  5. Check the balance. For a triangle test the position chart shows A and B equally often in every position when the panel size is a multiple of six. Choose 9-pt hedonic with 3 products and 24 panellists: the Williams design uses 6 sequences, each four times, and the carry-over matrix is uniform. Change to 25 panellists and watch the balance break.
  6. Make it real. Enter your own product names, print the ballots and the serving sheet, and press Save to project. The triangle-test and hedonic laboratories — and the project's sensory study designer — read the same data from this browser. If you run the session on a laptop in the booth, record each panellist's answer in the ballot card below the 3D view.
Key idea

A sensory booth is a measuring instrument in which the human is the sensor. Everything else in the room exists to remove information that is not the product's taste, smell or texture: other people's reactions, the look of the preparation, the meaning of a code, the colour of the crumb, and the order in which the samples arrive.

What the booth controls — and why

Sensory analysis uses people as instruments. People are extraordinarily sensitive, but they also use every scrap of information they can find: a colleague's grimace, the smell of the kitchen, a code that reads like a grade, a crumb that looks darker. ISO 8589:2007 describes test rooms designed to conduct evaluations “under known and controlled conditions with a minimum of distractions”, and ISO 4120:2021 lists the conditions under which triangle tests are carried out (see Lesson 13.3). This laboratory turns those recommendations into a model you can inspect and adjust.

Colour: the cue that gives the answer away

Bread with cricket flour is darker and redder than wheat bread. In a triangle test that asks whether people can taste the difference, a darker crumb would let everybody pick the odd sample by eye, and the result would say nothing about flavour. ISO 8589 therefore mentions dimmers, coloured light sources, coloured filters and monochromatic light as ways to mask unwanted colour differences.

Why does coloured light work? The colour signal that reaches the eye is the product of three spectra: the light source \(S(\lambda)\), the reflectance of the surface \(R(\lambda)\) and the sensitivities of our three cone types, summarised by the CIE colour-matching functions (Eq. B4). A browned crumb absorbs strongly in the blue and green and less in the red. Under white light both differences — in lightness and in hue — are visible. Under a narrow red LED only the reflectance around 630 nm is available: all surfaces become shades of the same red and the hue difference collapses. What remains is the lightness difference at that wavelength. The spectral chart shows this directly: where the two reflectance curves are close inside the light band, the products look alike.

A self-luminous ballot undoes the mask

A bright tablet or phone screen in a red-lit booth is a white light source that shines on the samples from a few centimetres away. Dim the screen, tilt it away from the tray, or use paper ballots in discrimination tests with masking light.

Not every test should hide the colour

In a hedonic test with consumers, the appearance of the product is part of what they like or dislike, and ISO 8589 notes that lighting typical of where the product is eaten may be chosen for consumer tests. Mask colour when the question is about taste or texture; show it when the question is about the product as people will meet it.

Codes: blind, meaningless and fresh

Each cup carries a random three-digit code (ISO 4120:2021, clause 5.5). Letters and single digits carry meaning — “A” sounds like a grade, “1” like “first” or “best” — and so do “memorable” numbers such as 123, 555 or 911. The code generator therefore draws from the 865 codes between 100 and 999 without repeated digits (111), without runs (123, 321) and without round hundreds or 666, 911 and 112. It draws without replacement, so that no code is used twice in the study, and fresh codes for every panellist are the default: panellists who talk after the session cannot tell each other “583 was the cricket bread”.

Serving orders: position and carry-over

The first sample of a session is often rated differently from later ones, and a strongly flavoured sample changes the perception of the next one. If everybody tasted the products in the same order, these effects would be confounded with the product differences. For the triangle test, ISO 4120:2021 (clause 7.1) prescribes the six arrangements ABB, AAB, ABA, BAA, BBA and BAB, used equally often and distributed at random in blocks of six. For rating tests the laboratory uses Williams (1949) Latin squares (Eq. B1), which balance both the serving position and the sample served immediately before — the design that MacFie et al. (1989) introduced to consumer testing. A paired preference test alternates AB and BA. Sequences are assigned to panellists in shuffled blocks, so that balance is complete after every full block even if the session stops early.

Running a session

Booths are used in rounds. With four booths and 24 panellists there are six rounds; the time per round is the instruction time plus the tasting time of every sample plus the rinsing pauses between them (Eq. B6). Serve each tray only when the assessor presses READY, keep samples at the temperature at which the product is normally eaten, give identical portions, and give no information about the samples until all panellists are finished. Water and unsalted crackers are the usual palate cleansers. The ethics of tasting studies — informed consent, allergens such as the cross-reactivity of insect proteins with crustaceans and dust mites, anonymity and the GDPR — are covered in Lesson 13.3.

The models behind the booth

Williams design for rating tests(B1)
\[ a = (0,\;1,\;t-1,\;2,\;t-2,\;\dots), \qquad s_{i,j} = (a_j + i) \bmod t \] \[ N_{\text{seq}} = \begin{cases} t & \text{if } t \text{ is even} \\ 2t & \text{if } t \text{ is odd} \end{cases} \]

write the first serving sequence by alternating between the low and the high end of the product numbers; each further sequence adds one to every entry, wrapping around after t minus one. For an even number of products these t sequences balance carry-over; for an odd number, their mirror images are added, giving 2t sequences.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(t\)number of products each panellist rates–
\(a_j\)product number (0 … \(t-1\)) in position \(j\) of the first sequence–
\(s_{i,j}\)product in position \(j\) of sequence \(i\)–
\(N_{\text{seq}}\)number of sequences in a complete design–
Follow the derivation
  1. Positions. Adding \(i\) modulo \(t\) to a permutation gives another permutation, and column \(j\) contains \(a_j, a_j+1, \dots, a_j+t-1\) (mod \(t\)): every product once per position — a Latin square.
  2. Carry-over. “Product \(u\) followed by \(v\)” occurs in row \(i\) at positions \(j, j+1\) when \(v-u \equiv a_{j+1}-a_j\). The zig-zag gives the differences \(1, -2, 3, -4, \dots\), which for even \(t\) are \(t-1\) distinct non-zero residues, so every ordered pair occurs exactly once.
  3. Odd \(t\). The differences repeat (for \(t=3\): \(1\) and \(-2\equiv1\)); adding the reversed sequences supplies the missing differences and every ordered pair occurs twice in \(2t\) sequences (Williams, 1949).
  4. Randomisation. The laboratory relabels the products at random (a relabelling keeps both balances) and assigns the sequences to panellists in shuffled blocks of \(N_{\text{seq}}\).
Assumptions and validity

Balance holds for first-order carry-over only and is exact only when the number of panellists is a multiple of \(N_{\text{seq}}\). Balance does not remove order effects — it spreads them evenly over the products, so that they end up in the error variance instead of biasing the means (MacFie et al., 1989).

All quantities are integer labels or counts ✓
Balanced triangle arrangements(B2)
\[ \mathcal{S} = \{\text{ABB},\ \text{AAB},\ \text{ABA},\ \text{BAA},\ \text{BBA},\ \text{BAB}\}, \qquad n_{A,j} = n_{B,j} = \frac{n}{2}\ \ \text{for } n = 6m \]

the six arrangements of two products in three positions are used equally often; when the panel size is a multiple of six, each product then appears in each position half of the time, and each product is the odd sample for exactly half of the panel.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathcal{S}\)set of arrangements (A = control, B = test product)–
\(n\), \(m\)number of panellists; number of complete blocks of six–
\(n_{A,j}\)number of times product A is served in position \(j\)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Choosing which of the three positions holds the odd sample (3 ways) and which product is odd (2 ways) gives \(3\times2=6\) arrangements.
  2. Position 1 holds A in ABB, AAB and ABA and B in the other three; positions 2 and 3 likewise. Each block of six therefore serves A and B three times in every position.
  3. The product that occurs once is the odd one: A is odd in ABB, BBA and BAB, B in AAB, ABA and BAA. Each product is therefore the odd sample in half of the arrangements, which balances any tendency to pick, say, the darker or the saltier sample.
Assumptions and validity

ISO 4120:2021 (clause 7.1) requires the arrangements to be distributed at random in groups of six. If \(n\) is not a multiple of six, the last block is incomplete; the imbalance is small but should be reported. The test itself is analysed with the exact binomial distribution with \(p_0 = 1/3\) (see the triangle-test laboratory).

Counts of servings — dimensionless ✓
Why codes are drawn without replacement(B3)
\[ P(\text{some code repeats}) = 1 - \prod_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{M}\right) \approx 1 - \exp\!\left(-\frac{m(m-1)}{2M}\right) \]

if m codes are drawn independently and with replacement from M possible codes, the probability that at least two of them coincide is one minus the probability that all are different — the product of the chances that each new code misses all earlier ones.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue here
\(m\)number of codes needed (panellists × samples)–24 × 3 = 72
\(M\)number of admissible three-digit codes–865
Follow the derivation
  1. The first code can be anything; the second must avoid 1 code (probability \(1-1/M\)); the \((i+1)\)-th must avoid \(i\) codes (\(1-i/M\)).
  2. Multiplying gives the probability that all \(m\) differ; its complement is the repeat probability (the “birthday problem”).
  3. Because \(\ln(1-x)\approx -x\) for small \(x\), \(\sum_i \ln(1-i/M) \approx -\sum_i i/M = -m(m-1)/(2M)\).
  4. With \(m = 72\) and \(M = 865\): \(1-\exp(-72\cdot71/1730) = 0.948\) (exact 0.952). A repeated code is almost certain — which is why the generator draws without replacement.
Probabilities are dimensionless ✓
Colour stimulus of a surface under a light(B4)
\[ X = k\sum_{\lambda} S(\lambda)\,R(\lambda)\,\bar x(\lambda)\,\Delta\lambda,\quad Y = k\sum_{\lambda} S\,R\,\bar y\,\Delta\lambda,\quad Z = k\sum_{\lambda} S\,R\,\bar z\,\Delta\lambda, \qquad k = \frac{100}{\sum_\lambda S(\lambda)\,\bar y(\lambda)\,\Delta\lambda} \]

the three tristimulus values of a surface are the light's spectrum times the surface reflectance times each colour-matching function, summed over wavelength; the constant k scales them so that a perfect white surface under the same light has Y equal to 100.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(S(\lambda)\)relative spectral power of the booth light (6500 K Planckian, or LED bands at 630 or 525 nm)relative
\(R(\lambda)\)diffuse reflectance of the sample surface– (0–1)
\(\bar x, \bar y, \bar z\)CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (analytic fit of Wyman et al., 2013)–
\(X, Y, Z\)tristimulus values; \(Y\) is the luminance factor in %–
\(\Delta\lambda\)wavelength step (5 nm, 380–780 nm)nm
Follow the derivation
  1. The light reaching the eye from a matt surface is \(S(\lambda)R(\lambda)\) (per unit wavelength).
  2. Human colour vision reduces this spectrum to three numbers. The CIE (2004) standard observer defines them as the overlap of the stimulus with \(\bar x, \bar y, \bar z\); \(\bar y\) equals the photopic luminous efficiency, so \(Y\) is proportional to luminance.
  3. For a perfect white, \(R = 1\), so \(k\sum S\bar y\,\Delta\lambda = 100\) fixes \(k\).
  4. For a narrow band centred on \(\lambda_0\), \(S\) is almost zero elsewhere: \(X, Y, Z \approx R(\lambda_0)\times(X_w, Y_w, Z_w)\). Every surface then has the chromaticity of the light itself and differs only in \(Y\) — the physics of colour masking.
Assumptions and validity

The reflectance spectra of the breads are illustrative logistic curves (rising from blue to red, darker and redder with browning), not measurements. The model ignores gloss, texture, translucency and the angular geometry of the booth light. The 6500 K Planckian is used as a stand-in for a daylight-type lamp.

\(S\,R\,\bar x\,\Delta\lambda\) and \(S\,\bar y\,\Delta\lambda\) have the same units, so \(X, Y, Z\) are dimensionless percentages ✓
CIELAB colour difference(B5)
\[ L^* = 116\,f\!\left(\tfrac{Y}{Y_w}\right) - 16,\quad a^* = 500\left[f\!\left(\tfrac{X}{X_w}\right) - f\!\left(\tfrac{Y}{Y_w}\right)\right],\quad b^* = 200\left[f\!\left(\tfrac{Y}{Y_w}\right) - f\!\left(\tfrac{Z}{Z_w}\right)\right] \] \[ \Delta E^*_{ab} = \sqrt{(\Delta L^*)^2 + (\Delta a^*)^2 + (\Delta b^*)^2}, \qquad f(q) = \begin{cases} q^{1/3} & q > 216/24389 \\ (24389q/27 + 16)/116 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

lightness is a cube-root function of the luminance relative to white; a-star measures red versus green and b-star yellow versus blue. The colour difference between two samples is the straight-line distance between them in this three-dimensional space.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(L^*, a^*, b^*\)CIELAB lightness, red–green and yellow–blue coordinates–
\(X_w, Y_w, Z_w\)tristimulus values of a perfect white under the same light (the adapted white)–
\(\Delta E^*_{ab}\)colour difference; about 2.3 is one just-noticeable difference (Mahy et al., 1994)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Perceived lightness is roughly a cube root of luminance; CIELAB (CIE, 2004) uses this to make equal distances correspond approximately to equal perceived differences.
  2. Dividing by the white of the same light models an observer adapted to the booth: after a minute under red light, white paper looks “white-ish”.
  3. Under a narrow band, \(X/X_w = Y/Y_w = Z/Z_w = R(\lambda_0)\), so \(a^* = b^* = 0\) and \(\Delta E^*_{ab} = |\Delta L^*|\): only the lightness difference at the band survives.
  4. Worked number (default spectra, browning 0.45): white light — A (85.6, −0.6, 29.0), B (73.6, 6.5, 31.5), \(\Delta E = \sqrt{12.0^2+7.1^2+2.5^2} = 14.2\); red light — A (90.7, 0.1, 0.4), B (82.5, 0.2, 0.9), \(\Delta E = 8.2\). Red light removes the hue part but a lightness difference of 8 remains clearly visible.
Assumptions and validity

ΔE*ab (CIE76) is used for transparency; the newer CIEDE2000 formula is more uniform. Just-noticeable differences depend on viewing conditions, and very dim light additionally reduces colour discrimination, which the model does not include.

All CIELAB coordinates are dimensionless, and so is ΔE ✓
Session length(B6)
\[ T = \left\lceil \frac{n}{B} \right\rceil \bigl(t_0 + L\,t_s + (L-1)\,t_r\bigr) + \left(\left\lceil \frac{n}{B} \right\rceil - 1\right) t_c \]

the session takes as many rounds as panellists divided by booths, rounded up; each round lasts the instruction time plus the tasting time of every sample plus the rinsing pauses between samples, and each change of round costs a changeover time.

Investigation tasks

Use the booth to answer. Tick each task when you are done; Save investigation copies your settings and readouts to your notebook.

  1. How much colour can red light hide?

    Choose a triangle test and red light. Raise the colour difference from 0 until the colour cue exceeds ΔE*ab ≈ 2.3. Repeat with white and green light. Report the three limits, explain the order using the spectra chart, and decide which light you would use for bread with 5 % and with 15 % cricket flour.

  2. Balanced or not?

    For hedonic tests with 3, 4 and 5 products, find the smallest panel of at least 20 panellists that is completely balanced (sequence use, position and carry-over readouts all green). State the rule and explain it with Eq. B1. What would you do if only 26 people come?

  3. Plan your session

    Enter the method, products and panel size of your project. How long will the session take with 2, 4 and 5 booths? How many booths do you need to finish within 90 minutes? Which assumption of Eq. B6 is most likely to be wrong on the day?

  4. A classroom instead of a laboratory

    Apply the Cramped classroom preset and inspect the booth from the assessor's view with the ISO dimensions switched on. List every recommendation of ISO 8589 that is not met and propose a cheap fix for each (cardboard dividers, a separate preparation room, coloured bulbs, a signalling card).

  5. Codes that tell stories

    Using Eq. B3, compute the probability of at least one repeated code if the 72 codes of a 24-panellist triangle test were drawn with replacement. Then explain, with an example, why “fresh codes for every panellist” protects a study whose panellists talk to each other in the corridor.

  6. Your own study

    Enter your product names, generate the plan, print the ballots and the serving sheet and save the design to the project. Run a pilot with two classmates, record their answers in the ballot card, and open the triangle-test or hedonic laboratory to see that the data arrive.

Model assumptions

  • Booth geometry. Counter height 0.76 m, dividers from the floor to a soffit at 2.05 m, extending 0.32 m beyond the counter; hatch 0.38 m high. Width and depth are adjustable; the ISO 8589 minimum values used for the checks are 0.9 m and 0.6 m.
  • Serving orders. Williams designs for rating tests (randomly relabelled), the six ISO 4120 arrangements for triangle tests and AB/BA for paired preference, assigned in shuffled blocks. With “fresh codes” every cup in the study has a different code; otherwise each product copy has one code for the whole study.
  • Codes. 865 admissible codes from 100 to 999 (no triple digits, no runs of three consecutive digits, no round hundreds, no 666, 911 or 112). The rule is a convention adopted for this course, not part of any standard.
  • Colour model. Reflectance spectra of the breads are illustrative logistic curves, not measured data; the light sources are a 6500 K Planckian radiator and Gaussian LED bands (630 nm, FWHM 20 nm; 525 nm, FWHM 35 nm). Colour-matching functions use the analytic fit of Wyman et al. (2013); ΔE*ab is computed for an observer fully adapted to each light. The 3D rendering uses RGB lighting, so the colours of the cups are an approximation of the spectral calculation shown in the charts.
  • Timing and detectability. Instruction time 1.5 min and changeover 2 min per round are planning assumptions; the detectable difference assumes an error SD of 1.2 scale points.
  • Lighting in 3D. Area lights do not cast shadows and are not blocked by walls; a spot light in the active booth provides shadows. The 3D scene is illustrative; the design tables and the colour calculation are the computed quantities.
