Drone NDVI survey

A 6-hectare spring-wheat field near Västerås at stem elongation, just before the supplementary nitrogen dose. Somewhere in it are nitrogen-starved patches, waterlogged hollows, a compacted headland and creeping-thistle patches — but you cannot see them in the numbers yet. Plan a multispectral drone flight, watch the orthomosaic build up image by image, turn it into management zones and a variable-rate nitrogen prescription, and then walk into the crop to discover whether your map told the truth.

What to look at first

  1. Watch the survey fly. The drone takes off from the orange pad on the farm track, climbs to its flight height and flies the lawn-mower pattern: straight lines along the field, turning beyond the edge. Each time it fires the camera, the cyan footprint on the ground is added to the map — both on the crop in 3D and in the orthomosaic below. Choose Camera → Follow drone to ride along.
  2. Read the true-colour and the index maps. Switch Map layer between True colour and Vegetation index. The paler, yellower patches in true colour become obvious low-NDVI patches; the wheel tracks every 24 m become brown lines. Weed patches look lush in both.
  3. Trade detail against time. Lower the flight height to 30 m: the ground sampling distance falls to about 2 cm and the number of images and flight lines explodes; the camera cannot fire fast enough and the forward overlap drops (warning in the readouts). At 120 m the whole field takes under 11 minutes — and the map is still more than detailed enough for nitrogen zones.
  4. Make zones and a prescription. When the survey is complete, the index is smoothed, clustered into k zones and each zone receives a nitrogen rate. Compare the rules: the linear compensating rule (more nitrogen where the crop is thin), the reinforcing rule (β < 0) and the sufficiency-index rule that compares each zone with the N-rich strip.
  5. Reveal the truth. Choose Hidden causes. Which low-index zones are really short of nitrogen, and which are waterlogged or compacted — places where extra nitrogen is wasted and may leach? Look at Net return and N surplus for each rule.
  6. Ground-truth it. Click anywhere on the crop (or on the map). The camera walks you into a 12 m × 8 m plot built from the hidden model — pale, thin nitrogen-starved plants, stunted plants in waterlogged hollows with standing water, dark thistles overtopping the wheat — with a label giving the plant data an agronomist would record.
Key idea

A drone map shows where the crop differs, not why. Variable-rate nitrogen only pays where the low index is caused by nitrogen shortage. Zones must be checked in the field (ground-truthing) before a prescription is sent to the spreader — the “null hypothesis” of precision agriculture is that uniform management is good enough until proven otherwise (Whelan & McBratney, 2000).

From flight plan to prescription map

A drone survey for nitrogen management has five steps, each of which is simulated here with a physical or statistical model: (1) plan a flight that images every part of the field with enough overlap to build a mosaic; (2) record calibrated reflectance in the red, red-edge and near-infrared bands; (3) compute a vegetation index for every cell of the mosaic; (4) summarise the map into a few management zones; (5) assign a nitrogen rate to each zone and export the prescription to a variable-rate spreader (see Lesson 11.1 and Lesson 11.2).

1 · Flight planning

The camera's ground sampling distance (GSD) grows linearly with flight height (Eq. D1). The image footprint sets how far apart the flight lines can be for a given side overlap, and how often the camera must fire for a given forward overlap (Eq. D2). Overlaps of about 70–80 % in both directions are needed because the mosaicking software matches features between neighbouring images; over a uniform crop canopy there are few distinct features, so generous overlap is the norm (see Worked example 3 in Lesson 11.2). The number of images and the flight time then follow from the field size and the ground speed. Lower flights give finer detail but more flight lines, more images and more batteries — and a camera that must fire faster than it can store images. In the EU, drones in the open category may fly no higher than 120 m above the ground (Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2019/947).

2 · Reflectance of the crop

Every 1 m cell of the virtual field has a leaf area index, a leaf chlorophyll content, a soil moisture and a weed cover set by the hidden causes. Its reflectance in each camera band is computed with the same physics as the Spectral signatures laboratory: PROSPECT-D gives the leaf reflectance and transmittance, a Kubelka–Munk canopy model the reflectance of the plants, and the fraction of soil visible between the rows follows Beer's law (Eq. D3). The camera adds radiometric noise, which averages out over the many pixels in each cell (Eq. D4). NDVI and NDRE are then computed cell by cell (Eq. D5).

3 · From map to zones

A 1 m map contains far more detail than a fertiliser spreader with a 24 m working width can use. The map is therefore cleaned (wheel tracks masked), smoothed with a moving window and clustered into k zones with k-means (Eq. D6; Lesson 11.1, Eq. 11.1.10), and small fragments are removed with a majority filter. The share of the map's variance that the zones explain tells you how well k zones summarise the field; more zones always explain more, but each must be large enough to manage and to verify (Fridgen et al., 2004).

4 · From zones to nitrogen rates

Two families of rules are used in practice. Index-proportional rules start from the rate the farmer planned and move it up or down with the zone's index (Eq. D7). With β > 0 the rule compensates: thin crop gets more nitrogen, dense crop less, which suits nitrogen-limited variation. With β < 0 it reinforces: nitrogen follows yield potential, which suits variation caused by soil or water. Both keep the field total fixed, so they redistribute rather than save nitrogen. Sufficiency-index rules compare every zone with a well-fertilised reference — an N-rich strip, or a high percentile of the field as a “virtual reference” (Holland & Schepers, 2013) — and convert the shortfall into a rate through the assumed shape of the nitrogen-response curve (Eq. D8; Holland & Schepers, 2010). Where the index is lower than even an unfertilised crop would be, the low value cannot be caused by nitrogen, and the rate is backed off.

Sweden: satellites and tractor sensors

Swedish farmers can build nitrogen prescriptions for free from Sentinel-2 images in the CropSAT service (Söderström et al., 2017), and tractor-mounted reflectance sensors such as the Yara N-Sensor adjust the rate on the go. Choose Sentinel-2 as the sensor to see what a 10 m satellite pixel makes of the same field.

5 · Judging a prescription

In a real field you never know the true nitrogen need of every square metre. Here you do: each cell has a hidden yield potential and nitrogen need, and a quadratic-plateau response (Eq. D9) turns any rate map into yield, nitrogen cost and nitrogen surplus. The readouts compare your prescription with a uniform application and with the ideal (perfect-information) map. Expect modest gains — typically a few euros per hectare — which is consistent with the long-standing finding that the value of variable-rate nitrogen depends heavily on how much of the variation is nitrogen-responsive (Bullock et al., 2002; Lowenberg-DeBoer & Erickson, 2019). The environmental benefit, less surplus nitrogen where the crop cannot use it, is often the stronger argument.

Common misconception

“A finer drone map gives a better prescription.” For nitrogen zoning, a 5 cm and a 50 cm map give almost the same zones after smoothing to the spreader's working width. Fine GSD pays for other jobs — counting plants, finding weed patches, detecting disease foci — not for zone maps. Try 30 m and 120 m and compare the zones.

The equations behind the simulation

Ground sampling distance and image footprint(D1)
\[ \text{GSD}=\frac{H\,p}{f},\qquad W=N_x\,\text{GSD},\qquad L=N_y\,\text{GSD} \]

the ground distance covered by one pixel is the flight height times the pixel pitch divided by the focal length; the footprint of an image is the number of pixels across (W, across the flight direction) or along (L) times the GSD.

Follow the derivation
  1. Treat the camera as a pinhole at height H with the detector a distance f behind it (Lesson 11.2, Eq. 11.2.7).
  2. A ray from the edge of one ground pixel through the pinhole lands on the edge of one detector pixel. The triangles on either side of the pinhole are similar, so GSD/H = p/f.
  3. Worked number: \(80\times3.75\times10^{-6}/(5.4\times10^{-3})=0.0556\) m = 5.6 cm; W = 1280 × 0.0556 = 71.1 m, L = 960 × 0.0556 = 53.3 m. The MicaSense specification, 8 cm at 120 m, is the same equation at H = 120 m (8.3 cm).
  4. DJI states the Mavic 3M multispectral GSD as H/21.7 cm per pixel, i.e. \(p/f=1/2170\) — the same equation with the ratio given directly.
Assumptions and validity

Nadir images over flat ground; lens distortion, terrain relief and aircraft tilt are ignored. The effective resolution of the final orthomosaic is somewhat coarser than the GSD because of motion blur, optics and resampling.

Line spacing, photo interval, number of lines and flight time(D2)
\[ S=W(1-o_s),\quad d=L(1-o_f),\quad \Delta t=\frac{d}{v}\ge\Delta t_{\min},\quad n=\left\lceil\frac{D}{S}\right\rceil+1 \] \[ T\approx\frac{n\,(W_F+L)}{v}+\frac{(n-1)\,\pi S/2}{v_{\text{turn}}}+\frac{2H}{v_z} \]

flight lines are spaced one footprint width reduced by the side overlap; the camera fires every footprint length reduced by the forward overlap, which at ground speed v means every Δt seconds; enough lines are needed to cover the field width D; the flight time adds the straight lines, the semicircular turns and the climb and descent.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Two neighbouring strips of width W overlap by \(o_sW\) when their centre lines are \(W(1-o_s)\) apart.
  2. The lines are centred on the field; n lines with spacing S span \((n-1)S\) between the outermost centre lines, and the outermost strips reach W/2 beyond them. Requiring \((n-1)S\ge D\) guarantees coverage with margin: \(n=\lceil D/S\rceil+1\).
  3. Along a line the same reasoning gives \(d=L(1-o_f)\). If \(d/v\) is shorter than the camera's minimum interval, the achieved overlap is only \(1-v\,\Delta t_{\min}/L\) — slow down or fly higher.
  4. Worked number (80 m, 75 %/75 %, 8 m s⁻¹): S = 0.25 × 71.1 = 17.8 m, n = ⌈200/17.8⌉ + 1 = 13; d = 0.25 × 53.3 = 13.3 m, Δt = 1.67 s; each line is 300 + 53 = 353 m, so 13 × 353/8 = 574 s of imaging, 12 turns of π × 8.9 m at 4 m s⁻¹ = 84 s, plus climb, transit and landing: ≈ 12.7 min, 351 images.
Assumptions and validity

Constant speed and height, no wind, instantaneous acceleration, semicircular turns outside the field. Real flight-planning software adds lead-in distances, takes wind into account and may fly lines perpendicular to the wind. Usable battery time (22 min for a RedEdge-carrying quadcopter, 35 min for the Mavic 3M, whose maximum flight time without wind is 43 min according to DJI) is an assumption with a reserve for landing.

Reflectance of a 1 m cell: plants, soil and wheel tracks(D3)
\[ f_c=1-e^{-0.5\,L},\qquad \rho_{\text{crop}}=f_c\,\rho_{\text{KM}}\!\left(r,t,\tfrac{L}{f_c},\rho_s\right)+(1-f_c)\,\rho_s \] \[ \rho_{\text{cell}}=(1-f_{\text{tr}})\,\rho_{\text{crop}}+f_{\text{tr}}\,0.92\,\rho_s \]

the fraction of ground covered by plants seen from above follows Beer's law with the leaf area index; the vegetated part reflects like a Kubelka–Munk canopy whose local leaf area index is the cell's LAI divided by the cover, the rest is bare soil; wheel tracks replace part of the cell with slightly darker compacted soil.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(L\)leaf area index of wheat plus thistles in the cellm² m⁻²
\(f_c\)vertical (nadir) canopy cover; 0.5 = extinction coefficient for random leaf angles (Lesson 11.2, Eq. 11.2.10)–
\(r, t\)band-averaged leaf reflectance and transmittance from PROSPECT-D at the cell's chlorophyll content (LAI-weighted mix of wheat and thistle leaves)–
\(\rho_{\text{KM}}\)Kubelka–Munk canopy reflectance (Spectral signatures lab, Eq. S3)–

\(f_{\text{tr}}\)fraction of the cell occupied by wheel tracks (0.5 m wide)–
Why combine a gap fraction with a turbid-medium canopy?
  1. Drilled cereals grow in rows 12–15 cm apart. Seen from above, part of the soil between the rows is visible until the canopy closes. A turbid-medium canopy spread evenly over the whole cell would hide the soil too early and make NDVI saturate at unrealistically low LAI.
  2. Beer's law gives the probability that a vertical line of sight passes the canopy without hitting a leaf: \(P_0=e^{-0.5L}\); the covered fraction is \(1-P_0\).
  3. The leaves are concentrated in the covered fraction, where their local LAI is \(L/f_c\); that part reflects like a Kubelka–Munk canopy over soil.
  4. Result: at L = 2.3 the cover is 0.68 and NDVI ≈ 0.68 in the lab model with MicaSense bands (the simplified calculator below, with fixed leaf and soil reflectances, gives ≈ 0.70), close to typical drone values for wheat at stem elongation; at L = 5 the cover is 0.92 and NDVI ≈ 0.88.
Assumptions and validity

Shadows, leaf angle effects on the view and the sun–view geometry (bidirectional effects) are ignored, as is multiple scattering between the vegetated and bare fractions. Model parameters such as how strongly waterlogging reduces LAI are illustrative assumptions chosen to produce realistic index ranges; they are not measured values for a particular field.

all reflectances and fractions dimensionless; \(0.5L\): m² m⁻² × 1 ✓
Radiometric noise and pixel averaging(D4)
\[ \sigma_{\text{px}}=0.003+0.02\,\rho,\qquad \sigma_{\text{cell}}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{px}}}{\sqrt{n_{\text{px}}}}=\sigma_{\text{px}}\,\frac{\text{GSD}}{1\ \text{m}} \]

each pixel's reflectance carries a small absolute error plus two per cent of the signal; averaging the n pixels that fall into a 1 m cell reduces the error by the square root of n, which for square pixels is the ratio of the GSD to the cell size.

Assumptions and validity

Errors are independent between pixels, so they average out; systematic errors — a wrongly calibrated reflectance panel, a cloud passing during the flight, the sun angle changing between the first and last line — do not average out and are not simulated. For Sentinel-2 the model averages the true reflectance over each 10 m (20 m for red-edge bands) pixel and adds σpx once; atmospheric correction errors are ignored.

\[ \text{NDVI}=\frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}}},\qquad \text{NDRE}=\frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{RE}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{RE}}} \]

both indices are normalised differences; NDVI uses the red band where chlorophyll absorbs strongly, NDRE the red-edge band where it absorbs less, so NDRE keeps distinguishing chlorophyll (nitrogen) levels after NDVI has saturated.

Smoothing and k-means zoning(D6)
\[ \bar V_i=\frac{1}{|N_i|}\sum_{j\in N_i}V_j,\qquad \min_{C_1..C_k}\sum_{z=1}^{k}\sum_{i\in C_z}\left(\bar V_i-\mu_z\right)^2,\qquad R^2=1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} \]

each cell's index is replaced by the mean of its neighbourhood (tracks excluded); the cells are then divided into k groups so that the summed squared distance between each cell's smoothed index and its group mean is as small as possible; R² is the share of the smoothed map's variance explained by the zones.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(N_i\)the (2r + 1) × (2r + 1) cells around cell i (5, 9 or 15 m windows)–
\(C_z, \mu_z\)cells of zone z and their mean smoothed index–
SSE, SSTwithin-zone and total sum of squares–
Algorithm (Lloyd, 1982)
  1. Start the k centres at the (z − ½)/k quantiles of the smoothed index.
  2. Assign every cell to its nearest centre; recompute each centre as the mean of its cells.
  3. Repeat until no cell changes zone; J cannot increase, so the algorithm converges.
  4. Sort zones from low to high index; replace each label by the most common label in its 5 × 5 neighbourhood (majority filter) so that no zone is too fragmented for a spreader.
sums of squared index values — dimensionless ✓
Linear, mass-conserving nitrogen rule(D7)
\[ N_z=\bar N\left[1-\beta\,\frac{V_z-\bar V}{\bar V}\right],\qquad \bar V=\sum_z a_z V_z,\qquad \sum_z a_z N_z=\bar N \]

each zone receives the planned mean rate, changed by β times the zone's relative index deviation from the area-weighted field mean; because the deviations sum to zero over the field, the field total is unchanged.

Implementation details

This is Eq. 11.1.11 of Lesson 11.1, where the conservation of the total is proved. Rates are clipped to 0–150 kg N ha⁻¹ and, if clipping occurred, the unclipped zones are rescaled so that the total is conserved; finally rates are rounded to 5 kg N ha⁻¹, a typical step of a spreader controller.

Sufficiency-index rule with back-off(D8)
\[ \text{SI}_z=\frac{V_z}{V_{\text{ref}}},\qquad N_z=N_{\text{opt}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\text{SI}_z}{\Delta\text{SI}}}\quad\text{for}\ 1-\Delta\text{SI}\le \text{SI}_z\le1 \] \[ N_z\to0\ \text{for}\ \text{SI}_z<1-\Delta\text{SI}\quad\text{(back-off)} \]

the sufficiency index is the zone's index divided by that of a non-limited reference; the rate is the optimum rate times the square root of the index shortfall relative to the shortfall of an unfertilised crop; where the index is lower still, nitrogen is not what limits the crop and the rate is reduced.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(V_{\text{ref}}\)index of the N-rich strip (or 95th percentile of the field)–from the map
\(N_{\text{opt}}\)economic optimum rate for a crop with no N supply from earlier doseskg N ha⁻¹120
\(\Delta\text{SI}\)1 − SI of an unfertilised crop–0.30 (NDVI), 0.45 (NDRE)
Follow the derivation
  1. Assume, as Holland & Schepers (2010) do, that the index of the crop responds to the nitrogen it has received like a quadratic that levels off at the optimum: \(\text{SI}(N)=1-\Delta\text{SI}\,(1-N/N_{\text{opt}})^2\) for \(0\le N\le N_{\text{opt}}\).
  2. At N = 0 this gives SI = 1 − ΔSI (the unfertilised crop); at \(N=N_{\text{opt}}\) it gives SI = 1 (the reference) with zero slope.
  3. A zone observed at SI has behaved as if it had received \(N_{\text{eff}}=N_{\text{opt}}\left(1-\sqrt{(1-\text{SI})/\Delta\text{SI}}\right)\).
  4. The nitrogen still missing is \(N_{\text{opt}}-N_{\text{eff}}=N_{\text{opt}}\sqrt{(1-\text{SI})/\Delta\text{SI}}\) ✓. Holland & Schepers subtract earlier fertiliser and other credits from \(N_{\text{opt}}\); here \(N_{\text{opt}}\) is already the remaining optimum.
  5. Back-off: an index below 1 − ΔSI is lower than an unfertilised crop would show, so something else (water, compaction, damage) limits the crop. The laboratory reduces the rate linearly to zero over a further 0.08 of SI — a simplified version of the back-off option described by Holland & Schepers.
  6. Worked number: V = 0.62, Vref = 0.70, SI = 0.886; ΔSI = 0.30, Nopt = 120: \(N=120\sqrt{0.114/0.30}=74\) kg N ha⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

The quadratic shape is an assumption about how the index (not the yield) responds to nitrogen; ΔSI and Nopt must be calibrated for the crop, stage, sensor and region, and the reference must be representative. The strip must not be affected by the same non-nitrogen problems as the zones it is compared with.

kg N ha⁻¹ × \(\sqrt{\text{dimensionless}}\) = kg N ha⁻¹ ✓
Hidden yield response, net return and nitrogen surplus(D9)
\[ Y=Y_p\left[1-c\left(1-\frac{\min(N,N^\ast)}{N^\ast}\right)^{2}\right] \] \[ \Pi=p_g\,Y-p_N\,N,\qquad N_{\text{surplus}}=\max(0,\,N-N^\ast) \]

grain yield rises along a quadratic to a plateau at the cell's nitrogen need; the net return is the grain value minus the nitrogen cost; nitrogen applied above the need is a surplus at risk of leaching.

\(Y_p\)yield potential of the cell (reduced by waterlogging, compaction, weeds)t ha⁻¹3–9
\(N^\ast\)supplementary nitrogen need of the cell (hidden)kg N ha⁻¹0 (strip) – 130

Assumptions and validity

Quadratic-plateau functions are a standard description of cereal yield response to nitrogen (Cerrato & Blackmer, 1990). Here the plateau is placed exactly at the hidden need, so the economic optimum equals the need; real response curves are estimated from trials with considerable uncertainty. Protein, lodging, leaching and nitrous-oxide emissions are not modelled; the surplus is an indicator of risk only. Prices are illustrative defaults — adjust them to current values.

Model assumptions

  • The field is a synthetic 300 m × 200 m spring-wheat field on a 1 m grid. The spatial patterns (patches, hollows, headland, thistle patches) are random but realistic in size; their effects on LAI, chlorophyll, height and yield potential are illustrative assumptions chosen to give realistic index ranges, not measurements from a particular field.
  • Reflectance is computed with PROSPECT-D (Féret et al., 2017) for the leaves, a Kubelka–Munk canopy (Allen & Richardson, 1968) and Beer's-law cover (Eq. D3) over measured dry and wet reference soils. Bidirectional effects, shadows and multiple scattering between plants and soil are ignored.
  • The camera records calibrated surface reflectance with random noise (Eq. D4). Systematic errors (panel calibration, changing sunlight, vignetting) and mosaicking artefacts are not simulated; every cell is mapped by the first image that covers it.
  • The flight is flown at constant height and speed without wind; the minimum capture interval (1 s for the RedEdge-MX, 2 s for the Mavic 3M) and usable battery times are assumptions. The Sentinel-2 overpass is an illustrative animation; cloud cover and revisit time are not simulated.
  • Zones are k-means clusters of the smoothed index; spatial contiguity is encouraged only by smoothing and a majority filter.
  • Economics use a quadratic-plateau response with the plateau at the hidden need (Eq. D9); prices are adjustable illustrative values; application costs, the cost of the drone survey and quality premiums are not included.
  • The 3D scene is painted with the true-colour reflectance of the same model, seen at an oblique angle and white-balanced for display; buildings, trees and neighbouring crops are illustrative.
