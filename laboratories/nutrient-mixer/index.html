Nutrient-solution mixer

Every hydroponic grower eventually faces the same puzzle: a crop needs about a dozen ions in the right amounts, but every fertiliser salt delivers two of them at once, the tap water already contains some of them and too much of others, and a concentrated stock solution will turn to stone if calcium meets sulfate. This laboratory is the tool professionals use to solve the puzzle. Enter your water analysis, choose a crop recipe and the fertilisers on your shelf, and a least-squares solver finds how many grams to weigh into the A and B stock tanks — with the ion balance, two independent EC estimates, the acid dose for your water, precipitation and solubility checks, and a printable mixing sheet for your project.

Target versus achieved

Ion concentrations of the working solution (mmol L⁻¹): recipe target, what the chosen salts and water actually give, and the part that comes from the water alone. Na⁺, Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻ come only from the water.

Charge balance

Cations and anions in milliequivalents per litre. Electroneutrality demands equal columns; because every salt is neutral, a recipe built from salts balances automatically — any gap comes from the water analysis.

Where each nutrient comes from

Element concentrations (mg L⁻¹) split by source: the water and each fertiliser. With hard water, a large share of the calcium, magnesium and sulfur is already there before you weigh anything.

What to look at first

  1. Reproduce a professional recipe. Choose Lettuce · Dutch standard and Rain / reverse-osmosis water, concentration factor 100× and a stock volume of 1000 L. The solver asks for about 97 kg of calcium nitrate, 61 kg of potassium nitrate, 20 kg of monopotassium phosphate, 25 kg of Epsom salt and 17 kg of potassium sulfate — the same amounts, within a few per cent, as the published A+B recipe for lettuce in water culture (van der Lugt, 2016). The small differences come from the ammonium source.
  2. Now use real tap water. Switch to the hard Swedish groundwater example. Calcium nitrate falls sharply because the water already supplies 2.7 mmol L⁻¹ of calcium; the acid dose rises to neutralise 5.7 mmol L⁻¹ of bicarbonate; sodium and chloride appear in the charge balance and push the EC above the recipe value. Compare the three charts before and after.
  3. Break the rule on purpose. In the weighing list, press → A next to magnesium sulfate. The A tank turns cloudy and a white layer of gypsum settles at the bottom; the readout Stock tanks: compatibility turns red. At 100× concentration the ion product of Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ exceeds the solubility product of gypsum more than a thousand-fold. Move it back.
  4. Become a private buyer. Tick Private buyer in the EU. Nitric acid and ammonium nitrate disappear from your shelf — they are restricted explosives precursors. With hard water, phosphoric acid now has to neutralise all the bicarbonate, and the phosphorus overshoots its target: a real constraint for project groups working at home.
  5. Watch pH drift before it happens. Compare the two pH readouts: just after mixing, CO₂ from the neutralised bicarbonate is still dissolved; in an aerated tank it escapes and the pH rises. Switch Acid calculated for to target pH, aerated and see how much more acid a DWC or NFT system needs.
  6. Take it to the bench. Set your own system volume and press Mixing sheet for a printable weighing list with check boxes, the expected EC and pH, and the safety procedure.
Key idea

A nutrient recipe is a small system of linear equations: each salt contributes fixed amounts of two (or three) ions, the water contributes its own ions, and the sum must match the target. Because salts come in fixed pairs, not every target can be met exactly — the solver finds the closest non-negative combination and tells you where it falls short.

From a list of ions to a list of salts

A recipe is written in millimoles of ions per litre — the language of plant uptake (Lesson 5.2). A fertiliser bag is sold in kilograms of salt. Translating between the two is bookkeeping with a twist: each salt brings a pair of ions in a fixed ratio. Potassium nitrate gives equal amounts of K⁺ and NO₃⁻; commercial calcium nitrate gives 2.2 NO₃⁻ and 0.2 NH₄⁺ with every Ca²⁺; potassium sulfate gives two K⁺ per SO₄²⁻. The grower's problem is to choose amounts of 8–11 salts so that about 8 ion concentrations match their targets, with the obvious constraint that you cannot weigh a negative mass.

Water first

Tap water is not an empty solvent. Water from Lake Mälaren delivered by Norrvatten contained on average 34 mg L⁻¹ Ca, 4.9 mg L⁻¹ Mg, 13 mg L⁻¹ Na, 50 mg L⁻¹ SO₄²⁻ and 70 mg L⁻¹ HCO₃⁻ in 2025 (Norrvatten, 2025) — soft to medium-hard. Groundwater from the Uppsala esker at Vänge carried 110 mg L⁻¹ Ca, 46 mg L⁻¹ Na and 349 mg L⁻¹ HCO₃⁻ (Uppsala Vatten, 2023). The solver subtracts what is already there; useful ions (Ca, Mg, SO₄) save fertiliser, while sodium and chloride are ballast that raise the EC without feeding the crop. A useful classification for recirculating systems: below 1.5 mmol L⁻¹ Na or Cl the water suits all crops; 1.5–2.5 mmol L⁻¹ rules out recirculation for sensitive crops; above that, salt-sensitive crops suffer (van der Lugt, 2016).

Never use softened water

Household water softeners exchange calcium and magnesium for sodium. At Vänge the softened tap water held 190 mg L⁻¹ of sodium — 8.3 mmol L⁻¹, five times the limit for recirculation — while the calcium the crop needs had been removed (Uppsala Vatten, 2023). Take hydroponic water from before the softener, or collect rain water.

Bicarbonate, acid and the target pH

Bicarbonate is a base. Unless it is neutralised, it pushes the pH of the nutrient solution towards 7–8, where iron, manganese and phosphate become unavailable (pH laboratory). The mixer offers three ways to calculate the acid: the grower's rule of leaving about 0.5 mmol L⁻¹ of bicarbonate as a buffer (van der Lugt, 2016); an equilibrium calculation to the target pH in a closed tank, where the CO₂ formed stays dissolved; and the same calculation for an aerated tank, where CO₂ escapes to the air and almost all bicarbonate must go. Phosphoric acid is used first because it doubles as the phosphorus source; nitric acid supplies the rest and adds nitrate, which the solver counts.

Two ways to predict EC

Conductivity is carried by ions, so it can be estimated from the composition. The grower's rule — EC in dS m⁻¹ ≈ sum of cations in meq L⁻¹ divided by 10 (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009) — is quick and surprisingly good for nutrient solutions. A more physical estimate sums the contribution of each ion, charge × equivalent conductance × concentration, which would be exact at infinite dilution (Kohlrausch's law), and then corrects for the drag of the ionic atmosphere at real concentrations with the square of a Davies activity coefficient. This is the procedure of Standard Methods 2510 for checking water analyses, where calculated and measured conductivities of natural waters agreed within 2 % (APHA, 1992). For the Cornell lettuce solution the two estimates give 1.20 and 1.26 dS m⁻¹ against a target of 1.2; the uncorrected Kohlrausch sum would give 1.6.

Why A and B tanks — and how concentrated?

At working strength a nutrient solution is undersaturated with respect to gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) and calcium phosphates. Concentrate it 100 times and the ion products rise 10 000-fold: calcium must be kept away from sulfate and phosphate. Tank A therefore holds calcium nitrate and the iron chelate; tank B holds phosphates, sulfates, micronutrient salts and the acid; potassium nitrate is split between the two so that both tanks hold similar masses (van der Lugt, 2016). A second limit is solubility: potassium sulfate dissolves only to about 111 g L⁻¹ at 20 °C, and potassium nitrate falls from 316 g L⁻¹ at 20 °C to 133 g L⁻¹ at 0 °C (Haynes, 2014) — a stock prepared at 100× in a warm lab can crystallise in a cold shed. The mixer compares the concentration of every salt in each tank with its solubility at your storage temperature.

Iron and micronutrients

Iron is supplied as a chelate whose pH stability matters: Fe-EDTA holds iron up to about pH 6–6.5, Fe-DTPA to about 7–7.5 and Fe-EDDHA to about 9 (van der Lugt, 2016; Hershkowitz et al., 2025). Manganese, zinc and copper can be given as sulfates or as EDTA chelates; the sulfates are cheaper, but free metal ions can displace iron from its chelate, so recirculating systems often use chelated forms (van der Lugt, 2016). Micronutrient masses are small — grams per thousand litres — so weigh them on a 0.01 g balance or make a separate micronutrient pre-stock.

Regulation · what a private buyer can get (as of 2026)

Since 1 February 2021, Regulation (EU) 2019/1148 on explosives precursors forbids making nitric acid above 3 % w/w and ammonium nitrate (16 % N or more from ammonium nitrate) available to members of the general public; professional users such as growers and universities can still buy them. Calcium nitrate fertiliser, whose small ammonium-nitrate content gives far less than 16 % N, is not affected. A student mixing at home therefore relies on phosphoric acid (or ready-made "pH down") and on calcium nitrate and monoammonium phosphate for ammonium. Tick Private buyer to see what this means for your water.

The equations behind the mixer

Ions supplied by a salt mixture and grams per stock tank(N1)
\[ c_i = c_{i,\text{w}} + \sum_j \nu_{ij}\,x_j, \qquad m_j = x_j\,M_j\,\frac{F\,V_s}{1000}, \qquad V_{\text{acid},j} = \frac{x_j\,F\,V_s}{C_{\text{acid},j}} \]

the concentration of ion \(i\) in the working solution equals what the water brings plus, for every fertiliser \(j\), the number of ions per unit of that fertiliser times the amount dissolved. The mass to weigh into a stock tank is the amount per litre of working solution times the molar mass of the unit times the number of litres of working solution the tank will serve, which is the concentration factor times the tank volume; acids are measured by volume instead.

Follow the derivation
  1. Mass balance for ion \(i\) in one litre of working solution: what is present = what the water brought + what each salt released. Salts dissolve completely at these concentrations.
  2. One litre of stock tank, diluted \(F\) times, makes \(F\) litres of working solution; a tank of \(V_s\) litres serves \(F V_s\) litres. It must therefore contain \(x_j F V_s\) mmol of unit \(j\), i.e. \(x_j F V_s M_j/1000\) grams.
  3. Example (Dutch lettuce recipe, rain water): calcium nitrate \(x = 4.50\) mmol L⁻¹ → \(4.50 \times 216.1 \times 100 \times 1000 / 1000 = 97\,200\) g = 97 kg per 1000 L tank at 100×.
Assumptions and validity

Commercial salts are assumed to match their formulae; check the label (calcium nitrate is hygroscopic; iron chelates vary from 3 to 13 % Fe). The volume occupied by dissolved salt is ignored — dissolve in three-quarters of the water, then fill to the mark.

mmol L⁻¹ × g mol⁻¹ × L ÷ 1000 = mmol × mg mmol⁻¹ ÷ 1000 = g ✓; mmol L⁻¹ × L ÷ mol L⁻¹ = mL ✓
Weighted non-negative least squares(N2)
\[ \min_{\mathbf{x}\,\ge\,0}\ \sum_i \left(\frac{w_i}{\max(1,\,b_i)}\Big(\sum_j A_{ij}x_j - b_i\Big)\right)^{2} + \varepsilon\sum_j \lambda_j x_j^2, \qquad b_i = c_i^{\text{target}} - c_{i,\text{w}} \]

choose non-negative amounts that minimise the weighted squared differences between supplied and needed ions — relative differences for concentrations above 1 mmol L⁻¹, absolute ones below — plus a tiny penalty on less conventional salts that decides between chemically equivalent mixtures.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(A_{ij}\)matrix of \(\nu_{ij}\) for the salts you have enabled; the last row holds the H⁺ each acid suppliesmol mol⁻¹Figure 1
\(b_i\)ions still needed after the water (never negative); for H⁺: the acid requirement (Eq. N3)mmol L⁻¹–
\(w_i\)importance weights–1 for K, Ca, Mg, P; 0.8 NO₃, SO₄; 0.35 NH₄; 0.3 Cl; 3 for H⁺
\(\lambda_j,\ \varepsilon\)preference penalty; its scale–1–2 for HNO₃, KH₂PO₄, Mg(NO₃)₂, K₂SO₄, NH₄H₂PO₄, CaCl₂; 0 otherwise; ε = 10⁻³
How the solver works (Lawson & Hanson)
  1. Start with all amounts zero ("passive" set). Compute the gradient \(\mathbf{w} = A^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x})\): a positive component means that adding that salt would reduce the error.
  2. Move the salt with the largest positive gradient into the "active" set and solve the ordinary least-squares problem for the active salts only.
  3. If any active amount comes out negative, step back along the line towards the previous feasible point until the first amount hits zero, and return that salt to the passive set; repeat.
  4. Stop when no passive salt has a positive gradient: the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions for a minimum under \(\mathbf{x}\ge 0\) are satisfied (Lawson & Hanson, 1974).
  5. Why the penalty? Chemistry creates exact ties: MgSO₄ + 2 KNO₃ supply exactly the same ions as Mg(NO₃)₂ + K₂SO₄, and HNO₃ + KH₂PO₄ the same as H₃PO₄ + KNO₃. A tiny quadratic penalty on the "secondary" salt of each pair selects the conventional allocation used by advisers — magnesium from Epsom salt first, phosphorus from phosphoric acid first — without measurably changing the fit.
Assumptions and validity

The weights express nutritional priorities (a 10 % error in calcium matters more than in ammonium) and are a design choice, not chemistry. If a target is impossible with the enabled salts — for example, more sulfate in the water than in the recipe — the solver returns the closest compromise and the readout Largest deviation reports it.

Each residual is in mmol L⁻¹ divided by mmol L⁻¹ (or by 1 mmol L⁻¹): the objective is dimensionless, so ions of very different size can be compared ✓
Acid requirement for a target pH(N3)
\[ n_{\mathrm{H^+}} = A_{\text{w}} - A(\text{pH}^{*}), \qquad A(\text{pH}) = C_T\,(\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2) + P_T\,(\alpha_{\mathrm{HPO_4}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{H_3PO_4}}) + [\mathrm{OH^-}] - [\mathrm{H^+}] \]

the acid needed, in millimoles of H⁺ per litre, is the alkalinity of the water minus the alkalinity that the solution may keep at the target pH. That alkalinity is the carbonate that is still present as bicarbonate or carbonate, plus the part of the phosphate that has lost a second proton, plus hydroxide minus hydrogen ions.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(A_{\text{w}}\)alkalinity of the source water (≈ its HCO₃⁻)mmol L⁻¹
\(C_T\)total inorganic carbon; closed tank: that of the water, \(C_T = A_{\text{w}}/(\alpha_1+2\alpha_2)\) at its own pH; aerated tank: \(C_T = [\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{air}}/\alpha_0(\text{pH}^*)\)mmol L⁻¹
\(\alpha_0,\ \alpha_1,\ \alpha_2\)fractions of \(C_T\) as CO₂, HCO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻ (pK 6.35, 10.33)–
\(P_T\)phosphate from the recipe (reference species H₂PO₄⁻; pK 2.15, 7.20, 12.35)mmol L⁻¹
\([\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{air}}\)CO₂ in equilibrium with air, \(K_H\,p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) = 0.0334 mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹ × 425 ppm0.014 mmol L⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Alkalinity is the capacity of a solution to neutralise acid, relative to a reference: CO₂ for carbon, H₂PO₄⁻ for phosphate (KH₂PO₄ and H₃PO₄ at the target pH both end up mostly as H₂PO₄⁻).
  2. A strong acid lowers alkalinity one-for-one; the salts, being neutral at the reference level, do not change it. So the acid needed is \(A_{\text{w}} - A(\text{pH}^*)\).
  3. Closed tank: no CO₂ escapes, \(C_T\) is conserved; at pH 5.8 about a fifth of it remains as HCO₃⁻, which is why "leave ≈ 0.5 mmol L⁻¹" works for moderately hard water.
  4. Aerated tank: CO₂ equilibrates with air at only 0.014 mmol L⁻¹, so at pH 5.8 almost no bicarbonate may remain — nearly all alkalinity must be neutralised.
  5. Example, Mälaren water (1.15 mmol L⁻¹ HCO₃⁻, pH 8.2) with 1 mmol L⁻¹ phosphate: 0.86 mmol L⁻¹ closed, 1.11 aerated, 0.65 by the 0.5 mmol L⁻¹ rule.
Assumptions and validity

25 °C constants without activity corrections; other bases in the water (silicate, organic matter) are ignored. A titration of your own water with dilute acid is the definitive test (see the pH laboratory).

All terms are mmol L⁻¹ of charge equivalents; α's are dimensionless ✓
Ion balance(N4)
\[ \Sigma_+ = \sum_{\text{cations}} z_i c_i, \qquad \Sigma_- = \sum_{\text{anions}} |z_i|\,c_i, \qquad \text{IBE} = 100\,\frac{\Sigma_+ - \Sigma_-}{\Sigma_+ + \Sigma_-}\ \% \]

sum the charge concentrations of cations and anions; the ion-balance error is their difference divided by their sum, in per cent.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(z_i,\ c_i\)charge number; concentration (NH₄⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺ / NO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, SO₄²⁻, Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻)–; mmol L⁻¹

Why a recipe balances automatically

Every salt and acid is electrically neutral, so it adds equal cation and anion charge (H⁺ from acids is consumed by bicarbonate). A computed recipe can only be unbalanced if the water analysis is — for instance because potassium or nitrate were not reported. Laboratories accept an IBE within about ±5 % (Lesson 5.2); van der Lugt (2016) tolerates up to 10 % in routine analyses.

mmol L⁻¹ × charge = meq L⁻¹ on both sides; IBE is a ratio ✓
Two estimates of electrical conductivity(N5)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{EC}_{\text{rule}} \approx \frac{\Sigma_+}{10}, \qquad \kappa^{\circ} = \sum_i |z_i|\,\lambda_i^{\circ}\,c_i, \qquad \kappa = \kappa^{\circ}\,y^2 \\[6pt] \log_{10} y = -0.5\Big(\frac{\sqrt I}{1+\sqrt I} - 0.3\,I\Big), \qquad I = \tfrac12\sum_i z_i^2 c_i \end{gathered} \]

the quick estimate divides the cation sum in meq per litre by ten. The physical estimate multiplies each ion's charge, equivalent conductance and concentration and adds them up, then multiplies by the square of the Davies activity coefficient of a monovalent ion at the solution's ionic strength.

Follow the derivation
  1. Each ion carries current in proportion to its charge, concentration and mobility; at infinite dilution the contributions add (Kohlrausch). With \(c\) in mmol L⁻¹ and \(\lambda\) in S cm² eq⁻¹, \(\sum |z|\lambda c\) is in µS cm⁻¹ (Lesson 5.2, Eq. 5.2.4).
  2. At real concentrations each ion drags an atmosphere of opposite charge that slows it. Standard Methods 2510 approximates the whole effect by multiplying \(\kappa^{\circ}\) by \(y^2\), with \(y\) from the Davies equation; for 39 natural waters the result matched measurements within 2 % (APHA, 1992).
  3. The rule \(\Sigma_+/10\) is its empirical shortcut for nutrient solutions (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009); van der Lugt (2016) writes it as \((\Sigma_+ + \Sigma_-)/20\), which is the same thing for a balanced solution.
  4. Example (Cornell lettuce solution): \(\kappa^{\circ}\) = 1.61 dS m⁻¹, \(I\) = 0.016 mol L⁻¹, \(y\) = 0.88, \(\kappa\) = 1.25 dS m⁻¹; rule 1.19; target 1.2.

Stocks versus working solution

In the working solution (I ≈ 0.02–0.04 mol L⁻¹) the Davies equation is reliable and gypsum stays undersaturated (SI ≈ −1.5 to −0.6 for the recipes here). In a stock tank at 100× the ionic strength exceeds 1 mol L⁻¹, where no simple activity model is valid; the mixer therefore reports the ideal ion product and flags any tank that contains calcium together with sulfate or phosphate. Putting Epsom salt into tank A with the Dutch lettuce recipe gives [Ca²⁺][SO₄²⁻] = 0.45 × 0.10 = 10⁻¹·³⁵ (mol L⁻¹)², over three orders of magnitude above the solubility product.

Investigation tasks

Save each result with Save investigation; the link stores all your settings.

  1. Your own water

    Find the latest drinking-water analysis of your municipality (most Swedish utilities publish annual means of Ca, Mg, Na, Cl, SO₄ and alkalinity). Enter it, check its ion balance with Eq. N4, and classify it for recirculation. How many grams of calcium nitrate and how many millilitres of acid does it save or cost per 100 L compared with rain water, for the recipe you will use in your project?

  2. Reproduce and criticise a published recipe

    Choose the Dutch tomato recipe with rain water, 100× and 1000 L tanks, and enable calcium chloride. Compare the solver's masses with the published tomato A+B recipe (van der Lugt, 2016: 106 kg calcium nitrate, 43 kg potassium nitrate, 35 kg potassium sulfate, 17 kg monopotassium phosphate, 59 kg magnesium sulfate, 6 kg calcium chloride, 3 kg MAP). Explain every difference larger than 5 %.

  3. The acid budget of a DWC system

    With the hard-water example and the Cornell recipe, compare the acid dose for the three bases (0.5 mM rule, closed, aerated). Then use the pH laboratory's titration simulator to check the closed and aerated numbers. Which basis would you use for an NFT channel, a DWC tank and a drip-to-waste rock-wool system, and why?

  4. How concentrated can the stocks be?

    For the tomato recipe, raise the concentration factor to 400× and the storage temperature down to 2 °C. Which salt limits first, in which tank? Propose two changes to the recipe or the tank layout that let you keep 200× through a Swedish winter in an unheated shed.

  5. A legal home recipe

    Tick Private buyer and use your own water. Can you meet the lettuce target with phosphoric acid as the only acid? If the phosphorus overshoots, quantify by how much, and design a workable alternative (e.g., blending tap water with rain water — which blend ratio keeps P within 20 % of target?).

  6. EC: rule versus physics

    For each of the four recipes with rain water, record both EC estimates and the recipe's nominal EC. Which method is closer? Now use the softened water: why do the two methods diverge so much for a sodium-rich solution? (Hint: compare λ° of Na⁺ with those of K⁺ and Ca²⁺.)

Model assumptions

  • Compositions are those of the ideal formulae (commercial calcium nitrate as 5Ca(NO₃)₂·NH₄NO₃·10H₂O; iron chelates 6–13 % Fe; micronutrient contents of van der Lugt, 2016, Table 5). Real products differ slightly; always check the label.
  • Recipes: Dutch standard solutions for lettuce and herbs in water culture and for tomato on inert substrate (van der Lugt, 2016, after De Kreij et al., 1999), and the Cornell deep-water lettuce solution as tabulated in Lesson 5.2 (Brechner & Both, 2013). Micronutrients are scaled with the strength slider but are not optimised — each comes from one source.
  • Water examples are typical, published annual means (Norrvatten 2025; Uppsala Vatten 2023, data for 2022) and not guaranteed for any tap today. Potassium was not reported for Vänge and is taken as zero, which leaves an ion-balance error of about 3 %.
  • Solver: weighted non-negative least squares (Lawson & Hanson, 1974) with importance weights and a small preference penalty that selects conventional salt pairs; the resulting allocation is one of several chemically equivalent mixtures.
  • Carbonate chemistry at 25 °C without activity corrections; CO₂ in air 425 ppm (NOAA's 2024 global mean was 422.8 ppm); Henry's constant 0.0334 mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹ (Sander, 2023). Phosphoric acid is counted as delivering one proton (H₂PO₄⁻ reference).
  • EC by Σ cations/10 (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009) and by APHA Standard Methods 2510 (κ°·y², Davies); ion pairs not treated explicitly.
  • Precipitation: saturation indices for gypsum, brushite and calcite in the working solution with Davies activities; stock tanks are flagged by rule (Ca with SO₄ or PO₄) and by solubility of the pure salts, with temperature dependence only for KNO₃.
  • The animation shows ions as particles in proportion to their molar concentrations; it is qualitative.
