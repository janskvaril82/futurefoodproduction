Aquaponics system simulator

Run a complete coupled aquaponic system in a Nordic greenhouse. Nile tilapia swim in a glass-fronted tank, a swirl separator catches their faeces, a moving-bed biofilter turns ammonia into nitrate, and lettuce grows on floating rafts — all driven by a live model of fish appetite and growth, nitrogen excretion, two-step nitrification, plant uptake, dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide and pH. Start a new system and watch it “cycle”, overfeed it, switch off the blower or the base pump, and see every consequence in the water chemistry, the charts and the fish themselves. A guided tour of the system

The simulation starts from a mature system: 45 kg of 250 g tilapia (25 kg m⁻³) are fed 1.6 % of their body weight per day with a 32 % protein feed, and 10 m² of lettuce rafts take up the nitrate. One simulated day passes every second. Follow these steps in order.

  1. Follow the water. Pumped water enters the fish tank through the spray bar at the back, leaves over the stand-pipe to the swirl separator (tangential inlet: the water spins and settleable solids slide down into the cone), then flows to the moving-bed biofilter, down the manifold into the two raft beds, along the beds, into the sump, and is pumped back. The flow particles in the pipes show the path.
  2. Watch the daily rhythm. Set the speed to 6 h/s. During the feeding window (07:00–19:00 in the HUD) ammonia rises and oxygen falls in the charts; at night they recover. Fish digest for hours, so excretion peaks in the evening.
  3. Look at the biofilter. Click the translucent biofilter. Thousands of plastic carriers tumble in the aeration current; their colour darkens as the nitrifier biofilm grows. The inspector shows how much of the biofilm capacity is used.
  4. Read the lettuce production line. New transplants float at the far end of the beds; as they grow the rafts are pushed towards the inlet end, where the heads are harvested. Every 4.7 days the oldest cohort is harvested and a new one is planted, so the size gradient appears to move along the beds. Switch on Cut-away raft bed to see the roots hanging in the water.
  5. Start a new system. Choose the preset Start-up (new system). Only a trace of nitrifiers exists. Ammonia climbs first; a few days later nitrite takes over; after about three weeks both fall and nitrate appears — the system has “cycled”. Note that the start-up preset stocks only 5 kg m⁻³: with more fish the peaks would kill them.
  6. Break the blower. Choose Aeration failure (speed 1 h/s). Oxygen falls by roughly 2 mg L⁻¹ per hour; below 2 mg L⁻¹ the fish crowd at the surface, below 0.3 mg L⁻¹ they begin to die. Restore the aeration slider to rescue them — how many hours do you have?
  7. Overfeed. Choose Overfed. The fish cannot eat 4 % of their body weight at 26 °C: pellets settle on the tank floor, the water turns turbid, the sludge cone fills faster, oxygen demand rises and nitrite creeps above 1 mg L⁻¹.
  8. Switch off the base pump. Choose No pH control. Within days the alkalinity is gone, pH falls below 6, nitrification slows and ammonium accumulates — harmless to the fish only as long as the pH stays low.
Key idea

An aquaponic system is one body of water shared by three kinds of organisms. Everything is driven by the feed: its nitrogen becomes fish, ammonia and solids; the ammonia becomes nitrite and nitrate at a cost of oxygen and alkalinity; the nitrate feeds the plants — or accumulates. Change one flow and every other one responds, with time constants from hours (oxygen) to weeks (nitrifier growth, nitrate accumulation).

What you see is computed

The number of fish in the tank (one drawn fish represents four), their swimming speed and depth, the water turbidity, the sludge level, the carrier colour, the lettuce sizes, the bubbles and the flow particles are all driven by the model state. Labels and the inspector show live values; the HUD warns when a water-quality limit is exceeded.

The system as coupled mass balances

Engineers describe a recirculating system as compartments (pools) linked by flows. Because the whole water volume passes through all components about ten times a day, this simulator treats the water as one well-mixed volume, and follows eight pools: fish, fish-gut contents, suspended organic solids, total ammonia nitrogen (TAN = NH₃ + NH₄⁺), nitrite, nitrate, dissolved oxygen and the carbonate system (alkalinity and total inorganic carbon), plus two nitrifier populations attached to surfaces and six cohorts of lettuce. Figure 1 shows where each flow happens physically.

1 · The feed drives everything

Fish eat only up to their appetite. The maximum daily intake of Nile tilapia grows with body mass but more slowly than mass itself: in the experiments of Tran-Duy et al. (2008) at 32–33 °C, fish of about 37 g ate 5.8 % of their weight per day to satiation and fish of about 190 g only 3.2 %. The appetite also collapses in cold water (tilapia stop feeding below about 17 °C; Popma & Masser, 1999), at low dissolved oxygen, and at high un-ionised ammonia. Whatever is offered above the appetite is wasted: it becomes suspended solids that consume oxygen as bacteria decompose them. Food that is eaten is digested over several hours; about 80 % of its nitrogen is absorbed (a design value; Timmons & Ebeling, 2013), part is built into new fish protein, and the rest is excreted across the gills as ammonia. This is why the charts show ammonia peaking in the evening, hours after the meals.

2 · Nitrification: slow bacteria, fast consequences

Ammonia-oxidising bacteria (AOB) turn TAN into nitrite, nitrite-oxidising bacteria (NOB) turn nitrite into nitrate (Lesson 6.1). Both live in the biofilm on the moving-bed carriers, the tank walls and the undersides of the rafts. Their growth follows Monod kinetics on their substrate and on oxygen, doubles roughly every ten degrees, and falls steeply below pH ≈ 6.5. Because a new system starts with only a trace of them, and because NOB can only grow once AOB produce nitrite, a new system passes through an ammonia peak and then a nitrite peak. In a mature system the biofilm grows until it removes exactly the ammonia load; TAN then settles at a few tenths of a milligram per litre — not zero, because a biofilm needs some substrate to maintain itself. When the load approaches the capacity of the biofilm area, the TAN concentration rises steeply: that is what the biofilter capacity readout warns about.

Common misconception

“The plants clean the water.” In this system the lettuce takes up only about 5 % of the feed nitrogen. The water is cleaned of ammonia by bacteria — using oxygen and destroying alkalinity — and the nitrate that the lettuce does not take up is removed by denitrification in anoxic niches and by water exchange. Lennard (2017, cited in Lennard & Goddek, 2019) points out that the widely used UVI design is over-supplied with nitrogen and relies on passive denitrification.

3 · Oxygen: the fastest variable

Fish respiration, nitrification (4.57 g O₂ per g N) and the decomposition of solids all draw on the same dissolved oxygen, which is replenished by the blower through the transfer coefficient kLa. The system water holds only about 7 mg L⁻¹ × 6.0 m³ ≈ 42 g of oxygen, while the system consumes about 500 g per day — the whole oxygen content is used in about two hours. That is why an aeration failure is an emergency measured in hours, and why commercial systems keep back-up blowers or pure oxygen on standby.

4 · pH, alkalinity and CO₂: one triangle

Nitrification releases two hydrogen ions per ammonium oxidised, consuming about 7 g of alkalinity (as CaCO₃) per gram of nitrogen. The pH, however, depends on the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO₂: \(\text{pH} = \text{p}K_1 + \log_{10}([\text{HCO}_3^-]/[\text{CO}_2])\). In an air-aerated system CO₂ is stripped almost to equilibrium with air (a few mg L⁻¹), so holding the pH at 7.2 is only possible with a low alkalinity of 15–20 mg L⁻¹ as CaCO₃; operators who want a larger buffer must accept a higher pH — which raises the toxic NH₃ fraction. This is the pH compromise of coupled aquaponics: nitrifiers and fish prefer about 7–8, plants and nutrient availability 5.5–6.5, and the usual answer is pH ≈ 7 (Somerville et al., 2014; Lennard & Goddek, 2019).

5 · Balancing fish and plants

Rakocy et al. (2006) balanced the UVI raft system with a feed-rate ratio of 60–100 g of feed per m² of plant area per day; FAO recommends 40–50 g m⁻² d⁻¹ for leafy greens in small systems (Somerville et al., 2014). In the model you can check what these ratios mean for nitrogen: at 70 g m⁻² d⁻¹ the lettuce takes up a small part of the feed nitrogen and nitrate settles where denitrification and water exchange remove the surplus. Potassium, calcium and iron — scarce in fish feed — are supplied through the base (KOH or Ca(OH)₂) and chelated iron; the nutrient mass balance laboratory quantifies these deficits.

Frontier — decoupling the loops

Because fish, bacteria and plants want different pH, temperature and nutrient levels, European research has moved towards decoupled (multi-loop) aquaponics: the fish loop sends water to the plant loop only as fast as the crop evapotranspires it, sludge is mineralised in separate reactors, and nutrients are adjusted for the plants (Goddek et al., 2019). The single-loop system simulated here is the classical design; the nutrient mass balance lab compares both.

The equations behind the simulation

The model is a system of 41 ordinary differential equations (fish weight and number, gut contents, TAN, nitrite, nitrate, two nitrifier populations, suspended solids and their nitrogen, oxygen, alkalinity, inorganic carbon, six lettuce cohorts and cumulative budgets) integrated with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method in steps of four simulated minutes. \[ I_{\max} = a\,W^{\,b}\;\tau(T)\,\sigma(\text{DO})\,\upsilon(\text{UIA}), \qquad I = \min\!\left(\tfrac{R}{100}\,W,\; I_{\max}\right) \]

the most feed a tilapia will eat per day is a power function of its body mass, reduced by three factors for temperature, oxygen and un-ionised ammonia; the fish eats the ration offered or its appetite, whichever is smaller — the rest is wasted.

Follow the derivation (fitting to experimental data)
  1. Brett's classical growth–ration curve is close to a straight line between the maintenance ration and satiation (Brett, 1979; Jobling, 1994): \(G = e_g(I - M)\).
  2. Tran-Duy et al. (2008) give intake and growth at two oxygen levels for two sizes, e.g. small fish: \((I, G) = (2.15, 1.75)\) and \((1.54, 1.24)\) g d⁻¹. Two points fix a line: \(e_g = (1.75-1.24)/(2.15-1.54) = 0.84\).

\[ \dot m_{\text{O}_2,\text{fish}} = \frac{I\cdot ME - G\cdot E_{\text{fish}}}{Q_{\text{ox}}}, \qquad \dot m_{\text{CO}_2} = RQ\,\frac{44}{32}\,\dot m_{\text{O}_2} \]

\[ \frac{dW_k}{dt} = r\,W_k\left(1-\frac{W_k}{W_{\max}}\right)\frac{S_N}{K_N+S_N}\,f_{\text{DO}}\,f_T, \qquad U = n_{\text{pl}}\sum_k n_k\,\frac{dW_k}{dt} \]

each lettuce cohort grows logistically, slowed by a Michaelis–Menten factor for dissolved inorganic nitrogen and by root-zone oxygen and temperature; the crop takes up nitrogen in proportion to its growth.

Follow the derivation (calibrating growth)
  1. The logistic solution is \(W(t) = W_{\max}/(1 + A e^{-rt})\) with \(A = (W_{\max}-W_0)/W_0\).
  2. A transplant of \(W_0 = 3\) g reaches 150 g when \(e^{-rt} = (W_{\max}/150-1)/A = 0.667/82.3\), i.e. \(t = \ln(123.5)/r = 4.82/r\).
  3. Cornell's hydroponic system harvests 150 g heads 24 days after transplanting with CO₂ enrichment and 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (Brechner & Both, 2013); in a greenhouse without enrichment we assume ≈ 27 days: \(r = 4.82/27 \approx 0.18\) d⁻¹. Harvest is every 28/6 = 4.7 days.

Dissolved-oxygen balance(A10)
\[ V\frac{dC}{dt} = k_La\,\theta_a^{T-20}\,V\,(C_s - C) - \dot m_{\text{O}_2,\text{fish}} - 3.43\,r_A - 1.14\,r_N - 1.0\,k_{\min}S + Q_{\text{ex}}(C_s - C) \]

oxygen in the water increases by transfer from the air bubbles, in proportion to the deficit below saturation, and decreases by fish respiration, the two nitrification steps and the decomposition of solids.

Alkalinity, inorganic carbon and pH(A11)
\[ \text{Alk} = C_T(\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2) + \frac{K_w}{[\text{H}^+]} - [\text{H}^+],\quad V\frac{d\text{Alk}}{dt} = -1.98\frac{r_A}{14} + \frac{U_{\text{NO}_3}-U_{\text{NH}_4}+r_{\text{den}}}{14} + D_{\text{base}} + Q_{\text{ex}}(\text{Alk}_{\text{in}}-\text{Alk}) \]

alkalinity is the charge carried by bicarbonate, carbonate and hydroxide minus hydrogen ions; it is destroyed by ammonia oxidation, restored by nitrate uptake, denitrification, base dosing and exchange. Total inorganic carbon gains the CO₂ of respiration and loses CO₂ to the air bubbles; the pH is the value of [H⁺] that satisfies the charge balance.

Follow the derivation
  1. Carbon balance: \(V\,dC_T/dt = \) (CO₂ from fish and heterotrophs)/44 − \(0.9\,k_La\,V\,([\text{CO}_2]-[\text{CO}_2]_{\text{eq}})\) + carbon added with NaHCO₃ + exchange. The factor 0.9 is \((D_{\text{CO}_2}/D_{\text{O}_2})^{1/2}\) from penetration theory.
  2. Nitrification converts bicarbonate into CO₂: \(C_T\) is unchanged but alkalinity falls, so the pH drops.
  3. Every time step the charge balance is solved for pH by Newton's method, then \([\text{CO}_2] = C_T\,\alpha_0\).
  4. Near pH 7 this reduces to the Henderson–Hasselbalch form \(\text{pH} \approx \text{p}K_1 + \log_{10}([\text{HCO}_3^-]/[\text{CO}_2])\) used in the calculator below.
Assumptions and validity

Ionic strength corrections, ammonium buffering and calcium carbonate precipitation are neglected. Following standard RAS practice (Ebeling et al., 2006), excreted TAN is treated as NH₄⁺; the alkalinity gained when excreted NH₃ is protonated is neglected, which makes the predicted base demand conservative. One mole of OH⁻ per mole of nitrate taken up is an upper-bound assumption.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator to answer. Save investigation stores your settings and readouts in your notebook; Download time series gives the data behind the charts for your own plots.

  1. Cycling a new system

    Run Start-up (new system). From the chart and the CSV, find the day and height of the TAN peak and of the nitrite peak, and the day on which both stay below 0.5 mg L⁻¹. Repeat at 22 °C and 30 °C. Explain the delay between the peaks with Eq. A7, and estimate how much the temperature changes the cycling time. Why does the preset stock only 5 kg m⁻³?

  2. Balance the nitrogen

    In the mature system, record steady nitrate-N for plant areas of 5.8, 10.1 and 20.2 m² and exchange rates of 0.5, 1.5 and 5 % d⁻¹ (let each run for 60 days). Compute the feed-rate ratio of each design and compare it with the UVI range (60–100 g m⁻² d⁻¹) and the FAO recommendation for leafy greens (40–50). Which lever controls nitrate most strongly, and why do the plants remove so little nitrogen? Use the Sankey diagram as evidence.

  3. The oxygen emergency

    Run Aeration failure at 1 h/s. Measure the time from the failure until DO falls below 4, 2 and 0.3 mg L⁻¹, and the first fish deaths. Repeat at 15 and 40 kg m⁻³ and at 22 °C. Relate your times to Eq. A10 (\(dC/dt \approx -D/V\)) and propose an alarm threshold and a response plan (back-up blower, oxygen cylinder, stop feeding).

  4. The pH compromise

    Change the pH set-point from 6.6 to 7.8 in steps of 0.3 (mature system, 20 days each). Record un-ionised NH₃-N, TAN, alkalinity, CO₂ and base use. At which pH is the fish's ammonia exposure lowest, and what does it cost the nitrifiers? Repeat one setting with Ca(OH)₂ and NaHCO₃: how much K, Ca or Na does each base add per day, and which would you choose for lettuce?

  5. Feeding and solids

    Increase the feeding rate from 1.0 to 4.0 % BW d⁻¹. Plot FCR, suspended solids, nitrite and DO against the ration. Where does feed start to be wasted? Then keep the ration at 3 % and raise the separator efficiency from 30 to 90 %: which water-quality variables improve, and what happens to the nitrogen that leaves with the sludge?

  6. Fish or lettuce? The temperature trade-off

    For water temperatures of 20, 23, 26, 29 and 32 °C, record fish growth (harvest rate), FCR and lettuce production after 30 days. Tilapia want 29–31 °C, lettuce roots ≤ 25 °C (Brechner & Both, 2013). Which temperature maximises the combined value if tilapia sell for 60 SEK kg⁻¹ and lettuce for 40 SEK kg⁻¹? What does this suggest about choosing a cool-water species such as rainbow trout for Nordic aquaponics?

Model assumptions

  • One well-mixed water volume. Concentration differences between fish tank, biofilter and raft beds are ignored; in reality TAN is highest at the tank outlet and oxygen lowest there. Evaporation and transpiration are replaced by clean water that carries no alkalinity or nitrogen.
  • Fish. Nile tilapia of one mean size (250 g); satiation intake and the growth–ration line are fitted to Tran-Duy et al. (2008) at 32.7 °C and extrapolated to other temperatures with the cardinal-temperature function (Eq. A2) and Q₁₀ = 2 for maintenance. Nitrite and CO₂ do not affect appetite. With “staggered harvest” all positive growth is removed so that biomass is constant.
  • Mortality uses illustrative hazard functions anchored to published tolerance data: death starts below 0.3 mg O₂ L⁻¹ (half the fish in ≈ 5 h at anoxia), losses from un-ionised ammonia grow steeply from ≈ 1 mg NH₃ L⁻¹ (Popma & Masser, 1999), and nitrite kills half the fish in four days at 8 mg NO₂-N L⁻¹, the 96-h LC₅₀ for 90 g Nile tilapia reported by Atwood et al. (2001; reviewed in Kroupová et al., 2005). Chloride protection against nitrite is not modelled here.
  • Feed. Pellets with 32 % protein (variable), 94.5 % dry matter and 11.8 kJ g⁻¹ digestible energy; 80 % of the nitrogen and 70 % of the dry matter are digested; urea is lumped with ammonia. Feed is offered evenly between 07:00 and 19:00 and digested with a 5-hour time constant.
  • Biofilm. Area = media volume × 500 m² m⁻³ + raft undersides + 12 m² of walls and pipes. Kinetic constants are illustrative values of the right magnitude (Lesson 6.1; Rittmann & McCarty, 2020); the start-up inoculum is 2 × 10⁻⁵ of the maximum biomass. Heterotroph competition, diffusion within the film and comammox are not distinguished.
  • Denitrification is represented by one first-order constant, calibrated to lose about a quarter of the feed nitrogen, and returns 1 equivalent of alkalinity per mole of N. N₂O is not separated from N₂.
  • Lettuce grows logistically in six cohorts transplanted every 4.7 days; light, CO₂, potassium, phosphorus, calcium and iron are assumed non-limiting. The temperature penalty above 25 °C is illustrative.
  • Chemistry. Carbonate equilibria from Plummer and Busenberg (1982) without ionic-strength corrections; CO₂ stripping uses kLa(CO₂) = 0.9 kLa(O₂); atmospheric CO₂ = 425 ppm; make-up water has 100 mg L⁻¹ alkalinity as CaCO₃. Ammonium, phosphate and organic acids do not buffer.
  • The 3D scene is illustrative of a small commercial or teaching system (≈ 6 m³); one drawn fish represents four, the moving-bed carriers are drawn about twice their real size, and the lettuce heads show the cohort masses.
