A guided tour in seven steps

  1. Walk the pipeline. Click the eight thumbnails under the image from left to right. The RGB photo becomes chromatic coordinates (brightness removed — notice how the uneven lighting disappears), then a greenness index, a black-and-white mask, a cleaned mask, coloured objects, lesion pixels and finally the measured result. Hover over the image to read the numbers behind any pixel.
  2. Look at the histogram. Plant and background pixels form two humps; Otsu's method puts the threshold where the two classes are best separated (the peak of the magenta curve). Drag the threshold into the plant hump and watch leaf edges and pale inner leaves disappear from the mask; drag it into the background hump and the raft starts to count as lettuce.
  3. Break it with light. Choose the preset Red + blue LEDs. Under “blurple” light the green channel is weak, the lettuce looks purple-black and excess green fails. Then apply … white-balanced: correcting the colours with the white border of the reference card brings the plants back (recall recovers), but the strongly amplified, noisy green channel now adds false plant pixels. Take your project photos under white light.
  4. Let the plants grow. Move Days after transplanting to 33. Neighbouring canopies touch and merge into one connected component: the readout Plants found drops. Switch One plant = to Nearest planting hole, which splits the canopy between the known raft positions.
  5. From pixels to grams. Every image contains a 5 cm blue card, so the lab knows how many millimetres a pixel spans. Half of the plants are “harvested” and weighed; a power law fitted to them estimates the mass of the rest. Compare the estimates with the truth in the table and the Non-destructive estimates chart. Then lower the camera to 50 cm and watch perspective inflate the areas.
  6. Find the sick plants. Step 7 shows brown pixels (lesions and tipburn) in magenta. The confusion matrix compares flagged plants with the truth. Lower the flagging threshold until every symptomatic plant is caught — how many healthy plants are flagged now?
  7. Try hard cases, then your own photo. Red lettuce on coir fools both the greenness index (anthocyanins make leaves red) and the lesion rule (the brown substrate looks like necrosis). Then choose My photo, upload a top-view picture of your own plants with a reference of known size, and measure them.
Key idea

Classical image analysis is a chain of simple, transparent operations. Each has an assumption — plants are greener than the background, the light is white, plants do not touch, brown means disease — and each assumption can fail. Look at every intermediate image, and check the final numbers against ground truth (weighed plants, a hand count) before you trust them.

Non-destructive growth measurement in your project

Photograph each plant or tray from straight above at a fixed height, at the same time of day, with a reference card at canopy height and ideally under the room's white light. Measure projected area with this pipeline and store it in the Grow log (column area_cm2). Weigh a few plants at harvest to calibrate area against fresh mass (Eq. PV9). Your image series then becomes a growth curve in the Model-fitting lab — without harvesting a single plant before the end of the experiment.

An image is a table of numbers

A colour photo is three matrices — red, green and blue intensities from 0 to 255 for every pixel. Leaves reflect green more than red and blue because chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light; soil, polystyrene and plastic reflect the three bands more evenly. Everything in this lab exploits that single physical fact (Woebbecke et al., 1995; Hamuda et al., 2016). The pipeline below is the backbone of widely used tools such as ImageJ, Easy Leaf Area and PlantCV (Schneider et al., 2012; Easlon & Bloom, 2014; Gehan et al., 2017).

Why normalise and why an index?

Brightness varies across a photo — lamps are closer to some plants, the edge of the image is darker (vignetting), a leaf shades its neighbour. Dividing each channel by the sum of the three (chromatic coordinates) removes most of this variation, because a shadow darkens R, G and B by nearly the same factor (Eq. PV1). A colour index then compresses the three numbers into one “greenness” score: excess green, ExG = 2g − r − b, is positive for leaves and near zero for grey or black backgrounds (Woebbecke et al., 1995); ExG − ExR subtracts an excess-red term and is often more robust on soil (Meyer & Neto, 2008). The same idea in other colour spaces uses hue (HSV; Smith, 1978) or the green–red axis a* of CIELAB, which was designed to be perceptually uniform.

Choosing the threshold

After the index, one number per pixel decides plant or background. Otsu (1979) chooses the threshold that makes the two resulting classes as internally homogeneous — equivalently, as different from each other — as possible (Eq. PV5). It needs no tuning and adapts to exposure changes, but it assumes two reasonably separated humps in the histogram. With very small plants (few plant pixels) or strongly coloured backgrounds it can fail, and a fixed, validated threshold may be better (Hamuda et al., 2016).

Cleaning, counting and measuring

A thresholded mask contains specks (noise, algae, reflections) and ragged edges. Morphological opening — an erosion followed by a dilation with a small structuring element — removes objects thinner than the element while preserving the size of larger ones (Serra, 1982). Connected-component labelling then groups touching pixels into objects (Rosenfeld & Pfaltz, 1966). Each object's pixel count times the area of one pixel is its projected leaf area; the pixel size comes from a reference of known size photographed in the same plane (Eq. PV6). Two practical failures dominate: touching canopies merge into one object (use plant spacing or the known planting grid), and a reference lying lower than the leaves makes them look too large (perspective, Eq. PV6).

From projected area to fresh mass

A camera sees the plant's shadow, not its volume. The relation between projected area and fresh mass must therefore be calibrated with a set of plants that are photographed and then weighed. Mass usually increases faster than projected area — leaves stack and heads thicken — so a power law \(W = aA^b\) with \(b > 1\) fits better than a straight line (Eq. PV9; Golzarian et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2020). The calibration only holds for the same cultivar, growing system, camera set-up and size range; the prediction interval tells you how precisely a single plant can be weighed by photo.

Detecting symptoms — and what colour cannot tell you

Necrotic tissue is brown: low hue, moderate saturation, neither black nor white. A simple HSV rule finds brown pixels next to plants; the share of brown pixels per plant decides whether it is flagged (Eq. PV10). Evaluating such a detector needs ground truth and the right metrics: precision (how many flagged plants really have symptoms), recall (how many symptomatic plants are found) and their harmonic mean F1 (Eq. PV11; Fawcett, 2006). Colour cannot tell a fungal lesion from tipburn or from a brown substrate: that is why the “truth” in this lab is “has symptoms” and why a coir background produces false alarms. Deep-learning classifiers learn such distinctions from thousands of labelled images — with their own risks when field conditions differ from the training images (Mohanty et al., 2016; Barbedo, 2018; Mahlein, 2016).

Common misconception

“My segmentation looks perfect, so my areas are right.” A perfect mask can still give wrong square centimetres: a reference lying on the tray while the canopy is 10 cm closer to a camera at 60 cm overestimates areas by 44 % (Eq. PV6). Check scale and perspective with an object of known size at canopy height.

Frontier

Research greenhouses now combine depth cameras and deep networks to estimate lettuce fresh mass, leaf area and height non-destructively (Zhang et al., 2020; Petropoulou et al., 2023), and foundation models such as Segment Anything can outline leaves without training (Kirillov et al., 2023). The classical pipeline in this lab remains the transparent baseline against which such systems are judged — see Lesson 10.3.

The equations behind the pipeline

Chromatic coordinates(PV1)
\[ r = \frac{R}{R+G+B},\qquad g = \frac{G}{R+G+B},\qquad b = \frac{B}{R+G+B},\qquad r+g+b=1 \]

each channel is divided by the sum of the three, so only the proportions of red, green and blue remain and the overall brightness is removed.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(R, G, B\)pixel intensities in the red, green and blue channel0–255 (digital numbers)
\(r, g, b\)chromatic coordinates–
Why shadows almost disappear

If a shadow reduces the light on a leaf by a factor \(k\), all channels scale approximately as \(kR, kG, kB\); the ratios \(kR/(kR+kG+kB) = R/(R+G+B)\) are unchanged. This fails for coloured light (a red+blue LED changes the ratios themselves — see PV4) and for saturated pixels (a channel stuck at 255).

A ratio of intensities is dimensionless ✓ (as in Lesson 10.3, Eq. 10.3.1)
Excess green and excess red(PV2)
\[ \text{ExG} = 2g-r-b,\qquad \text{ExR} = 1.4\,r-g,\qquad \text{ExG}-\text{ExR} = 3g-2.4\,r-b \]

excess green measures how much the green proportion exceeds the average of red and blue (times two); excess red does the same for red; their difference separates plants from soil and residue on both sides.

Why “2g − r − b”?

With \(r+g+b = 1\), \(\text{ExG} = 2g - (1-g) = 3g - 1\): excess green is simply a rescaled green chromatic coordinate, zero for a neutral grey pixel (\(g = 1/3\)). ExG − ExR has a natural threshold at 0 (Meyer & Neto, 2008).

HSV and CIELAB a*(PV3)
\[ V=\max(R,G,B),\quad S=\frac{V-\min(R,G,B)}{V},\quad H = 60^{\circ}\times\begin{cases}\frac{G-B}{\Delta}\bmod 6 & V=R\\ \frac{B-R}{\Delta}+2 & V=G\\ \frac{R-G}{\Delta}+4 & V=B\end{cases},\qquad a^{*} = 500\bigl[f(X/X_n)-f(Y/Y_n)\bigr] \]

value is the brightest channel, saturation the relative spread between brightest and darkest, and hue the angle on the colour wheel (0° red, 60° yellow, 120° green); in CIELAB, a* is negative for green and positive for red, computed from linearised sRGB via the CIE XYZ values.

Where each space helps

Hue separates “what colour” from “how bright”, so a hue window can segment plants under uneven light and a low-hue rule finds brown tissue (Smith, 1978). a* is a perceptual green–red axis; −a* is used as a greenness index in this lab. Both are defined for sRGB images with white illumination; coloured light shifts them too.

\(S\) and \(a^*\) are dimensionless; \(H\) is an angle ✓
White balance on a grey reference (von Kries scaling)(PV4)
\[ R' = k_R R,\quad G' = k_G G,\quad B' = k_B B,\qquad k_c = \frac{(\bar R_w+\bar G_w+\bar B_w)/3}{\bar c_w} \]

each channel is multiplied by a gain chosen so that a surface known to be white or grey — here the white border of the reference card — comes out neutral, with the same mean brightness as before.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\bar c_w\)mean of channel \(c\) over the white reference pixelsDN
\(k_c\)gain of channel \(c\)–
Why it cannot fully rescue red+blue light

A diagonal gain corrects the colour cast only if each camera channel sees a scaled version of the same spectrum. Under narrow-band red and blue LEDs almost no green light reaches the leaf, so the green channel carries little information and noise is amplified by the large \(k_G\). Take phenotyping photos under white light or with a flash.

Gains are ratios of intensities → dimensionless ✓
Otsu's threshold(PV5)
\[ \sigma_B^{2}(t) = \omega_0(t)\,\omega_1(t)\,\bigl[\mu_0(t)-\mu_1(t)\bigr]^{2},\qquad t^{*} = \arg\max_t\ \sigma_B^{2}(t) \]

for every candidate threshold, split the histogram into a background class below and a plant class above; the between-class variance is the product of the two class weights times the squared difference of their means; the best threshold maximises it.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\omega_0, \omega_1\)fractions of pixels below and above \(t\)–
\(\mu_0, \mu_1\)mean index of the two classesunit of the index
Why maximise the between-class variance?
  1. The total variance of the index is fixed: \(\sigma_T^2 = \sigma_W^2(t) + \sigma_B^2(t)\).
  2. Maximising \(\sigma_B^2\) is therefore the same as minimising the within-class variance \(\sigma_W^2\): the two classes become as homogeneous as possible (Otsu, 1979).
  3. The lab evaluates all 127 candidate thresholds of a 128-bin histogram between the 0.2nd and 99.8th percentiles — the magenta curve in the histogram chart.
\(\sigma_B^2\) has the unit of the index squared; \(t^*\) the unit of the index ✓
Pixel scale, projected area and perspective(PV6)
\[ s = \frac{L_{\text{ref}}}{\sqrt{N_{\text{ref}}}},\qquad A = N_{\text{px}}\,s^{2}\left(\frac{D-h}{D}\right)^{2} \]

the pixel scale is the side of the square reference divided by the square root of its pixel count; a plant's projected area is its pixel count times the area of one pixel, corrected by the squared ratio of distances when the leaves are a height \(h\) closer to the camera than the reference.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(L_{\text{ref}}, N_{\text{ref}}\)side of the blue reference square (5 cm) and its pixel countcm, px
\(s\)object size per pixel (ground sampling distance)cm px⁻¹
\(D\)camera height above the reference planecm
\(h\)canopy height above the reference planecm
Follow the derivation
  1. A square of side \(L\) covering \(N\) pixels: \(N s^2 = L^2\) ⇒ \(s = L/\sqrt N\). Using the area of the card rather than one edge averages over many pixels and is less sensitive to blur.
  2. Pinhole camera: image size ∝ object size / distance (Lesson 10.3, Eq. 10.3.7). Leaves at distance \(D-h\) are magnified by \(D/(D-h)\) relative to the reference, areas by its square.
  3. Example: \(D = 60\) cm, \(h = 10\) cm → factor \((50/60)^2 = 0.69\): without correction, areas are 44 % too large.
\(\text{px}\cdot(\text{cm px}^{-1})^2 = \text{cm}^2\) ✓; the distance ratio is dimensionless ✓
Erosion, dilation and opening(PV7)
\[ (A\ominus B)(x) = \min_{b\in B} A(x+b),\qquad (A\oplus B)(x) = \max_{b\in B} A(x+b),\qquad A\circ B = (A\ominus B)\oplus B \]

erosion keeps a pixel only if the whole structuring element around it lies inside the mask; dilation switches a pixel on if any part of the element touches the mask; opening erodes and then dilates, removing objects smaller than the element while restoring the size of the rest.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(A\)binary mask (1 = plant)–
\(B\)structuring element: a square of side \(2r+1\) pixelspx
Fast computation

For a square element, erosion and dilation are separable: a horizontal pass followed by a vertical pass. With running sums, each pass costs a constant time per pixel regardless of \(r\) — about 20 ms for this 960 × 600 image. Opening is idempotent (opening twice changes nothing) and never adds pixels (Serra, 1982). Closing, \((A\oplus B)\ominus B\), is its dual and fills small holes.

Hole filling

Pale rosette hearts and midribs can fall below the threshold and leave holes inside a plant. The lab optionally fills them: a flood fill from the image border marks all background that can be reached (4-connectivity); every unreached background pixel lies inside an object and is set to plant. Beware: background enclosed by touching plants is filled too.

Binary images in, binary images out ✓
Connected components (8-connectivity)(PV8)
\[ p\sim q \iff \exists\ p=p_0,p_1,\ldots,p_k=q:\ A(p_i)=1,\ \ \lVert p_{i+1}-p_i\rVert_\infty = 1 \]

two plant pixels belong to the same object if a chain of plant pixels, each touching the next horizontally, vertically or diagonally, connects them.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty\)chessboard distance: 8 neighbours at distance 1px
The two-pass algorithm
  1. Scan row by row; give each plant pixel the smallest label among its already-visited neighbours (left, upper-left, up, upper-right), or a new label.
  2. When neighbours carry different labels, record that they are equivalent (union–find).
  3. Second pass: replace each label by the representative of its equivalence class (Rosenfeld & Pfaltz, 1966). Objects smaller than the minimum area are discarded as noise.
Labels are integers; areas are pixel counts ✓
Calibrating fresh mass against projected area(PV9)
\[ W = a\,A^{b}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ln W = \ln a + b\ln A,\qquad \hat W_{\text{new}} = \hat a\,A_{\text{new}}^{\hat b},\qquad \text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum\frac{|\hat W_i - W_i|}{W_i} \]

fresh mass is modelled as a power of projected area; taking logarithms makes it a straight line fitted by least squares to the harvested plants; the fitted curve then estimates the mass of plants that were only photographed, and the mean absolute percentage error measures how well.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a, b\)calibration coefficients (synthetic truth here: 0.122 and 1.18, as in Lesson 10.3, Eq. 10.3.8)g cm⁻²ᵇ, –
\(A\)measured projected areacm²
Assumptions and validity

Same cultivar, growing system, camera geometry and size range as the calibration plants. Back-transforming a log-scale fit estimates the median mass; the smearing factor \(e^{s^2/2}\) converts to the mean (Lesson 10.3). In this lab the true masses contain 8 % random plant-to-plant variation, so even a perfect area measurement cannot estimate single plants better than about 8 %.

\([a][A]^b = \text{g}\) by the choice of units for \(a\) ✓
Lesion pixels and the flagging rule(PV10)
\[ \text{lesion}(x) = \bigl[H(x)\le H_{\max}\bigr]\wedge\bigl[S(x)\ge S_{\min}\bigr]\wedge\bigl[0.1\le V(x)\le 0.85\bigr]\wedge\bigl[\operatorname{dist}(x,\text{plant})\le 0.8\ \text{cm}\bigr],\qquad \phi_k = \frac{n_{\text{lesion},k}}{n_{\text{plant},k}+n_{\text{lesion},k}} \ge \tau \]

a pixel counts as necrotic when its hue is brownish, it is saturated enough, neither black nor white, and it lies close to a plant; a plant is flagged when the share of such pixels in its area exceeds the threshold τ.

\(H_{\max}\)largest “brown” hue45°
\(S_{\min}\)minimum saturation0.30

\(\tau\)flagging threshold1 %
Assumptions and validity

A colour rule cannot distinguish disease from tipburn, senescence, nutrient deficiency or a brown background — hence the “has symptoms” ground truth and the false alarms on coir. Symptoms hidden under younger leaves cannot be seen from above at all (a source of false negatives).

\(\phi\) is a ratio of pixel counts → dimensionless ✓
Confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1 and IoU(PV11)
\[ \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}},\qquad \text{IoU} = \frac{|M\cap G|}{|M\cup G|} \]

precision is the share of flagged plants that truly have symptoms, recall the share of symptomatic plants that were flagged, F1 their harmonic mean; for the segmentation itself, the intersection over union compares the predicted plant mask with the true one.

SymbolMeaningUnit
TP, FP, FN, TNtrue/false positives and negatives (plants)count
\(M, G\)predicted and true plant pixelspx
Which metric matters?

For a disease scout, missing an infected plant (FN) is usually worse than inspecting a healthy one (FP): prefer high recall and accept lower precision. Accuracy is misleading when symptomatic plants are rare. The lab computes these with classificationMetrics of the course library (Fawcett, 2006; Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.10).

All are ratios of counts → dimensionless (0–1) ✓

Assumptions and simplifications

  • Synthetic images. Rosettes are drawn procedurally (leaf outlines with wavy or lobed margins, colour gradients, veins and soft shadows) on four backgrounds. They are realistic enough to exercise the pipeline, but real photos add specular highlights, motion blur, compression artefacts, algae, labels and cables.
  • Ground truth is known exactly for every pixel. True fresh masses are synthetic: \(W = 0.122\,A^{1.18}e^{0.08\varepsilon}\), the illustrative calibration of Lesson 10.3, with 8 % random variation. Real calibrations must be measured for your cultivar and system.
  • Symptoms. Infected plants carry brown necrotic spots with a chlorotic halo; tipburn is drawn as brown margins on the youngest leaves after day 14. “Truth” = infected or tipburn. Symptoms under younger leaves are invisible from above, as in reality.
  • Light and camera colours. Illumination is represented by three broad bands (red, green and blue light) that multiply the surface colours, followed by a camera cross-talk matrix (each colour channel sees a little of the neighbouring bands) and Gaussian sensor noise. White LED = equal bands; warm/HPS-like = little blue; red + blue LED = about 5 % green. These band intensities are illustrative, not measured spectra.
  • Camera model. A pinhole camera looking straight down; leaves are enlarged by \(D/(D-h)\) with a canopy height that grows with plant size; lens distortion and oblique views are ignored.
  • Algorithms. Square structuring element; optional hole filling by flood fill from the border; 8-connectivity; Otsu on a 128-bin histogram between the 0.2nd and 99.8th percentiles; white balance by diagonal (von Kries) gains; brown-pixel rule in HSV. These are standard textbook choices, not the only ones (Hamuda et al., 2016; Szeliski, 2022).
  • Your photos are read with the browser's FileReader, scaled to at most 1 100 × 760 px and never leave your computer.
