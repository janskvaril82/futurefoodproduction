Hedonic study analyser

Every consumer in a hedonic test rates every product on the 9-point scale — and every consumer uses the scale differently. This analyser turns a table of ratings into the answers a product developer needs: how much each product is liked (with its uncertainty), whether the products differ once each panellist's personal scale use is removed, which pairs differ and by how much, whether the panel hides segments that love or hate the novel product, and whether liking follows food neophobia. Start with the illustrative cricket-bread study, simulate your own scenarios, paste your data, or load the ratings recorded in the sensory booth.

Your data

One row per panellist, one column per product (9-point ratings, CSV or tab-separated, e.g. copied from a spreadsheet). A first column of panellist codes and a column named FNS (or covariate) are recognised automatically. Rows with missing values or values outside 1–9 are dropped and reported.

Why blocking matters

The same ratings split into sums of squares. A one-way ANOVA leaves the differences between panellists in the error term; the block ANOVA moves them out, so the same product differences stand out against a smaller error.

Which products differ? (Tukey HSD)

Simultaneous 95 % confidence intervals for all pairwise differences, from the block error term (thick; magenta = significant). Thin grey lines: the same intervals if panellists were ignored.

Consumer segments

Mean liking profile of each k-means segment compared with the whole panel. Diverging profiles mean that the panel average describes nobody.

Liking and food neophobia

Each point is a panellist: covariate (e.g., Food Neophobia Scale score) against the chosen liking measure, with the least-squares line and its 95 % confidence band.

What to look at first

  1. Look at the raw ratings. In the Ratings view every dot is one panellist. The black diamond and bar show the mean and its 95 % confidence interval; the box shows the quartiles; the letters summarise Tukey's test. Liking falls as the share of cricket flour rises — but look at how widely the dots spread: individual consumers disagree by several scale points.
  2. See the panellists. Switch to Panellists. Many lines run roughly parallel: some people rate everything high, others everything low. That is scale use, not product liking. The block ANOVA removes it; compare the two F tests in the readouts and the Why blocking matters chart. In the example the block ANOVA gives F(2, 70) = 18.1 against F(2, 105) = 10.5 for the one-way analysis, and blocking is worth about 1.7 times as many panellists.
  3. Find the hidden segment. With k = 2 the lines are coloured by segment. One group likes all three breads almost equally, the other rejects the cricket breads. The Consumer segments chart shows the two profiles, and the covariate chart shows that the rejecters tend to score higher on food neophobia. (The example is simulated and labelled as such — your own data may or may not show segments.)
  4. Break the blocks. Apply the preset Huge scale-use differences: the one-way ANOVA loses almost all its power while the block ANOVA hardly notices. The efficiency of blocking readout tells you how many more panellists a design without blocking would need.
  5. Check the assumptions. The Residuals view shows the residuals after removing panellist and product means. Stripes are normal for integer ratings; strong skewness or outliers call for the Friedman test, which the readouts report as a rank-based check.
  6. Use your own data. Paste your table below the readouts, or choose Project to load the ratings recorded in the sensory booth. Read off the numbers for your report: means with CIs, F and p of the block ANOVA, Tukey comparisons with effect sizes.
Key idea

In a hedonic test each consumer is a block. Comparing products within consumers removes the largest source of noise — the way each person uses the scale — and turns a small student panel into a sensitive instrument. But an average can hide opposite opinions: always look at the distribution and at possible segments before concluding that “consumers like the product moderately”.

From ratings to conclusions

The 9-point hedonic scale (Peryam & Pilgrim, 1957) asks consumers how much they like a product, from “dislike extremely” (1) to “like extremely” (9). In a typical study every consumer tastes every product in a balanced order (sensory booth), so the data form a table with one row per consumer and one column per product. Three questions follow: how much is each product liked, do the products differ, and is the answer the same for everybody?

1. How much is each product liked?

The mean rating with a 95 % confidence interval (Eq. H1) is the basic summary. Remember what it is: an average of ordered categories whose spacing is not perfectly equal (Lesson 13.3). Report the share of consumers who like the product (6–9) alongside the mean — it is easier to communicate and less sensitive to how the categories are spaced.

2. Do the products differ? Blocking

The ratings vary for three reasons: products differ, consumers differ in how they use the scale, and every single rating contains noise. A one-way ANOVA compares products against all variation within products — including the consumer differences. The randomised complete block ANOVA (Eq. H2) estimates a separate effect for each consumer and removes it, so the product F test is computed against the residual noise only. The relative efficiency (Eq. H3) turns the gain into a number of consumers: an efficiency of 2 means that a design without blocking would need twice as many consumers for the same precision.

3. Which products differ, and by how much?

A significant F says only that some products differ. Tukey's honestly significant difference (Eq. H4) compares all pairs while keeping the familywise error rate at 5 %; it must use the error mean square and degrees of freedom of the block ANOVA. Effect sizes (Eq. H5) express the differences in scale points and in standardised form. When the residuals are badly skewed — many ratings at the ends of the scale, a few wild consumers — the Friedman test (Eq. H6), which uses only the ranks within each consumer, is a useful check.

4. Is the answer the same for everybody? Segmentation

For novel foods, a mean rating of 5.5 may hide a group that likes the product and a group that rejects it. Consumer segmentation groups panellists with similar liking profiles. The analyser uses k-means (Eq. H7): it finds k centre profiles that minimise the squared distance of each panellist to its nearest centre (Lloyd, 1982). Centring each panellist's ratings first removes scale use, so that the clusters reflect what people like rather than how high they rate. Segments found in a small panel are hypotheses, not findings: k-means always returns k groups, even in random data, so check whether the segments make sense and, ideally, whether they relate to something measured independently — such as food neophobia (Eq. H8; neophobia-scales laboratory).

Common pitfalls with student data

(1) Treating the ratings of one consumer as independent observations — use the block ANOVA. (2) Running many t-tests between pairs of products — use Tukey. (3) Claiming “no difference” from a non-significant F with 15 consumers — report the confidence intervals. (4) Deleting consumers who “rate strangely” — that is exactly the variation a segmentation should explain.

The statistics behind the analyser

Product mean and its confidence interval(H1)
\[ \bar y_{\cdot j} = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b} y_{ij}, \qquad \bar y_{\cdot j} \pm t_{0.975,\,b-1}\,\frac{s_j}{\sqrt b} \]

the mean liking of product j is the average rating over the b panellists; its 95 % confidence interval extends a t-quantile times the standard error on either side.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(y_{ij}\)rating of product \(j\) by panellist \(i\)points (1–9)
\(b\)number of panellists–
\(s_j\)standard deviation of the ratings of product \(j\)points
Assumptions and validity

Independent panellists; the interval treats the category numbers as interval-scaled and relies on the central limit theorem, which is reasonable for about 20 or more panellists. This interval contains the panellist variation; the block analysis below compares products within panellists and is more precise for differences.

\(t\) is dimensionless, \(s/\sqrt b\) is in points ✓
Block ANOVA: products and panellists(H2)
\[ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_j + \beta_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad SS_T = SS_{\text{prod}} + SS_{\text{pan}} + SS_E, \qquad F = \frac{SS_{\text{prod}}/(t-1)}{SS_E/[(t-1)(b-1)]} \]

each rating is the overall mean plus a product effect plus a panellist effect plus random error; the total variation splits into the three parts, and the product effect is tested against the error mean square with t − 1 and (t − 1)(b − 1) degrees of freedom.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\tau_j, \beta_i\)effect of product \(j\); effect (scale use) of panellist \(i\)points
\(SS_{\text{prod}} = b\sum_j(\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y)^2\)between-product sum of squarespoints²
\(SS_{\text{pan}} = t\sum_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y)^2\)between-panellist sum of squarespoints²
\(SS_E\)residual sum of squares (by subtraction)points²
Follow the derivation
  1. Split every deviation: \(y_{ij}-\bar y = (\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y) + (\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y) + (y_{ij}-\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{i\cdot}+\bar y)\).
  2. Square and sum over \(i, j\): the cross-products vanish because each part sums to zero over the index it does not depend on.
  3. Degrees of freedom: \(bt-1 = (t-1)+(b-1)+(t-1)(b-1)\).
  4. A one-way ANOVA puts \(SS_{\text{pan}}+SS_E\) into its error term with \(t(b-1)\) df — that is the grey bar of the Why blocking matters chart.
Assumptions and validity

Additivity (no panellist × product interaction beyond the error), approximately normal errors with equal variance, independent panellists. With balanced data a mixed model with random panellists gives the same product F test (Næs et al., 2010). The analyser is computed with anovaRCBD from the course statistics library.

SS in points², so F = points²/points² is dimensionless ✓
Relative efficiency of blocking(H3)
\[ \mathrm{RE} = \frac{(b-1)\,MS_{\text{pan}} + b(t-1)\,MS_E}{(bt-1)\,MS_E}\cdot\frac{(f_1+1)(f_2+3)}{(f_1+3)(f_2+1)}, \qquad f_1 = (t-1)(b-1),\; f_2 = t(b-1) \]

the error variance that a design without blocking would have had is estimated from the block ANOVA's mean squares; its ratio to the block design's error mean square, with a small correction for the different degrees of freedom, is how many times more panellists the unblocked design would need.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(MS_{\text{pan}}, MS_E\)panellist and error mean squares of the block ANOVApoints²
\(f_1, f_2\)error degrees of freedom of the block and the unblocked design–
RErelative efficiency (≈ factor by which the panel would have to grow)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Without blocking, the error would contain the panellist variation: its expected value is estimated by pooling panellist and error sums of squares, \([(b-1)MS_{\text{pan}} + b(t-1)MS_E]/(bt-1)\) (Montgomery, 2017).
  2. The variance of a product mean is error variance divided by \(b\); equal precision therefore needs \(b\) to grow in proportion to the error variance.
  3. Fisher's factor corrects for the fact that fewer error degrees of freedom make an estimated variance slightly less useful.
points²/points² — dimensionless ✓
Tukey HSD after the block ANOVA(H4)
\[ \text{HSD} = q_{0.95;\,t,\,(t-1)(b-1)}\sqrt{\frac{MS_E}{b}}, \qquad (\bar y_{\cdot k}-\bar y_{\cdot j}) \pm \text{HSD} \]

two product means differ significantly when their difference exceeds the studentised-range quantile times the standard error of a mean from the block error term; the same half-width gives simultaneous 95 % intervals for all pairwise differences.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(q\)upper 5 % point of the studentised range for \(t\) means and \((t-1)(b-1)\) df–
\(MS_E\)error mean square of the block ANOVApoints²
Where it comes from

If no products differ, the range of the \(t\) means divided by \(\sqrt{MS_E/b}\) follows the studentised range distribution; controlling the range controls every pairwise comparison, so the familywise error rate is 5 %. The analyser uses tukeyRCBD from the course library (see also Eq. 13.4.9 in Lesson 13.4). The grey intervals use the one-way error term instead and are wider whenever panellists differ.

points ✓
Effect sizes(H5)
\[ \eta_p^2 = \frac{SS_{\text{prod}}}{SS_{\text{prod}} + SS_E}, \qquad d_z = \frac{\bar d}{s_d}, \qquad \bar d \pm t_{0.975,\,b-1}\frac{s_d}{\sqrt b} \]

partial eta squared is the product share of product-plus-error variation; for a pair of products, d z is the mean of the within-panellist differences divided by their standard deviation, and the paired confidence interval gives the difference in scale points.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(d_i = y_{ik}-y_{ij}\)difference between two ratings of panellist \(i\)points
\(\bar d, s_d\)mean and SD of the differencespoints
Interpretation

Report the difference in points with its interval first (“the 10 % bread was liked 1.3 points less, 95 % CI 0.8–1.8”), then \(d_z\) or \(\eta_p^2\). Cohen's benchmarks were defined for independent groups and should not be applied mechanically to \(d_z\) (Lakens, 2013).

\(\bar d/s_d\): points/points — dimensionless ✓
Friedman rank test (with ties)(H6)
\[ \chi^2_F = \frac{\dfrac{12}{b\,t(t+1)}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t} R_j^2 - 3b(t+1)}{1 - \dfrac{\sum \left(\tau^3-\tau\right)}{b\,(t^3-t)}}, \qquad \chi^2_F \;\dot\sim\; \chi^2_{t-1} \]

rank the products within each panellist, add the ranks of each product over panellists, and compare the spread of the rank sums with what random ranking would give; the denominator corrects for tied ratings.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(R_j\)sum over panellists of the within-panellist rank of product \(j\) (ties: average ranks)–
\(\tau\)size of each group of tied ratings within a panellist–
Why use it

The test uses only the order of the products within each consumer, so it needs no assumption about the spacing of the scale categories or about normality (Friedman, 1937). With 9-point data many ratings are tied, hence the correction. It answers the same question as the block ANOVA's F test and should agree with it when the ANOVA assumptions hold.

ranks are dimensionless ✓
k-means segmentation of panellists(H7)
\[ \min_{c_1,\dots,c_k}\ \sum_{i=1}^{b} \min_{s}\ \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c}_s \rVert^2, \qquad x_{ij} = y_{ij} - \bar y_{i\cdot}\ \ \text{(centred profiles)} \]

choose k centre profiles so that the sum of squared distances from each panellist's liking profile to its nearest centre is as small as possible; with centring, each profile is the panellist's ratings minus his or her own mean.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathbf{x}_i\)profile of panellist \(i\) (ratings of the \(t\) products, optionally centred)points
\(\mathbf{c}_s\)centre (mean profile) of segment \(s\)points
\(k\)number of segments–
Follow the algorithm
  1. Start: pick initial centres at random, spread out (k-means++: each new centre with probability proportional to its squared distance from the existing ones).
  2. Assign every panellist to the nearest centre.
  3. Update each centre to the mean profile of its members (Lloyd, 1982). Steps 2–3 never increase the objective, so the algorithm converges.
  4. Because it can stop in a local minimum, the analyser restarts 24 times and keeps the best solution; segments are labelled A, B, … from the highest to the lowest liking of the most novel product.
  5. The readout reports the share of the variation between segments, \(1 - \text{WSS}/\text{TSS}\); even random data give a positive share, so treat segments as hypotheses.
squared distances in points² ✓
Liking and a covariate: correlation and regression(H8)
\[ r = \frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2}}, \qquad \tanh\!\left(\operatorname{artanh} r \pm \frac{1.96}{\sqrt{n-3}}\right), \qquad \hat y = a + b\,x \]

the Pearson correlation measures how closely liking follows the covariate on a straight line; its confidence interval is computed on Fisher's z scale and transformed back; the least-squares line gives the expected change in liking per point of the covariate.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x_i\)covariate of panellist \(i\), e.g. FNS scorepoints (10–70)
\(y_i\)liking measure, e.g. mean of novel products minus controlpoints
\(r\)Pearson correlation–
Assumptions and validity

A correlation in one study panel is not causation: neophobic students may also dislike rye bread. The covariate should be measured before tasting. Measurement error in the scale attenuates the correlation — see the neophobia-scales laboratory.

\(r\) is dimensionless; the slope is in liking points per covariate point ✓

Investigation tasks

  1. What is blocking worth?

    Simulate 24 panellists with a panellist SD of 0.3, 0.9 and 1.8 points (all else equal). For each, record the F tests of both ANOVAs and the efficiency of blocking. Explain the pattern with Eqs. H2–H3.

  2. How many consumers?

    With a true drop of 0.4 points per novelty step and an error SD of 1.1, resample the panel several times for b = 12, 24 and 48. How often is the 0 % – 10 % difference significant (Tukey)? Relate your answer to Eq. B7 of the sensory-booth laboratory.

  3. Segments or noise?

    Apply No product effect and set k = 2. The algorithm still finds two segments — how much variation do they “explain”? Now apply Love it or hate it. What distinguishes a real segment from an artefact? Which evidence would convince you?

  4. Neophobia and liking

    In the illustrative study, report r between FNS and the novel-minus-control liking with its confidence interval. How does the correlation change when you choose “mean liking” instead? Why is a difference score a better measure of attitude to the novel product than the raw rating?

  5. Ordinal data

    Compare the p-values of the block ANOVA and the Friedman test for several simulated panels, including one with a very high control mean (8.5). When do they disagree, and why? Look at the Scale use and Residuals views.

  6. Your study

    Load or paste your own ratings and write the results section: means with CIs, the block ANOVA, Tukey comparisons with differences in points and dz, and — clearly labelled as exploratory — any segments and covariate relations.

Model assumptions

  • Data: complete blocks only (every panellist rated every product); incomplete rows are dropped and counted. Ratings must be whole or decimal numbers from 1 to 9.
  • Block ANOVA without interaction (anovaRCBD), one-way ANOVA (anova1) and Tukey HSD from the block error term (tukeyRCBD) from the course statistics library; the one-way Tukey intervals shown for comparison use the one-way error mean square.
  • Relative efficiency with Fisher's degrees-of-freedom correction; Friedman test with average ranks and tie correction, χ² approximation.
  • k-means on raw or panellist-centred profiles, k-means++ initialisation, 24 restarts, deterministic seed; segments ordered by liking of the most novel product.
  • The example data are simulated (not measured): 36 panellists, three breads, product means falling with cricket flour, a panellist SD of 0.9 and an error SD of 1.0 points, and a hidden segment (about one third) that likes the cricket breads less and has higher FNS scores. The simulation model is Eq. H2 plus a segment term, rounded to the 9-point scale.
  • Project data are read from the browser (key ffp-sensory-v1, hedonic responses {panellist, sample, score}).
