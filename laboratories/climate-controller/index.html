A guided tour of the test day

The simulation starts automatically with a well-tuned PI controller at 120× speed: four simulated hours take two real minutes. The chamber begins the day at its night set-point of 16 °C with the lights off.

  1. The dawn step (10 min). The set-point jumps to 22 °C. Watch the heater coils on the back wall start to glow — they only become visibly red close to full power, because a coil at 50 % power stays below the ≈ 525 °C at which steel begins to glow. The air follows within minutes, but the dashed amber slow mass (panel skins, floor, nutrient tank) creeps upwards for hours and keeps soaking up heat.
  2. The humidifier. Warming air at constant water content means falling relative humidity (the right axis of the second chart). Below 67 % the humidifier switches on and a plume of mist rises beside the rack. Evaporating 0.4 kg of water per hour takes 270 W of sensible heat from the air: see the small dips in the temperature and the controller compensating.
  3. Lights on (60 min). 650 W of LEDs switch on. About 200 W leaves as water vapour (transpiration); the rest heats the air and the surfaces. The output crosses zero and the evaporator fans speed up; blue air streams leave the unit cooler above the rack.
  4. The corridor cools (100–110 min) from 20 to 10 °C. Thanks to 100 mm of insulation the chamber barely notices: the extra heat loss is only about 150 W.
  5. The door opens (150 min, 2 minutes). Warm chamber air flows out at the top of the doorway and cold corridor air in at the bottom: about 0.3 m³ s⁻¹, a 4–5 kW heat loss that saturates the heater. The temperature drops by several degrees within a minute — the hardest disturbance of the day.
  6. Switch to On/off (preset Out-of-the-box thermostat). The heater and the evaporator now fight: the dead time lets each stage overshoot into the other's band. Count the actuator starts and compare the electricity with the PI run. Then try the preset Wide dead band.
  7. Switch to MPC. The dashed magenta line is the controller's prediction over the next ten minutes, recomputed every 20 s. Notice that it starts heating before the dawn step and pre-cools before the lights switch on: it knows the schedule.
  8. Press Compare all five controllers and read the scatter plot. Then run the Step test and the Relay test to tune the PI and PID controllers the Ziegler–Nichols way.
Key idea

A controller is judged on several, conflicting criteria at once: how closely it tracks the set-point (IAE, overshoot, settling time), how well it rejects disturbances (door recovery), how hard it works the actuators (starts, noise) and how much electricity it uses. There is no single “best” controller, only the best compromise for your crop, your equipment and your electricity bill.

The chamber as a thermal circuit

A walk-in growth chamber is an insulated box of sandwich panels — two steel skins bonded to 100 mm of polyurethane foam — with an air-handling unit that heats, cools and circulates the air. To control its temperature we need a model that is simple enough to reason with but captures what matters for control: how quickly the air temperature responds, where heat is stored and which disturbances push it around.

The simulator uses a two-node resistance–capacitance (RC) model, the thermal counterpart of an electrical circuit (Figure 1). Temperatures play the role of voltages, heat flows of currents, thermal conductances \(H\) (W K⁻¹) of electrical conductances and heat capacities \(C\) (J K⁻¹) of capacitors:

  • The air node \(T_a\) lumps the 18 m³ of air (≈ 22 kJ K⁻¹) with the light, fast-responding internal mass that exchanges heat with it within seconds to minutes — shelves, trays, leaves and equipment housings (≈ 30 kJ K⁻¹). All the active equipment acts on this node.
  • The slow node \(T_m\) represents the heavy thermal mass: the inner steel skins of the panels, the surface layer of the floor slab and about 40 L of nutrient solution, some 1 MJ K⁻¹ in total. It exchanges heat with the air by convection (250 W K⁻¹) and loses heat through the insulation to the corridor (12 W K⁻¹).
  • The corridor temperature \(T_c\) is a boundary condition, a “voltage source”. Air infiltration (0.5 air changes per hour) connects it directly to the air node, and so does the open door.

The two nodes give the chamber two very different time constants. On its own the air node relaxes with \(\tau_a = C_a/(H_{am}+H_{\text{inf}}) \approx 52\,000/253 \approx 200\) s, a few minutes. The slow mass needs \(C_m/H_{\text{env}}\) — many hours — to settle, because the insulation is so good. For the controller this means: a heater pulse moves the air within minutes, but the mass keeps absorbing heat long after a set-point change, so a controller without integral action never quite gets there, and a fixed “manual reset” is right only for one moment of the day.

Delays, lags and why they limit the gain

Between the controller's decision and the measured response sit several delays (Figure 2). The heater element must warm up (time constant 20 s), the evaporator's refrigerant circuit is slower still (45 s), conditioned air needs time to mix through the room before it reaches the sensor (a dead time of about 20 s), and the sensor itself — a PT1000 in an aspirated radiation shield — responds with its own time constant of 20 s. Each lag and delay adds phase lag. When the total phase lag at some frequency reaches 180°, negative feedback turns into positive feedback: if the loop gain at that frequency exceeds one, oscillations grow. The gain at which this happens is the ultimate gain \(K_u\), and the period of the oscillation the ultimate period \(P_u\). Every tuning rule is, in one way or another, a safe distance from this limit.

Five ways to decide what to do

On/off with hysteresis switches full power on and off. It is cheap and robust but always produces a limit cycle, and with a heater and a cooler it can overshoot from one stage into the other: dead time is the enemy of narrow bands. Proportional (P) control makes the correction proportional to the error, but a steady load (the fans, the lamps, the slow mass) can only be balanced by a steady error — the offset. PI control adds the integral of the error, which grows until the offset has gone; anti-windup stops the integral from growing absurdly while the actuator is saturated (during the door opening). PID adds a derivative term that anticipates the trend, allowing a higher gain, but it differentiates the sensor noise, so it is filtered and it wears the actuators. Model predictive control (MPC) simulates the chamber over the next few minutes for many candidate input sequences, picks the cheapest one according to a cost that weighs tracking error, electricity and abrupt changes, applies only its first move and repeats 20 s later. Because it knows the lamp and set-point schedule, it acts before these disturbances arrive; a disturbance observer corrects its model for everything it cannot know, such as the door.

Ziegler and Nichols, 1942

Ziegler and Nichols proposed two experiments that are still taught and used. In the reaction-curve method you step the controller output in manual and read the steepest slope \(R\) and apparent dead time \(L\) of the response. In the ultimate-gain method you raise a P controller's gain until the loop oscillates steadily. Modern controllers find \(K_u\) and \(P_u\) safely with a relay test (Åström & Hägglund, 1984): the output is switched between two levels whenever the measurement crosses the set-point, which forces a small, controlled oscillation at the ultimate period. Both rule sets aim at a quarter-amplitude decay — aggressive by today's standards — so treat them as a starting point.

Frontier

Commercial greenhouse and vertical-farm climate computers still run mostly PI loops with set-point schedules, but predictive and economic controllers that use weather and electricity-price forecasts are entering practice, and reinforcement-learning controllers are being benchmarked against MPC (see Lesson 10.4). All of them depend on a good process model and good sensors — the theme of the lettuce digital-twin laboratory.

The equations behind the simulation

The plant model (Eqs. C1–C5) is integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method in 1-s steps. The PID-type controllers and the thermostat sample the sensor every 2 s; the MPC re-plans every 20 s. Follow the derivation
  1. Choose a control volume around the chamber air. The first law for an open system at constant pressure, with negligible kinetic and potential energy, says that the rate of change of stored enthalpy equals the heat flows in minus out plus the enthalpy carried in by entering air minus that carried out.
  2. The stored heat is \(C_aT_a\). Fast masses (shelves, leaves) that follow the air within seconds are added to \(C_a\); the slow ones get their own node (C2).
  3. Air entering at \(T_c\) and leaving at \(T_a\) with mass flow \(\dot m\) carries a net \(\dot m c_p(T_c-T_a)\); writing \(\dot m c_p = H\) gives the conductance form for infiltration and the door.
  4. The lamps' electrical power ends up as heat. The part that evaporates water in the leaves leaves the air as vapour, so only \(Q_{\text{LED,s}} = P_{\text{LED}}-L_vE\) is sensible heat; 60 % of it heats the air directly, the rest is absorbed by surfaces (C2).
  5. Mist from an ultrasonic humidifier evaporates in the air: the latent heat \(L_v\dot m_{\text{hum}}\) is taken from the air's sensible heat (adiabatic humidification).
  6. An evaporator spends part of its capacity condensing water; only \((1-\varphi_L)Q_c\) cools the air sensibly.
Assumptions and validity

The air is perfectly mixed (one temperature); in reality there are gradients of 0.5–1 K between the supply-air stream and the canopy. Heat capacities and conductances are constant. The latent heat of transpiration is taken from the lamps' power; plant-level energy balances show that under LEDs a large share of the absorbed radiation is indeed dissipated as latent heat (Graamans et al., 2017).

Follow the reasoning: two time scales
  1. With the heater off and \(T_c\) constant, Eqs. C1–C2 are two coupled linear ODEs. Their solution is a sum of two exponentials with time constants given by the eigenvalues of the system matrix.
  2. Because \(C_m \gg C_a\), the eigenvalues separate: a fast one \(\approx (H_{am}+H_{\text{inf}})/C_a\) (air settles against the momentarily fixed mass, \(\tau\approx 200\) s) and a slow one \(\approx (H_{\text{env}}+H_{am}H_{\text{inf}}/(H_{am}+H_{\text{inf}}))/C_m\) (everything drifts together towards the corridor, \(\tau\approx 19\) h).
  3. Consequence for control: after a set-point step, the air responds in minutes, but the slow mass keeps drawing \(H_{am}(T_a-T_m)\) — several hundred watts — for hours. A P controller's steady-state output, and therefore its offset, changes all day.

Follow the derivation (Brown & Solvason, 1962)
  1. Warm air is lighter. With the door closed, the pressure difference across it varies linearly with height; with a neutral plane at mid-height, at a distance \(s\) above or below it \(\Delta p(s) = \Delta\rho\,g\,s\).
  2. Bernoulli gives the local velocity \(v(s) = \sqrt{2\Delta p/\rho} = \sqrt{2g(\Delta\rho/\rho)s}\): warm air leaves through the upper half, cold air enters through the lower half.
  3. Integrate over the upper half with a discharge coefficient: \(\dot V = C_dW\int_0^{H/2}\sqrt{2g(\Delta\rho/\rho)s}\,\mathrm ds = C_dW\sqrt{2g\Delta\rho/\rho}\;\tfrac23\left(\tfrac H2\right)^{3/2} = \tfrac{C_d}{3}WH\sqrt{gH\,\Delta\rho/\rho}\).

A fully open door, no wind or fan-driven pressure differences, and no strip curtain. Refrigeration engineers use the same physics for cold-store doors; air curtains and strip curtains reduce the exchange by 60–90 %. The model ramps the flow on and off instantly and ignores the door's swing time.

Two-stage thermostat with hysteresis(C6)
\[ \text{heat}_k=\begin{cases}1 & y_k<r-h\\ 0 & y_k\ge r\\ \text{heat}_{k-1}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad \text{cool}_k=\begin{cases}1 & y_k>r+h\\ 0 & y_k\le r\\ \text{cool}_{k-1}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} \] \[ u_k = 100\,(\text{heat}_k-\text{cool}_k) \]

Follow the reasoning: why the stages fight
  1. After the heater switches off at \(r\), heat already “in the pipeline” (element lag, dead time, sensor lag) keeps raising the reading. The overshoot is roughly the heating rate times the total delay: at 2 kW on 52 kJ K⁻¹ the air warms at 2.3 K min⁻¹, so a 40-s delay gives ≈ 1.5 K.
  2. If this overshoot exceeds \(h\), the cooler switches on, overshoots downwards and triggers the heater: the stages hunt, wasting energy and wearing the compressor.
  3. Choosing \(h\) larger than the expected overshoot stops the fight but widens the temperature swing: with an oversized actuator, on/off control cannot be both tight and efficient.

Ziegler–Nichols rules and the relay test(C8)
\[ a=R\,L:\qquad K_c^{\text{PI}}=\frac{0.9}{a},\ T_i=\frac{L}{0.3};\qquad K_c^{\text{PID}}=\frac{1.2}{a},\ T_i=2L,\ T_d=0.5L \] \[ K_c^{\text{PI}}=0.45K_u,\ T_i=\frac{P_u}{1.2};\qquad K_c^{\text{PID}}=0.6K_u,\ T_i=\frac{P_u}{2},\ T_d=\frac{P_u}{8} \] \[ K_u\approx\frac{4d}{\pi\sqrt{a_r^2-\varepsilon^2}} \]

from a step test, multiply the steepest slope of the response per unit of output change by the apparent dead time and divide 0.9 (PI) or 1.2 (PID) by the product to get the gain. From an ultimate-gain experiment, take fixed fractions of the ultimate gain and period. A relay that switches the output by ±d produces an oscillation of amplitude \(a_r\), from which the ultimate gain follows.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(R\)steepest slope of the response divided by the output step Δu°C s⁻¹ %⁻¹
\(L\)apparent dead time: where the steepest tangent crosses the initial levels
\(K_u, P_u\)ultimate gain and period% °C⁻¹, s
\(d, a_r, \varepsilon\)relay amplitude; half peak-to-peak oscillation; relay hysteresis%, °C, °C
Follow the reasoning
  1. Reaction curve. Ziegler and Nichols (1942) characterised a process by the tangent at its inflection point: slope \(R\) (per unit input) and intercept delay \(L\). For an FOPDT process \(R = K/\tau\) and \(L=\theta\), so \(0.9/(RL) = 0.9\,\tau/(K\theta)\) — the form used in Lesson 10.4.
  2. Ultimate gain. At \(K_c = K_u\) the loop has a phase lag of 180° and a gain of 1 at \(\omega_u = 2\pi/P_u\). The rules place the gain at 45–60 % of this limit.
  3. Relay. A relay with amplitude \(d\) is a very nonlinear gain; its describing function (the ratio of the fundamental of its square-wave output, \(4d/\pi\), to the input amplitude) is \(4d/(\pi a_r)\). A stable oscillation occurs where this equals \(1/|G(j\omega_u)| = K_u\). Hysteresis \(\varepsilon\) (needed against noise) shifts the switching and gives the \(\sqrt{a_r^2-\varepsilon^2}\) correction.
  4. The laboratory also computes \(K_u\) of the linearised model from its frequency response, separately for heating and cooling. The relay's ultimate period lies between them because the relay exercises both actuators; its \(K_u\) comes out lower than both, a reminder that the describing function is only an approximation.
Assumptions and validity

The rules target quarter-amplitude decay and give aggressive, poorly damped loops with large noise sensitivity; SIMC (Skogestad, 2003) or model-based tuning is usually preferred. The describing-function estimate of \(K_u\) is typically within 10–20 % for processes with lags and dead time.

Model predictive control by brute force(C9)
\[ \begin{aligned} \min_{u_1,u_2,u_3\in\mathcal U}\ J ={}& \sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\Delta t_c}{3600}\bigl(\hat T_{a,k}-r_k\bigr)^2+w_E\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}E_{\text{el},k}\\ &+w_\Delta\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{u_k-u_{k-1}}{100}\right)^2 \end{aligned} \] \[ \hat T_{a,k}=T^{\text{free}}_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\left(u_j^+S^{h}_{j,k}+u_j^-S^{c}_{j,k}\right) \] \[ u_k=\begin{cases}u_1&k=0\\u_2&1\le k<b\\u_3&b\le k<N\end{cases},\qquad -100\le u_j\le 100 \]

choose three output levels — one for the next 20 s, one for the following block, one for the rest of the horizon — that minimise the sum of the squared predicted tracking errors, the weighted electricity and the weighted squared moves. The predicted temperature is the response with no further action plus each block's contribution, obtained from pre-computed unit responses of the heater and evaporator channels.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(N, \Delta t_c\)horizon (steps); controller step–; s30; 20 (10 min)
\(T^{\text{free}}_{k}\)prediction with zero further output, including commands already in the dead-time buffer, the lamp schedule, the measured corridor temperature and the estimated disturbance \(\hat d\)°C–
\(S^h_{j,k}, S^c_{j,k}\)response at step \(k\) to 1 % heating / cooling during block \(j\)°C %⁻¹–
\(w_E\)energy weight°C² h kWh⁻¹0.05
\(w_\Delta\)move-suppression weight–0.02
\(\mathcal U\)candidate grid: 17 levels + fine steps around the previous move for \(u_1\); 11 levels for \(u_2, u_3\)%≈ 3000 sequences
Follow the reasoning
  1. The internal model is the linear part of Eqs. C1, C2 and C5 (heater, evaporator with a nominal sensible share of 0.8, air, mass, sensor) discretised exactly with the matrix exponential on a 2-s grid: \(x_{i+1} = Ax_i + Bv_i\).
  2. Because the model is linear in each actuator channel, the prediction for any candidate is a superposition: one free response plus six unit responses (3 blocks × 2 channels). Evaluating a candidate then costs only \(N\) multiply–adds per block, so ≈ 3000 candidates are scored in about a millisecond.
  3. “Brute force” means that the cost of every candidate on the grid is computed and the minimum kept; a short local pattern search then refines the three levels to 1 % resolution. Move blocking (three levels instead of \(N\)) keeps the grid small.
  4. Only \(u_1\) is applied. Twenty seconds later the horizon slides forward and the optimisation is repeated with new measurements: the receding-horizon principle turns an open-loop plan into feedback (Mayne, 2014).
  5. The known future set-point and lamp schedule enter \(T^{\text{free}}\) and \(r_k\): this is why MPC starts heating before dawn and pre-cools before the lamps switch on.
Assumptions and validity

Industrial MPC solves a quadratic or nonlinear programme with many more free moves (Qin & Badgwell, 2003); brute force is used here because it is transparent and the input is one-dimensional. The internal model ignores humidity, the door and the RH-dependent latent share; the observer (C10) absorbs these errors. The “model error” slider scales its heat capacities to test robustness.

\(\Delta t_c(\hat T-r)^2/3600\): h × °C²; \(w_EE_{\text{el}}\): °C² h kWh⁻¹ × kWh = °C² h; the move term is dimensionless and weighted accordingly ✓
Disturbance observer for offset-free MPC(C10)
\[ \begin{bmatrix}\hat x\\ \hat d\end{bmatrix}_{k|k}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat x\\ \hat d\end{bmatrix}_{k|k-1}+K\bigl(y_k-\hat T_{s,k|k-1}\bigr) \] \[ \begin{bmatrix}\hat x\\ \hat d\end{bmatrix}_{k+1|k}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B_d\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat x\\ \hat d\end{bmatrix}_{k|k}+\begin{bmatrix}B_u\\0\end{bmatrix}v_k \]

every 2 s the model's estimate of air, mass and sensor temperature and of an unknown heat load is corrected in proportion to the difference between the measured and the predicted sensor reading; then the model is stepped forward with the known inputs. The unknown load is modelled as a slowly wandering constant.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\hat x = [\hat T_a,\hat T_m,\hat T_s]\)estimated temperatures°C
\(\hat d\)estimated unmeasured heat load on the air (door, humidifier, model error)W
\(K\)steady-state Kalman gain, from iterating the Riccati equation°C °C⁻¹, W °C⁻¹
Follow the reasoning
  1. A model is never perfect. If the MPC predicted with a biased model it would settle with an offset, like a P controller.
  2. Augmenting the state with a disturbance \(d\) that follows a random walk and estimating it from the innovations makes the prediction unbiased at steady state: this is the standard route to offset-free MPC.
  3. The gain \(K\) follows from assumed process-noise and measurement-noise covariances (Kalman, 1960); it is computed once when the controller is created by iterating \(P\leftarrow APA^\top+Q\), \(K = PC^\top/(CPC^\top+R)\), \(P\leftarrow(I-KC)P\) to convergence.
Assumptions and validity

Linear model, Gaussian noise, one lumped disturbance on the air node. The door's sudden loss appears in \(\hat d\) only after the sensor has seen it — no feedback controller can act before a disturbance is measured unless it is scheduled.

\(K\) maps °C of innovation to °C of state correction and to W of disturbance correction ✓
Performance metrics(C11)
\[ \text{IAE}=\frac{1}{3600}\int_0^{4\,\text{h}}\bigl|T_a(t)-r(t)\bigr|\,\mathrm dt,\qquad \text{OS}=\max_{10\le t<60\ \text{min}}\bigl(T_a-r\bigr) \] \[ t_s=\min\bigl\{t:\ |T_a-r|\le 0.3\ {}^{\circ}\text{C until } 60\ \text{min}\bigr\}-10\ \text{min} \]

the integral of absolute error adds up every degree-hour the true air temperature spends away from the set-point; the overshoot is the largest excursion above the new set-point after the dawn step; the settling time is how long after the step the air enters and stays within ±0.3 °C before the lights switch on.

Why several metrics?

IAE rewards fast, tight control; overshoot and settling describe the set-point response; the door metrics describe disturbance rejection; starts approximate actuator wear; electricity is the running cost. Optimising one usually worsens another — the scatter plot shows the trade-off between IAE and energy explicitly.

Model assumptions

  • The chamber (3.0 × 2.4 × 2.5 m, 100-mm polyurethane sandwich panels) is a representative design; heat capacities and conductances were estimated from its dimensions and typical material properties, not taken from a specific product. Its behaviour — air responding in minutes, surfaces in hours — matches what step tests on walk-in chambers show.
  • Air is perfectly mixed; vertical gradients and the jet from the unit cooler are ignored. The dead time lumps mixing and transport delays into one value.
  • The lamps' sensible heat is split 60/40 between air and surfaces; a fixed transpiration of 0.30 kg h⁻¹ (lights on) removes latent heat. Plant growth, CO₂ and stomatal responses are not modelled.
  • The evaporator's latent share depends only on relative humidity (Eq. C4), a simplification of coil physics; the heater and evaporator are modelled as first-order lags; compressor minimum run times, defrost cycles and refrigerant dynamics are ignored.
  • The door is either fully open or closed; the flow follows the buoyancy formula of Brown and Solvason (1962) without wind, pressure differences or curtains.
  • Sensor noise is Gaussian and white with a fixed seed, so every controller in a comparison sees the same noise sequence; the controllers see the sensor, the metrics use the true air temperature.
  • The MPC uses a linearised model (no humidity, nominal sensible share 0.8), move blocking with three levels and brute-force search; industrial MPC solves a constrained optimisation over more moves.
  • The 3D scene is illustrative; heater glow, fan speed, mist and door position are driven by the simulated heat flows and states.
