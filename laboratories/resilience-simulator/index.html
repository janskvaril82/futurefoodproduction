Food-system resilience simulator

A Swedish city eats fresh vegetables every week of the year: from Swedish fields in summer, from cold stores in winter, from greenhouses and vertical farms, and — above all — from importers in Spain, the Netherlands, Poland and beyond. Hit this network with droughts, energy crises, blocked trade routes, a pandemic, a cyber-attack or a storm, watch how deep and how long the shortfall becomes, and find out which strategy — diversity, stocks, local controlled-environment farms or backup power — actually buys resilience.

A guided tour of the network

The stage is the city's vegetable-supply network. On the left, four import origins feed the ports and border crossings; in the middle, Swedish fields, cold stores, greenhouses and vertical farms; on the right, the wholesale and distribution centres and the city. Node size follows the current flow, moving dots are the flows themselves, and the hexagons along the top are the drivers — weather, labour, energy and IT — on which production depends. When a shock strikes, its driver flashes red and the dependent nodes glow. The strip at the bottom is the two-year timeline: drag the white cursor to scrub through time, or drag a coloured shock bar to move the shock.

  1. Watch a normal year first. Choose the scenario No shocks and press Play. From late June the Swedish field node swells until it supplies more than half of the city's vegetables, and imports shrink to about 40 %. In September and October storage crops — roots, cabbage, onions — flow into the cold stores and are drawn down until June. In January about a quarter of supply is Swedish, almost all of it from cold stores, and the rest depends on a handful of importers — look at the diversity chart.
  2. Load the Perfect storm. A 2018-type summer drought halves the harvest in Sweden and northern Europe, then an energy crisis makes heating and lighting unprofitable, and in the second winter a blocked route cuts imports from Spain and Italy. Which shock produces the deepest triangle? Why does the drought, although severe, cost so little supply?
  3. Read the strategy chart. The bars rerun the same shocks with a lean baseline, with each strategy alone and with all combined. Diversification and a buffer stock cut the trade shock; backup power and non-fossil heat protect the greenhouses and vertical farms; more local production helps in summer but can add exposure to energy shocks.
  4. Push local CEA. Choose the strategy preset Local CEA push (30 % of demand from greenhouses and vertical farms, gas heating) and the scenario Energy crisis. The farms switch off within a week when the energy price passes their break-even, importers cannot re-source fast enough and the city loses supply. Now add backup and biofuel heat, or raise the energy-price headroom p*/p₀.
  5. Try the cyber-attack and the power cut. Vertical farms depend on climate-control software and on electricity every hour. A two-week IT outage or a three-day power cut destroys a crop that takes a cycle to regrow — watch the “run %” of the farms in the network. With the lean baseline the lost crop is so small that importers replace it within the week; with the Local CEA push preset the same shocks open short gaps. Which strategies help against these shocks — and which (diversified imports?) do not?
Key idea

Resilience is the capacity to keep supplying the city — not the survival of any one component. Every source fails for a different reason: fields to weather, greenhouses and vertical farms to energy prices and IT, imports to trade routes. The most resilient network is not the most local one but the one whose sources fail for different reasons, with a buffer that bridges the time until the others adapt.

What the simulator represents

The model follows the weekly supply of fresh vegetables to a Swedish city for two years, in percent of normal weekly demand. It is a stylised network: the shares, capacities and response times are chosen to reproduce the typical Swedish pattern — high self-sufficiency in summer, heavy dependence on imported fruit and vegetables in winter (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025) — not to describe a particular city.

Three kinds of response, three kinds of capacity

The model contains the three resilience capacities of Béné (2020). Absorptive capacity is the stock: seasonal cold storage and an optional emergency buffer, released when supply falls short. Adaptive capacity is re-sourcing: importers can buy more from other origins, but only up to their spare capacity and only as fast as new contracts and logistics can be arranged — the re-sourcing speed slider. The production decisions of controlled-environment farms are adaptive too, but in the other direction: when the energy price exceeds a farm's break-even price (Eq. 15.2.7 of Lesson 15.2) the farm stops lighting and heating, and restarting takes a crop cycle. Transformative capacity — changing the system itself — is what you do with the strategy controls.

Why energy crises hit indoor farms twice

An energy-price spike raises a vertical farm's costs in proportion to its electricity cost share. Zhuang et al. (2022) found that a 30 % fall in yield or in the price of lettuce could bankrupt plant factories; Lesson 15.2 showed that a farm with an energy safety factor of 3.4 falls to 1.1 after a 30 % price fall. In the simulator each farm has its own break-even price, spread around the mean p*/p₀ with a coefficient of variation of 25 %; the share of farms still producing is the probability that a farm's break-even exceeds the current price.

Diversity is not the same as independence

The Shannon index counts shares, not risks (Lesson 15.2, Worked example 2). In the simulator, the correlated 2018-type drought hits Swedish fields and northern European exporters at once, and a blocked route through southern Europe takes out one origin that the concentrated import strategy relies on for 70 % of its imports. Diversification helps exactly when the origins fail for different reasons.

Common misconception

“More local production always means more resilience.” Local controlled-environment farms remove the trade risk but add energy, electricity-grid and IT risk — and they cannot scale up quickly when imports fail. In the simulator, a large local CEA share with fossil heat and no backup power makes the city more vulnerable to an energy crisis than a lean import-based system.

Frontier · as of 2026

Sweden is rebuilding emergency food stocks: state-owned rotation stocks of grain in the four northernmost counties were in place from June 2026, and a law on emergency stocks in the food chain applies from 1 July 2026 (Jordbruksverket, 2026). Fresh vegetables cannot be stored for long, which is why their resilience depends on diversity, flexible trade and — for the storable part — cold stores.

The equations behind the simulator

Time runs in weeks, \(t = 0, 1, \dots, 103\); \(w = t \bmod 52\) is the week of the year (0 = first week of January). All flows are in percent of normal weekly demand \(D = 100\).

Seasonal field supply(R1)
\[ F(t) = F_{\text{pk}}\,h(w)\,\big(1-\delta_{\text{dr}}\big)\big(1-0.8\,\lambda\big), \qquad h(w) = \sin^2\!\left(\pi\,\frac{w-23}{21}\right)\ \text{for } 24 \le w \le 43,\ \text{else } 0 \]

field supply equals its summer peak share times the harvest profile of the week, reduced by the drought severity for the rest of the season and by the labour shortage, of which 80 % is felt in the harvest.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(F_{\text{pk}}\)field supply at the summer peak% of demand55
\(h(w)\)harvest profile, 0 outside weeks 24–43, 1 at the peak (week 34)––
\(\delta_{\text{dr}}\)drought severity, applied from the start of the drought to the end of the season–0–1
\(\lambda\)labour-shortage severity–0–1
Follow the derivation
  1. Swedish field vegetables are harvested from late June to mid-October; a smooth bump \(\sin^2\) that rises from zero and returns to zero reproduces this season.
  2. A drought damages the crop that is in the field: the lost yield is not recovered later in the same season, so the reduction applies until the season ends. The loss is a rectangle over the season, as in Lesson 15.2, Worked example 1.
  3. Harvest is labour-intensive (seasonal workers), so 80 % of a labour shortage reduces the harvest; greenhouses (40 %), vertical farms (20 %) and import logistics (15 %) are less exposed.
(% of demand) × (–) × (–) × (–) = % of demand ✓
Seasonal cold store: a mass balance(R2)
\[ S(t+1) = S(t) + H_s(t) - r(t), \qquad H_s = \sigma\,\frac{n_{\text{rel}}}{n_{\text{harv}}}\,(1-\delta_{\text{dr}})(1-0.8\,\lambda), \qquad r(t) = \frac{S(t)}{n_{\text{left}}(w)} \]

the stock next week is this week's stock plus the storage-crop harvest minus the release; the harvest is sized to supply the share σ of demand through the release season and is cut by drought and labour shortage like the fresh harvest; the planned release spreads the remaining stock evenly over the weeks left until early June.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(S\)stock in cold stores% of demand × week–
\(\sigma\)share of demand met from Swedish cold stores during the release season% of demand20
\(n_{\text{rel}}, n_{\text{harv}}\)release weeks (44–21) and harvest weeks (36–43)weeks30; 8
\(r\)planned release% of demand–
\(n_{\text{left}}\)weeks remaining until week 22weeks–
Follow the derivation
  1. Conservation of mass: stock changes by what goes in minus what goes out, \(\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = H_s - r\).
  2. To supply \(\sigma\) for \(n_{\text{rel}}\) weeks, \(\sigma n_{\text{rel}}\) %·weeks must be harvested in \(n_{\text{harv}}\) weeks: \(20\times30/8 = 75\) % of weekly demand per harvest week.
  3. A store manager who wants the stock to last until the new harvest divides the remaining stock by the remaining weeks. After a poor harvest this rule stretches the smaller stock thinner — and the summer drought reappears in winter as extra import need.
Assumptions and validity

No spoilage in store; stored vegetables substitute for fresh ones one-to-one in the supply count. In reality nutrition and consumer acceptance of stored roots differ from fresh leafy greens, and the seasonal store is not used as an emergency reserve in the model.

% of demand × week per week = % of demand ✓
Emergency buffer stock(R3)
\[ e(t) = \min\!\big(g(t),\,B(t),\,e_{\max}\big), \qquad B(t+1) = B(t) - e(t), \qquad \tau = \frac{B_0}{g} \]

the emergency release is the smallest of the supply gap, the remaining buffer and the maximum release rate; the buffer shrinks by what is released, so a buffer of B₀ lasts B₀ divided by the gap.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(g(t)\)gap between demand and all other supply% of demand–
\(B_0\)emergency stock held (weeks of demand × 100)% of demand × week0–600
\(e_{\max}\)maximum release rate (distribution capacity)% of demand25
\(\tau\)time until the buffer is empty (Lesson 15.2, Eq. 15.2.6)weeks–
Follow the derivation
  1. The buffer is a stock drained at the rate of the deficit it covers, the mass balance of Lesson 15.2: \(\mathrm{d}B/\mathrm{d}t = -g\).
  2. With a constant gap, \(\tau = B_0/g\): a 4-week stock (400 %·weeks) bridges a 40 % gap for 10 weeks.
  3. After the crisis, spare import capacity refills the buffer at up to 5 % of demand per week.
(% × week)/(%) = week ✓
Import capacity of the origins(R4)
\[ C_M(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{4} \pi_j\,M_{\max}\,\big(1-\delta_j(t)\big), \qquad M_{\max} = (1+h)\,\max_t M_{\text{sched}}(t) \]

import capacity is the sum over the four origins of each origin's share of the maximum import volume, reduced by the disruption hitting that origin; the maximum volume is the normal peak import need plus a headroom fraction.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(\pi_j\)share of imports from origin \(j\) (strategy)–0.70/0.20/0.05/0.05
\(\delta_j(t)\)disruption of origin \(j\) (trade shock; drought in northern Europe)–0–1
\(h\)spare capacity above the normal peak–0.15
\(M_{\text{sched}}(t)\)contracted imports: the import need of the same network without shocks% of demand–
Follow the derivation
  1. Each origin can deliver at most its share of the network's maximum import volume.
  2. A shock removes a fraction \(\delta_j\) of that origin's capacity; the first-order loss of imports is \(\sum_j\pi_j\delta_j\) — the exposure of Lesson 15.2, Eq. 15.2.9.
  3. Example: concentrated imports (70 % from Spain & Italy) and an 80 % blockade of that route remove \(0.7\times0.8 = 56\) % of import capacity; with diversified imports (30 %) only 24 %.
(–) × (% of demand) × (–) = % of demand ✓
Re-sourcing: how fast imports adapt(R5)
\[ M(t) = \min\!\Big(C_M(t),\; M_{\text{sched}}(t) + X(t)\Big), \qquad X(t) = \min\!\big(N(t) - M_{\text{sched}}(t),\; X(t-1) + \rho\big) \]

imports equal the smaller of the capacity and the contracted volume plus any extra purchases; the extra purchases can grow by at most the re-sourcing speed ρ each week, up to the extra need.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(N(t)\)import need: demand minus domestic supply% of demand–
\(X(t)\)extra imports above the contracted volume% of demand–
\(\rho\)re-sourcing speed (adaptive capacity)% of demand per week10
Follow the derivation
  1. In normal times importers deliver the contracted, seasonally planned volume \(M_{\text{sched}}\).
  2. When domestic supply falls unexpectedly, extra volume must be found, contracted and transported. A rate limit \(\rho\) makes the shortfall decay linearly — the rising edge of a resilience triangle (Lesson 15.2, Eq. 15.2.2) with recovery time \(\Delta Q/\rho\).
  3. No amount of re-sourcing can exceed the remaining capacity \(C_M\): if the gap is larger than the spare capacity, the shortfall persists until the shock ends.
% of demand + (% of demand per week) × week = % of demand ✓
Energy prices and the production decision of CEA farms(R6)
\[ u^{*}(t) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{m(t) - \bar F}{0.25\,\bar F}\right), \qquad m_{\text{VF}} = 1 + (m-1)(1-0.3\,b), \qquad m_{\text{GH}} = \begin{cases} m & \text{gas heat}\\ 1 + 0.4\,(m-1) & \text{bio heat}\end{cases} \]

the target share of farms that keep producing equals the probability that a farm's break-even energy-price multiplier exceeds the current multiplier, when break-even multipliers are normally distributed around their mean with a coefficient of variation of 25 %; vertical farms with on-site generation and greenhouses with non-fossil heat feel a smaller multiplier.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(m(t)\)energy-price multiplier, \(1 + 4\times\) shock severity–1
\(\bar F\)mean energy-price headroom \(p^{*}/p_0\) of the farms (Lesson 15.2, Eq. 15.2.7)–2.5
\(\Phi\)standard normal cumulative distribution function––
\(b\)share of CEA with backup power and on-site generation–0
\(u^{*}\)target operating level (share of capacity in production)–0–1
Follow the derivation
  1. A farm makes a loss on every kilogram when the energy price exceeds its break-even \(p^{*} = (P_{\text{sale}} - C_0)/e_{\text{el}}\) (Lesson 15.2). In multiples of the normal price, the condition is \(m > F_i = p^{*}_i/p_0\).
  2. Farms differ in contracts, efficiency and margins. If \(F_i\sim N(\bar F, (0.25\bar F)^2)\), the share with \(F_i > m\) is \(1-\Phi((m-\bar F)/(0.25\bar F))\).
  3. Example: \(\bar F = 2.5\) and a price tripling (\(m = 3\)): \(z = 0.5/0.625 = 0.8\), \(u^{*} = 1-\Phi(0.8) = 0.21\) — four in five farms stop.
  4. On-site generation fixes the price of 30 % of a vertical farm's electricity; a biofuel-heated greenhouse is exposed only through its electricity, about 40 % of its energy bill (model assumption).
Assumptions and validity

Short-run decision: farms with a loss switch off rather than go bankrupt, and restart when prices fall. In reality long crises also cause bankruptcies, after which capacity does not return — a transformation, not a recovery.

\(m\) and \(\bar F\) are ratios of prices: dimensionless ✓
Switching off fast, regrowing slowly(R7)
\[ u(t+1) = u(t) + \big(u^{*}(t) - u(t)\big)\left(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \begin{cases} 1\ \text{week} & u^{*} < u\\ 5\ \text{weeks} & u^{*} \ge u \end{cases} \]

the operating level of the farms moves toward its target each week by a fraction set by a time constant: one week when farms switch off, five weeks — about one lettuce crop cycle — when they restart.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(u(t)\)operating level of greenhouses or vertical farms–
\(u^{*}(t)\)target from Eq. R6, reduced by cyber-attacks (vertical farms fully, greenhouses by half)–
\(\tau\)time constantweeks
Follow the derivation
  1. First-order relaxation \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = (u^{*}-u)/\tau\) has the exact weekly solution above.
  2. Lights can be switched off in a day, but a crop that was lost must be sown and grown again: lettuce takes four to six weeks, hence \(\tau = 5\) weeks for recovery.
  3. A power cut of two days or more without backup kills the standing crop: \(u\) drops at once by the affected share and then regrows with \(\tau = 5\) weeks.
\(\Delta t/\tau\) is weeks per week: dimensionless ✓
Supply, shortfall and the resilience triangle(R8)
\[ Q(t) = \min\!\Big(100,\ F + r + G + V + M + e\Big), \qquad L = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big(100 - Q(t)\big)\Delta t, \qquad \Phi = 1 - \frac{L}{100\,T} \]

supply to consumers is the sum of all sources, capped at normal demand; the resilience loss is the summed shortfall over the two years; the resilience index is one minus that loss divided by the largest possible loss.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(F, r, G, V, M, e\)fresh field supply, store release, greenhouses, vertical farms, imports, emergency stock% of demand
\(L\)resilience loss (Lesson 15.2, Eq. 15.2.1a)%·weeks
\(\Phi\)resilience index over \(T = 104\) weeks (Eq. 15.2.1b)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Weekly steps turn the integral \(\int(100-Q)\,\mathrm{d}t\) into a sum with \(\Delta t = 1\) week.
  2. A drop of \(\Delta Q\) that is closed linearly by re-sourcing at speed \(\rho\) lasts \(T_r = \Delta Q/\rho\) weeks and costs \(L = \tfrac12\Delta Q\,T_r = \Delta Q^2/(2\rho)\): halving the depth quarters the loss, doubling the re-sourcing speed halves it.
  3. The readout “longest shortfall episode” is the longest run of consecutive weeks with a shortfall above 0.5 %.
% × week = %·week; \(\Phi\) dimensionless ✓
Diversity, concentration and self-sufficiency(R9)
\[ H = -\sum_{i} p_i \ln p_i, \quad D_1 = e^{H}, \quad \text{HHI} = \sum_i p_i^2, \quad \text{SSR} = \frac{F + r + G + V}{\sum \text{supply}}\times 100\,\% \]

the Shannon index is minus the sum of each source's share times its logarithm, and e to its power is the effective number of equally important sources; the Herfindahl–Hirschman index is the sum of squared shares; self-sufficiency is the domestic share of what consumers receive.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator; save each configuration with Save investigation (the page link also stores your shocks).

  1. The shape of a shock

    Run each single-shock scenario (drought, energy crisis, blocked route, pandemic, cyber-attack, power cut) with the lean baseline. For each, note the depth ΔQ, the longest shortfall episode and L. Which shocks make triangles, which rectangles, and which do almost nothing? Explain each shape with the equations R1–R7.

  2. What does a buffer buy?

    With the scenario Blocked trade route, increase the emergency buffer from 0 to 6 weeks in steps of one. Plot L against buffer size. Where do the returns diminish, and why (Eq. R3: think of the release rate and the length of the disruption)?

  3. Local production and energy risk

    For the scenario Energy crisis, vary the local CEA share from 0 to 40 % with gas heating, then with biofuel heat and 100 % backup. Is there a CEA share above which the network becomes less resilient? How does the energy-price headroom p*/p₀ change your answer?

  4. Diversity versus correlation

    Compare concentrated and diversified imports for the Blocked trade route (Spain & Italy) and for a trade disruption of all origins (add it yourself). Compute the first-order exposure Σπⱼδⱼ for both cases (Eq. R4) and compare it with the simulated depth. When does diversification not help?

  5. Design a resilient city

    Under the Perfect storm, find the cheapest combination of strategies (fewest weeks of buffer, lowest CEA share) that keeps supply above 90 % in every week. Record SSR, e^H and HHI of your design and argue whether it would also be robust to a shock you did not test.

  6. Your own shock

    Build a scenario from a real event — the 2021 Suez blockage (six days), the 2026 Strait of Hormuz fertiliser shock, or a winter storm cutting power to the Mälardalen region. Justify the start, duration and severity you chose and report which strategy helped most.

Model assumptions

  • Stylised network. Demand is constant at 100 % of a normal week. Default shares are chosen to reproduce the Swedish pattern of high summer and low winter self-sufficiency in fresh vegetables (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025); they are not calibrated to a particular city. Fresh, stored, greenhouse, vertical-farm and imported vegetables substitute for each other one-to-one.
  • Field and cold stores: fresh harvest in weeks 24–43 with a sin² profile; storage crops harvested in weeks 36–43 and released evenly until week 22. A drought cuts both from its start until the end of that year's season; a correlated drought also reduces the northern-European origin by half its severity for its duration plus four weeks.
  • Greenhouses produce 60 % of their summer output in midwinter (light-limited) with an annual mean equal to their share; vertical farms produce constantly.
  • Energy response: price multiplier 1 + 4 × severity; break-even multipliers of farms normally distributed (mean p*/p₀, CV 25 %); switching off with a 1-week and regrowth with a 5-week time constant. On-site generation fixes 30 % of the electricity price for farms with backup; biofuel-heated greenhouses are exposed only through electricity (40 % of their energy bill).
  • Cyber-attack: vertical farms lose operating capacity equal to the severity, greenhouses half of it; recovery through regrowth. Power cut: 2 days or more without backup kills the standing crop in the affected share of vertical farms (and 60 % of it in greenhouses).
  • Imports: origin shares from the diversification setting; capacity = (1 + spare) × the normal peak import need; contracted volumes follow the no-shock run; extra volumes grow by at most the re-sourcing speed per week. Trade disruptions recover linearly over three weeks after they end; labour shortages reduce import logistics by 15 % of their severity.
  • Emergency stock: released at up to 25 % of demand per week; refilled at up to 5 % per week from spare import capacity.
  • Not modelled: prices and affordability (who can still buy food — Lesson 15.3), nutrition and acceptance of substitutes, spoilage, bankruptcies and permanent loss of capacity, and feedbacks from the city's demand to prices.
