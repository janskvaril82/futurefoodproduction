Microalgae photobioreactor

For microalgae, light is the food. A culture of spirulina, Chlorella or Nannochloropsis can double in a day — but only the cells near the lit surface do the work, because every cell shades the ones behind it. Grow them in an open raceway pond, a flat-panel airlift reactor and a tubular photobioreactor under the real sun of Almería, Västerås or Hawaiʻi; watch the light die away inside the culture, find the density and harvest rate that give the most protein per square metre, and pay the energy bill for keeping it all moving.

A guided tour

  1. Press ▶ Run with the default spirulina raceway (Almería, 21 June, batch). The sun rises in the east; the culture, pale blue-green and translucent at first, darkens day by day as the biomass grows. Notice that the biomass falls slightly every night: the cells respire in the dark.
  2. Look at the cross-section board beside the pond and at the first chart. On day 1 light still reaches the bottom; by day 5 only the top few centimetres are bright and the rest of the pond is dark. The readout Culture volume growing tells you how much of the pond is still above the compensation point.
  3. Switch to continuous operation and set the dilution rate to the optimum marked in the second chart. The culture now settles at the density that uses the light best — thin enough that most cells see some light, dense enough that almost no light reaches the bottom unused.
  4. Choose the flat panel (or the Nannochloropsis panels preset). Rows of 1.5 m tall panels spread the same sunlight over up to five times more culture surface. Compare the efficiency curves in the last chart: diluted light is used more efficiently because the cells are not saturated.
  5. Choose the tubular reactor. The culture circulates through glass tubes and returns through a degasser column. Watch the HUD: the dissolved oxygen produced by photosynthesis builds up along the tubes — the reason why tubes cannot be made arbitrarily long.
  6. Try the Winter in Västerås preset. In January the sun barely clears the horizon and the pond is cold: growth almost stops. Compare with 21 June in Västerås, when the long Nordic day delivers more photons than Almería does in winter.
Key idea

A photobioreactor is a light-harvesting machine. Sunlight per square metre of ground is fixed by the site and season; the engineer decides how it is distributed over the culture (depth, thickness, spacing) and how dense the culture is kept (harvest rate). Productivity per m² is limited by photosynthetic efficiency, not by the volume of the reactor.

Light is the substrate

In the bioreactor laboratory the substrate was sugar, dissolved in the broth and available to every cell. Here it is light, and light is delivered only through the surface. As it travels into the culture it is absorbed by chlorophyll (and, in spirulina, by the blue pigment phycocyanin) and scattered by the cells, so it fades roughly exponentially with depth — Beer–Lambert's law (Eq. P1). A culture of 0.5 g L⁻¹ with a typical attenuation coefficient of 0.2 m² g⁻¹ absorbs 63 % of the light in the first centimetre and more than 99 % within 5 cm.

From local growth to reactor growth

Each cell's growth rate depends on the light it receives: proportional at low light (slope \(\alpha\)), saturating near \(\mu_{\max}\) and, for most strains, declining again above an optimum \(I_{\text{opt}}\) (photoinhibition; Bernard & Rémond, 2012). Because the culture is well mixed, the reactor grows at the depth-averaged rate \(\bar\mu\), multiplied by a temperature factor and minus the respiration that all cells pay day and night. Two consequences follow directly:

  • There is an optimal density. A thin culture lets light pass through unused; a thick culture wastes most of its volume respiring in the dark. The best continuous operation keeps the culture just dense enough to absorb nearly all light (Eq. P7).
  • Areal productivity hardly depends on depth. A pond twice as deep holds twice as much culture but receives the same photons, so at its optimum it produces about the same grams per m² per day — at half the concentration, which makes harvesting more expensive.

Light dilution and photosynthetic efficiency

Photosynthesis can store at most about 4.6 % of the total solar energy in C₃ biomass — roughly a tenth of the photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) — once all unavoidable losses are counted (Zhu et al., 2008). Real cultures reach much less, and the main reason is light saturation: at full sun (up to 2000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) the surface cells receive far more photons than their photosynthetic machinery can process, and the excess is dissipated as heat. Vertical panels and closely spaced tubes spread the same photons over more culture surface, so each cell works in the efficient, light-limited range. The efficiency chart shows this for your strain: efficiency falls as light increases, and the panel curve lies above the raceway curve when rows are close together.

Common misconception

"A closed photobioreactor produces more because it is more intensive." Per litre it does — a thin panel can be ten times more productive per litre than a pond. But per square metre of ground, every system receives the same sunlight; the closed systems win only through better light distribution, temperature control and freedom from contamination, and pay for it in steel, glass and pumping energy (Wijffels & Barbosa, 2010).

Temperature, oxygen and energy

Each strain has a temperature window: spirulina grows best at 35–37 °C (Vonshak, 1997) and stops at low temperature, Nannochloropsis prefers the mid-twenties and tolerates little above 32 °C (Weise et al., 2020). Photosynthesis releases about 1.9 g of O₂ for every gram of biomass formed; in a pond it escapes through the surface, but in a closed tube it accumulates until the degasser, and oxygen far above air saturation inhibits growth — a design limit for tube length (Molina et al., 2001; Chisti, 2007). Finally, every system needs mixing energy: a paddlewheel needs well under 1 W per m² of pond, a tubular reactor about ten times more and an intensively aerated panel even more (Eq. P11) — to be compared with the roughly 5.6 kWh of chemical energy in each kilogram of algal biomass.

Worked example 1How deep does the light reach?

A spirulina pond holds 0.5 g L⁻¹ of biomass (\(k_a = 0.20\) m² g⁻¹). At noon the surface receives 2000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. (a) At what depth is the light down to 90 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, where spirulina is about half-saturated? (b) What fraction of the surface light reaches the bottom of a 20 cm deep pond?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Attenuation coefficient. \(k = k_aX = 0.20\ \text{m}^2\text{g}^{-1}\times500\ \text{g m}^{-3} = 100\ \text{m}^{-1}\) (0.5 g L⁻¹ = 500 g m⁻³).
  2. (a) From \(I = I_0e^{-kz}\): \(z = \ln(I_0/I)/k = \ln(2000/90)/100 = 3.10/100 = 0.031\) m — about 3 cm.
  3. (b) \(I(0.2)/I_0 = e^{-100\times0.2} = e^{-20} = 2\times10^{-9}\): effectively nothing. Below about 5 cm the pond is dark even at noon.
(a) ≈ 3.1 cm; (b) ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁹ — the lower 15 cm of the pond is permanently dark at this density.

Check it in the simulator: set constant light 2000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and read the light profile when X ≈ 0.5 g L⁻¹.

Worked example 2How long can a tube be?

A Chlorella culture of 2 g L⁻¹ flows at 0.5 m s⁻¹ through 5 cm glass tubes and grows (gross) at 1.5 d⁻¹ around noon. It enters the tubes saturated with air (8.3 mg L⁻¹ O₂ at 25 °C). Dissolved oxygen should stay below about 400 % of air saturation (Chisti, 2007). What is the longest tube run?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. O₂ production rate (Eq. P12). \(r_{O_2} = Y_{O_2/X}\,\mu X = 1.886\times1.5\ \text{d}^{-1}\times2000\ \text{g m}^{-3} = 5658\) g m⁻³ d⁻¹ = 0.0655 g m⁻³ s⁻¹.
  2. Allowed increase. From 100 % to 400 % of saturation: \(\Delta C = 3\times8.3 = 24.9\) g m⁻³.
  3. Residence time and length. \(t = \Delta C/r_{O_2} = 24.9/0.0655 = 380\) s; \(L = u\,t = 0.5\times380 = 190\) m.
About 190 m at 0.5 m s⁻¹ (95 m at 0.25 m s⁻¹): denser, faster-growing cultures need shorter loops or faster flow.

Faster flow shortens the residence time but the pumping power grows with \(u^{2.75}\) (Eq. P11) — a classic engineering trade-off.

The equations behind the simulation

The simulator integrates one state variable — the biomass concentration \(X\) (g L⁻¹) — with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method and a 6-minute time step, and evaluates the depth-averaged growth rate in the geometry of the chosen reactor at every step. Time is in days.

Light attenuation (Beer–Lambert)(P1)
\[ I(z) = I_0\,e^{-k_aXz},\qquad \tau = k_aXL \]

the photon flux falls exponentially with depth; the product of the specific attenuation coefficient, the biomass concentration and the light path is the optical depth of the culture.

\(I_0\)photon flux density (PAR) at the culture surfaceµmol m⁻² s⁻¹0–2000
\(k_a\)biomass-specific attenuation coefficientm² g⁻¹0.15 (Chlorella), 0.225 (spirulina), 0.18 (Nannochloropsis)
\(X\)biomass concentration (1 g L⁻¹ = 1000 g m⁻³)g m⁻³—
\(z, L\)distance into the culture; light pathm—
\(\tau\)optical depth of the whole light path–—
Follow the derivation
  1. In a thin layer \(\mathrm{d}z\), the cells present (\(X\,\mathrm{d}z\) grams per m²) remove a fixed fraction of the photons passing through: \(\mathrm{d}I = -k_aX\,I\,\mathrm{d}z\).
  2. Separate variables and integrate from the surface: \(\ln(I/I_0) = -k_aXz\), hence \(I = I_0e^{-k_aXz}\).
  3. After one optical depth (\(k_aXz = 1\)) 37 % of the light remains, after 4.6 optical depths 1 %.
Assumptions and validity

Collimated light entering normal to the surface, pigment absorption averaged over the PAR spectrum, and scattering lumped into \(k_a\). Rigorous models solve the radiative transfer equation with separate absorption and scattering coefficients (e.g. Farges et al., 2009, report a mass absorption coefficient of about 0.2 m² g⁻¹ for spirulina). Real \(k_a\) values range from about 0.1 to 0.3 m² g⁻¹ and increase when cells acclimate to low light by making more chlorophyll (Schediwy et al., 2019). Background absorption by water is neglected.

\([k_aXz] = \text{m}^2\,\text{g}^{-1}\cdot\text{g m}^{-3}\cdot\text{m} = 1\) — dimensionless exponent ✓
Growth–irradiance response with photoinhibition(P2)
\[ \mu(I) = \frac{\mu_{\max}\,I}{I + \dfrac{\mu_{\max}}{\alpha}\left(\dfrac{I}{I_{\text{opt}}} - 1\right)^{2}} \]

at low light the growth rate rises in proportion to light with slope α; it reaches its maximum at the optimal irradiance and declines again at higher light because of photoinhibition.

SymbolMeaningUnitValues used (Chlorella · spirulina · Nannochloropsis)
\(\mu_{\max}\)maximum specific growth rate (at \(I_{\text{opt}}\), \(T_{\text{opt}}\))d⁻¹2.0 · 2.6 · 1.56
\(\alpha\)initial sloped⁻¹ (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹)⁻¹0.013 · 0.0288 · 0.0124
\(I_{\text{opt}}\)irradiance of maximal growthµmol m⁻² s⁻¹600 · 2000 · 500
\(I_k = \mu_{\max}/\alpha\)light-saturation parameterµmol m⁻² s⁻¹154 · 90 · 126
Follow the derivation
  1. Bernard and Rémond (2012) chose this form (after Peeters and Eilers) because its three parameters have a direct meaning: for \(I \to 0\) the denominator tends to \(\mu_{\max}/\alpha\), so \(\mu \approx \alpha I\); at \(I = I_{\text{opt}}\) the bracket vanishes and \(\mu = \mu_{\max}\).
  2. For \(I_{\text{opt}} \to \infty\) it becomes the hyperbola \(\mu = \mu_{\max}I/(I + \mu_{\max}/\alpha)\), the form used by Farges et al. (2009) for spirulina, which shows no photoinhibition up to full sunlight. We therefore use \(I_{\text{opt}} = 2000\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and their local kinetic constants: \(\alpha = \rho_M\phi E_a\) (0.8 × 1.85 × 10⁻⁹ kg µmol⁻¹ × 225 m² kg⁻¹ → 0.0288 d⁻¹ per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and saturation near \(\alpha K = 2.6\) d⁻¹ with \(K = 90\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  3. The slope \(\alpha\) sets the yield on light in the light-limited zone: \(Y = \alpha/(0.0864\,k_a)\) g per mol of photons, 0.8–1.5 g mol⁻¹ here. For spirulina it equals the quantum yield of Farges et al. (2009), \(\rho_M\phi\) = 1.48 g per mol of absorbed photons, close to the theoretical maximum of about 1.5–1.8 g mol⁻¹; measured values for green algae in photobioreactors are 0.75–0.78 g mol⁻¹ (Zijffers et al., 2010).
Assumptions and validity

A static response: real cells photoacclimate over days (changing \(\alpha\), \(k_a\) and \(I_{\text{opt}}\)) and respond to fast light/dark cycles caused by mixing. Except for spirulina (Farges et al., 2009) and \(\mu_{\max}\) of Nannochloropsis (Weise et al., 2020), the light parameters are representative values within the ranges compiled by Schediwy et al. (2019) — change them with the physiology sliders.

\(\dfrac{\text{d}^{-1}\cdot\mu\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}}{\mu\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} + (\text{d}^{-1}/[\text{d}^{-1}(\mu\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1})^{-1}])\cdot 1} = \text{d}^{-1}\) ✓
Temperature factor (cardinal temperature model with inflexion)(P3)
\[ \varphi(T) = \frac{(T - T_{\max})(T - T_{\min})^2}{(T_{\text{opt}} - T_{\min})\left[(T_{\text{opt}} - T_{\min})(T - T_{\text{opt}}) - (T_{\text{opt}} - T_{\max})(T_{\text{opt}} + T_{\min} - 2T)\right]} \]

a dimensionless factor that is one at the optimum temperature, rises from zero at the minimum and falls steeply to zero at the maximum temperature.

Follow the derivation

  2. At \(T = T_{\text{opt}}\) the numerator and denominator are both \((T_{\text{opt}} - T_{\max})(T_{\text{opt}} - T_{\min})^2\), so \(\varphi = 1\).
  3. The growth rate is multiplied by \(\varphi(T)\) (Eq. P4); the same multiplicative structure is used by Weise et al. (2020).
Assumptions and validity

Nannochloropsis values were fitted by Weise et al. (2020) for N. granulata (Bernard & Rémond, 2012, report −0.2, 26.7 and 33.3 °C for N. oceanica). For spirulina only the optimum is firmly established (35–37 °C; Vonshak, 1997) and for C. vulgaris reported optima range from 23 °C (Serra-Maia et al., 2016) to about 30 °C, so the minimum and maximum temperatures of these two strains are approximate. The culture temperature is a fixed setting here; real ponds follow the weather.

Depth-averaged growth rate in a pond(P4)
\[ \bar\mu_{\text{net}} = \frac{\varphi(T)}{L}\int_0^{L}\mu\!\left(I_0e^{-k_aXz}\right)\mathrm{d}z \;-\; r \;=\; \frac{\varphi(T)}{\tau}\int_0^{\tau}\mu\!\left(I_0e^{-\tau'}\right)\mathrm{d}\tau' \;-\; r \]

because mixing moves every cell through all depths, the culture grows at the average of the local growth rates over the light path, corrected for temperature, minus the respiration that every cell pays.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(\bar\mu_{\text{net}}\)net specific growth rate of the whole cultured⁻¹—
\(r\)respiration (maintenance) rate, day and nightd⁻¹0.1
\(\tau'\)optical depth variable \(k_aXz\)–—
Follow the derivation
  1. A thin layer at depth \(z\) produces \(\mu(I(z))\,X\,\mathrm{d}z\) grams per m² per day. Summing over the light path and dividing by the biomass per m² (\(XL\)) gives the average specific rate.
  2. Substituting \(\tau' = k_aXz\) (\(\mathrm{d}z = \mathrm{d}\tau'/k_aX\)) shows that \(\bar\mu\) depends only on \(I_0\) and the optical depth \(\tau\): doubling \(X\) has the same effect as doubling the depth.
  3. Numerically, the integral is evaluated with Simpson's rule on a grid that is dense near the lit surface (where the integrand changes fastest).
  4. In a dense culture (\(\tau \gg 1\)) nearly all light is absorbed and the areal growth \(\bar\mu XL \approx (1/k_a)\int\mu\,\mathrm{d}\tau'\) no longer depends on depth — the reason why areal productivity is set by light, not by volume.
Assumptions and validity

Mixing is fast compared with growth but slow compared with photosynthetic adaptation, so each cell responds to its instantaneous light (no "flashing-light" benefit). Respiration is constant and temperature-independent here; Zijffers et al. (2010) measured a maintenance requirement equivalent to about 0.12 d⁻¹ for Chlorella sorokiniana.

Flat panels: two-sided light and light dilution(P5)
\[ I_f = I_g\,\min\!\left(\frac{s}{2H},\ \frac12\right),\qquad I(z) = I_f\left(e^{-k_aXz} + e^{-k_aX(w - z)}\right),\qquad \frac{V}{A} = \frac{wH}{s} \]

the sunlight falling on the ground between two rows is shared by the two faces of the panels; inside a panel the light arriving from both faces adds up.

Follow the derivation
  1. Energy conservation: if rows are close enough to intercept all sunlight, the photons falling on a ground strip of width \(s\) end up on two faces of height \(H\): \(2HI_f = sI_g\).
  2. When rows are far apart (\(s > H\)) part of the light reaches the ground; a vertical face then receives at most about half of the horizontal irradiance on average, hence the cap of ½.
  3. The two exponentials are the light entering from the front and from the back face.

Biomass balance, batch and continuous(P7)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = \left(\bar\mu_{\text{net}}(X, I_0, T) - D\right)X,\qquad \text{steady state:}\ \ D = \bar\mu_{\text{net}}(\bar X) \]

biomass grows at the net depth-averaged rate and is removed by harvesting at the dilution rate; in steady continuous culture the culture settles at the density whose net growth rate equals the dilution rate.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(D\)dilution rate (0 in batch)d⁻¹
\(\bar X\)steady-state biomassg L⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Mass balance of a well-mixed culture with harvest \(F\) and an equal flow of fresh, cell-free medium: \(V\,\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = \bar\mu_{\text{net}}XV - FX\); divide by \(V\), with \(D = F/V\).
  2. Unlike the Monod chemostat of the bioreactor lab, the limiting "substrate" is light, which is not diluted: \(\bar\mu_{\text{net}}\) falls as \(X\) rises because of self-shading. Setting \(\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = 0\) gives the steady state.
  3. In batch (\(D = 0\)) the culture grows until self-shading makes \(\bar\mu_{\text{net}} = 0\): a light-limited stationary phase.
Assumptions and validity

Nutrients and CO₂ in excess (pH control), no grazers or contaminants, no cell death other than respiration losses.

  2. Summing over the day gives the daily PAR integral: about 64 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ in Almería and 62 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ in Västerås on 21 June, but only about 3 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ in Västerås in mid-January (clear sky).
Assumptions and validity

No clouds, a fixed atmospheric transmittance and horizontal irradiance used for all systems (the panel model redistributes it, Eq. P5). Real average days deliver less than clear-sky days.

Mixing and pumping power(P11)
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{pond:}&\quad \frac{P}{A} = \frac{\rho g\,d\,n^2u^3}{R_h^{4/3}}\cdot\frac{2}{\eta_w}, \qquad \text{tube:}\quad \frac{P}{V} = \frac{f\rho u^3}{2D_t}\cdot\frac{1.5}{\eta_p},\quad f = 0.316\,Re^{-1/4} \\ \text{panel (airlift):}&\quad \frac{P}{V} = \frac{\rho g\,U_G}{\eta_b},\qquad U_G = \frac{\text{vvm}\cdot H}{60} \end{aligned} \]

a pond loses energy to friction on the channel walls (Manning's law), a tube to wall friction (Darcy–Weisbach with the Blasius friction factor), and a bubble column spends the work needed to push air into the bottom of the liquid.

Follow the derivation
  1. Pond. Manning: head loss per metre of channel \(S = n^2u^2/R_h^{4/3}\). Power = \(\rho gQ\,S\,L_c\) with \(Q = uwd\); dividing by the pond area \(wL_c\) gives \(\rho g d n^2u^3/R_h^{4/3}\). Bends and the paddlewheel itself roughly double the losses (Chisti, 2016; assumed factor 2).
  2. Tube. Darcy–Weisbach: \(\Delta p/L = f\rho u^2/(2D_t)\); power per volume = \((\Delta p/L)\,Q/(\pi D_t^2/4) = (\Delta p/L)\,u\). Blasius' friction factor holds for smooth pipes at \(4000 < Re < 10^5\). Bends and fittings: ×1.5 (assumed).
  3. Airlift. Air injected at the bottom of a liquid column of height \(H\) must be compressed by \(\rho gH\); per unit liquid volume this is \(\rho g U_G\) (Chisti, 1989).
Assumptions and validity

Order-of-magnitude design estimates. Measured energy use depends strongly on the design details; the efficiencies are assumptions, not measured values. Degasser aeration, CO₂ supply, harvesting and drying are not included — drying alone usually costs far more energy than mixing.

Oxygen build-up along the tubes(P12)
\[ \Delta C_{O_2} = Y_{O_2/X}\,\mu\,X\,\frac{L}{u},\qquad Y_{O_2/X} = \frac{\gamma_X}{4}\cdot\frac{M_{O_2}}{M_X} = \frac{5.8}{4}\cdot\frac{32}{24.6} = 1.89\ \text{g g}^{-1} \]

the oxygen released by photosynthesis accumulates while the culture travels through a tube of length L at velocity u, at a rate set by how fast biomass is formed.

Follow the derivation
  1. Photosynthesis is the electron balance of respiration run backwards: water gives up the electrons that end up in biomass and releases O₂, one molecule per four electrons.
  2. With nitrate as nitrogen source, biomass CH₁.₈O₀.₅N₀.₂ holds 5.8 electrons per C-mol relative to CO₂, H₂O and NO₃⁻, so 1.45 mol O₂ is released per C-mol (24.6 g) — 1.89 g O₂ per g.
  3. The culture spends \(L/u\) seconds in the tube; with no gas phase the O₂ produced stays dissolved: \(\Delta C = r_{O_2}L/u\).
  4. The simulator uses the gross (light-driven) growth rate and adds \(\Delta C\) to air saturation at the degasser outlet.
Assumptions and validity

No O₂ loss to bubbles inside the tubes and no respiration; the effect of high O₂ on growth (photorespiration) is not simulated — the HUD shows the level so that you can compare it with the ≈ 400 % limit.

Model assumptions

  • Light. Beer–Lambert attenuation of PAR with a constant biomass-specific coefficient; light enters vertically (pond, tubes) or equally through both faces (panels, with energy-conserving light dilution); clear-sky sunlight from the solar geometry and air-mass model in physics.js (2.02 µmol J⁻¹); constant light is taken as horizontal irradiance for all systems. No clouds, no diffuse-light geometry, no ground reflection, no spectral effects.
  • Growth. Bernard & Rémond (2012) response with photoinhibition, averaged over the light path (perfect mixing, no light/dark-cycle effects), multiplied by the CTMI temperature factor (Rosso et al., 1993) and reduced by a constant respiration rate. Nutrients, CO₂ and pH are assumed non-limiting; no photoacclimation, contamination, grazing or culture crashes; high O₂ is reported but does not inhibit growth in the model.
  • Strain parameters. Spirulina light kinetics and attenuation from Farges et al. (2009); Nannochloropsis µ_max and cardinal temperatures from Weise et al. (2020); spirulina T_opt from Vonshak (1997); C. vulgaris T_opt between 23 °C (Serra-Maia et al., 2016) and 30 °C; other light parameters, the minimum/maximum temperatures of Chlorella and spirulina, and the respiration rate are representative values within published ranges (Schediwy et al., 2019; Zijffers et al., 2010) — adjust them with the physiology sliders. Protein contents from Becker (2007).
  • Temperature. Fixed culture temperature chosen by the user (real ponds follow the weather; closed reactors may need cooling).
  • Engineering. Mixing power from Manning (pond), Darcy–Weisbach/Blasius (tubes) and ρgU_G (airlift) with assumed efficiencies of 20 %, 60 % and 50 % and loss factors of 2 (bends, paddlewheel) and 1.5 (tube fittings). Productivity refers to the illuminated reactor area only.
  • 3D scene. Pond, panel and tube arrays are drawn at a representative, not full, scale (a commercial raceway is often 1000–5000 m²); the cross-section boards show the computed light field, magnified. Culture colour follows the optical depth; flow tracers move at the set velocity.
