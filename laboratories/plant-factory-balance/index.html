Plant factory energy and water balance

A plant factory turns electricity into lettuce — and into a great deal of heat, water vapour and a little stored chemical energy. In this laboratory you follow every kilowatt-hour, every litre and every gram of CO₂ through a real-sized facility for a whole year, hour by hour, in five climates from Kiruna to Singapore. Change the lamps, the crop, the building and the air-conditioning and watch the animated Sankey diagrams, the kilowatt-hours per kilogram, the coefficient of performance and the water and CO₂ use efficiencies respond — and compare your design with the landmark study of Graamans et al. (2018).

What to look at first

The model computes 12 months × 24 representative hours (one mean day per month) for the whole facility and adds them up to a year. The schematic replays one day of the month you choose; the Sankey diagrams, the readouts and the charts show the annual totals.

  1. Watch a day in January in Västerås. At 06:00 the lamps switch on: pink photons rain onto the lettuce, blue water vapour streams from the canopy to the cooling coil, condensate drips into the tank and is pumped back to the nutrient tank, and orange heat travels through the refrigerant line to the outdoor condenser. Look at the head-up display: the cooling load jumps to about 170 kW when the lamps are on and falls to a small latent load at night, when the crop still transpires a little.
  2. Follow the electricity in the large Sankey. About 80 % of the electricity goes to the LEDs. At a photon efficacy of 3.0 µmol J⁻¹ only 61 % of the LED electricity leaves the fixture as light, and about three quarters of that light is absorbed by leaves. The band that ends as chemical energy in the crop is only about 2 % of the electricity: everything else becomes heat that the HVAC system must remove.
  3. Notice how big the latent band is. In the default room the crop transpires about 2.1 L m⁻² d⁻¹ and the latent heat of that water is larger than the light the canopy absorbs — the leaves are cooler than the air and draw sensible heat from it. About 88 % of the heat the coil removes is latent. Now raise the relative humidity from 70 % to 85 %: transpiration falls to about 1.3 L m⁻² d⁻¹ and the latent share to about 57 %, yet the total heat the coils remove hardly changes.
  4. Move the factory to Abu Dhabi. Nothing inside the room changes, but the seasonal cooling COP falls from about 7.4 to 3.4 and the HVAC electricity more than doubles. Then switch the lamps on at 18:00 instead of 06:00: running the lights through the cooler night saves about 12 % of the cooling electricity.
  5. Read the water Sankey per kilogram (Display → Per kg of lettuce). About 15 L of water is transpired for each kilogram of lettuce, but only about 2 L of fresh water is needed, because almost all of the transpired water is recovered as condensate. Roughly half of that fresh water leaves inside the lettuce itself.
  6. Break the airtightness. Raise the air leakage from 0.02 to 0.2 h⁻¹: the CO₂-use efficiency falls from about 96 % to about 72 %. Then pick Leaky container farm and watch heating and reheat appear on winter nights.
Key idea

A plant factory is, thermodynamically, an electric heater that happens to grow lettuce. In steady state all electricity supplied ends up as heat except the 1–3 % stored in the crop. Transpiration does not add heat — it converts sensible heat into latent heat, which decides how the air-conditioning must be designed and how much water can be recycled.

Draw the control volume

Energy balances start with a boundary. Draw it around the air of the growing room and count what crosses it (Figure 1). Electricity enters through cables to the lamps, pumps, fans and the compressors of the HVAC system. Heat crosses the insulated envelope (inwards in summer, outwards in winter) and leaves through the cooling coils. Water enters as nutrient solution and leaves as condensate, as vapour in leaking air, and inside the harvested lettuce. CO₂ enters from the dosing system and leaves with leaking air or fixed in the crop. Because nothing accumulates over a year, what goes in must come out: that is the whole content of this laboratory.

Sensible and latent heat: why transpiration matters

Look at a single leaf in the room (Figure 2). It absorbs light, stores a few per cent of it as sugar, and must get rid of the rest. It can do so in two ways: as sensible heat — by being warmer than the air, so that convection carries heat away — or as latent heat, by evaporating water from its stomata. Evaporating one kilogram of water at 22 °C takes 2.45 MJ.

In a plant factory the air is kept at a fixed temperature and humidity and blown over the canopy. The vapour pressure deficit (VPD) of the air pulls water out of the leaves whether or not there is light. With the default settings the canopy transpires about 81 W m⁻² in the light — almost twice the 43 W m⁻² of PAR it absorbs. The difference is supplied by the air: the leaves are cooler than the air and receive sensible heat from it, which leaves the room as latent heat of water vapour. Graamans et al. (2017) found exactly this: the latent heat flux is the largest single energy flow in a plant factory and can even exceed the energy input by illumination.

For the air-conditioning engineer, the consequence is decisive. The total heat the coils must remove is fixed by the electricity dissipated in the room (plus envelope and leakage exchange) — transpiration cannot change it, because evaporation creates no energy. What transpiration changes is the split: every joule of latent heat is a joule less of sensible heat. The latent share of the cooling load therefore rises almost linearly with transpiration (the chart "Transpiration shifts the cooling load"), until the crop evaporates more than the heat input. Beyond that point the room must be reheated while it is dehumidified — the condenser heat of the dehumidifying heat pump is then the natural source.

Common misconception

"The plants cool the room, so more transpiration means less air-conditioning." Transpiration cools the leaves and the air, but the heat is not destroyed — it is stored in water vapour, and the same amount is released on the cooling coil when the vapour condenses. The coil load stays; what changes is that it must be removed at a colder coil (below the dew point), which lowers the COP.

Why does the latent share matter if the total does not change? Because removing latent heat requires a coil colder than the dew point of the room air — about 16 °C at 22 °C and 70 % RH — whereas sensible heat could be removed with a warmer, more efficient coil. A single cold coil does both at the lower temperature. Splitting the task between a dry sensible coil and a dedicated dehumidifier raises the average COP; you can test it with the Coil arrangement switch.

Removing the heat: COP and the outdoor climate

The HVAC system is a heat pump that moves heat from the cold coil (≈ 11 °C) to an outdoor condenser. The work it needs depends on the temperature lift: in Västerås in January the outdoor air is so cold that the chiller works almost for free (the model caps the COP at 10 to account for fans and pumps), whereas in Abu Dhabi in August the condenser must reject heat into 43 °C air. Kozai (2013) reported an annual cooling COP of 5–6 for a Tokyo plant factory kept at 25 °C — about 4 in summer and 10 in winter — and the Carnot-fraction model of Lesson 7.2 reproduces these values. Two design levers follow directly: light at night, when the outdoor air is coolest, and site the factory where it is cold — or where the rejected heat has a customer, such as a district-heating network.

Heat is a product, not only a waste

The default room rejects roughly 1.1 GWh of low-temperature heat per year (the "Rejected outdoors" band of the Sankey diagram). Coupling plant factories to district heating or to neighbouring greenhouses, usually with a heat pump that lifts the temperature, turns this cooling load into a revenue — a design question you can explore in Lesson 8.1.

Closing the water loop

Almost all the water a lettuce plant takes up is transpired: about 15 L per kg of lettuce with the default settings. In a greenhouse this vapour leaves through the vents; in an airtight plant factory it condenses on the cooling coil, is collected and returned to the nutrient tank. The fresh water that must be bought covers only the water inside the harvested lettuce (about 0.95 L per kg at 5 % dry matter), the nutrient solution that is periodically discharged, the vapour that escapes with leaking air and the condensate that is not reused. Kozai (2013) defined the water-use efficiency of a closed plant production system as the fraction of the supplied water that is either recycled or ends up in the plants, and reported 0.95–0.98, against 0.02–0.03 for a ventilated greenhouse. Van Delden et al. (2021), summarising Graamans et al. (2018), put the saving at about 90 % compared with greenhouses and about 99 % compared with open fields.

CO₂: fixed or leaked

Lettuce dry matter is about 40 % carbon (Anderson et al., 2018), so every gram of dry matter holds 1.47 g of CO₂. Enriching the air to 1000 µmol mol⁻¹ raises the yield by about 30 % compared with outdoor air (Lesson 8.3), but whatever leaks out with the air exchange is lost. With 0.01–0.02 air changes per hour, Kozai (2013) reported CO₂-use efficiencies of 0.87–0.89; in this room, with little air volume per square metre of trays, the model gives about 0.96. The CO₂ enrichment balance laboratory explores the same balance in greenhouses, where ventilation is a thousand times larger.

Graamans et al. (2018) also showed the other side of the coin: counting the sunlight, the plant factory used less total energy (1411 MJ kg⁻¹ DW) than even the most efficient greenhouse (1699 MJ kg⁻¹ DW), and it was more productive per unit of water, CO₂ and land. Sunlight is free and renewable, electricity is not — which is why the source of the electricity decides the carbon footprint (Lesson 15.1).

Research frontier

As LED efficacy approaches 3.5–4 µmol J⁻¹, less of the electricity becomes sensible heat while the crop's transpiration stays the same. Rooms with very efficient lamps therefore become dehumidification-dominated and may need reheat — try Best-in-class 2026. Research in 2024–2026 focuses on controlling VPD and air speed to limit transpiration without harming calcium transport (tipburn), on desiccant and heat-pump dehumidifiers that recover condenser heat, and on coupling farms to heat networks.

Assumptions and validity

Only the operational energy and the dosed CO₂ are counted — not the building, the lamps, seeds, substrate, packaging or transport, which a full LCA includes (Lesson 15.1). The grid factor is a location-based annual average; marginal factors can be much higher.

Start each task from the preset Swedish plant factory. Use Save investigation to store settings and results in your notebook, and CSV (monthly) to analyse the month-by-month results in a spreadsheet.

  1. Close the energy balance yourself

    Switch the Sankey diagrams to Per kg of lettuce.   2. How transpiration shapes the HVAC design

  3. Climate, COP and the timing of the lights

    For all five climates, record the seasonal COP and the kWh per kg with the lamps switched on at 06:00 and at 18:00. In which climates does night lighting pay off, and by how much? Estimate the annual saving in euros at €0.10 kWh⁻¹. Why is the effect small in Västerås and Singapore?

  4. What should the next euro buy?

    Starting from the default, compare four upgrades one at a time: photon efficacy 3.0 → 3.5 µmol J⁻¹; utilisation 0.90 → 0.95; light-use efficiency 0.55 → 0.65 g mol⁻¹; split sensible/latent coils.   5. Envelope: insulate or not?

    In Kiruna and in Abu Dhabi, vary the U-value from 0.1 to 3 W m⁻² K⁻¹ (with and without reuse condenser heat). Where does a poorly insulated envelope help, where does it hurt, and why?   6. Carbon: Sweden versus Poland

    Compute the carbon footprint per kg for the three grids and for the three CO₂ sources. Compare with imported field lettuce (≈ 0.6 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹, Lesson 15.1). At what grid factor does your plant factory break even with the imported lettuce? (Hint: Eq. PF12 is linear in the grid factor.)

Model assumptions

  • Time resolution. One representative day per month, hourly steps; the outdoor temperature follows a cosine between the monthly mean daily minimum and maximum (maximum at 15:00); outdoor humidity is constant during the day. Weather variability, heat waves and solar gains on the envelope are ignored (Graamans et al., 2018 found albedo effects of less than 8 %).
  • Building. A square room; height 1.0 m + 0.45 m per tier; walls and roof exchange heat with outdoor air, the floor with the ground at the annual mean outdoor temperature, all with the same U-value.
  • Set-points. Temperature, relative humidity and CO₂ are held constant day and night. In practice many farms lower the temperature at night and stop dosing CO₂.
  • Lighting and crop. Uniform PPFD; constant light-use efficiency (Jin et al., 2023) scaled with CO₂ by Eq. 8.3.2 of Lesson 8.3; roots are 10 % of the total dry matter (Kozai, 2013); dry matter stores 17.5 kJ g⁻¹ and is 40 % carbon (Anderson et al., 2018). The crop is treated as a continuous, staggered production with a constant canopy.
  • Transpiration. Penman–Monteith for one big leaf per tier (Lesson 2.2), stomatal conductance at night 10 % of the day value; in the prescribed mode 90 % of the daily water is transpired in the light.
  • HVAC. Cooling COP from a Carnot-fraction model (η = 0.40, condenser 15 K above outdoor air, cap 10; Lesson 7.2) or fixed values; no free cooling with outdoor air; heating by an air-source heat pump (COP 1–5) after any recovery of condenser heat. Fans inside the air handler are part of the auxiliary electricity.
  • Water. The nutrient solution is fully recirculated apart from the discharge; the water chemically split in photosynthesis (≈ 0.6 kg per kg DW) and the water in the roots are neglected.
  • CO₂. Worker respiration and CO₂ dissolved in the nutrient solution are neglected; the set-point is held for 24 h.
  • Grid factors. Location-based life-cycle averages for 2025 (Ember, via Our World in Data, accessed September 2026): Sweden 35, EU-27 210 and Poland 589 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹.
