Stomata and transpiration

Every molecule of CO₂ a leaf takes in costs it hundreds of molecules of water, and both travel through the same microscopic pores. Zoom into a leaf epidermis where kidney-shaped guard cells open and close as you change light, CO₂, humidity and root-zone water — then step back to a whole leaf in an air stream, seen through a thermal camera. Behind both views runs the physics growers use to manage climate: a leaf energy balance that solves for leaf temperature and transpiration, with stomata and the boundary layer as conductances in series.

A guided tour from pore to leaf

  1. Read the epidermis. The patch is 260 × 180 µm of the lower leaf surface — about the size of a grain of fine sand. The interlocking “jigsaw” shapes are pavement cells; they are transparent, so the green you see is the mesophyll below, glowing through. Six stomata (≈ 128 per mm²) are scattered among them, each a pair of kidney-shaped guard cells with green chloroplasts and a nucleus. Click a guard cell to read its current pore width.
  2. Watch the pores respond. Drag the relative humidity from 60 % to 30 %: within the stomatal response time the guard cells move together and the water-vapour plume thins, although drier air pulls harder on each pore. Now set PPFD to 0 (darkness) or raise CO₂ to 1500 ppm — both close the pores, for different reasons. Use Close-up to see one pore.
  3. Dry the root zone. Lower Root-zone water below 40 %. Roots send the hormone abscisic acid (ABA) to the leaves and the stomata close although the leaf itself is still turgid. Look at leaf temperature: with less evaporative cooling it rises.
  4. Step back to the whole leaf. Switch the scale to Whole leaf. A fan blows air across a lettuce leaf; the thermal camera shows its surface temperature. The upwind (leading) edge, where the boundary layer is thinnest, stays closest to air temperature. Turn the wind down to 0.05 m s⁻¹ and watch the translucent boundary layer thicken and the leaf warm up; make the leaf wider and the same happens.
  5. Play a day. Press Play the day. With the LED source the lights switch on at dawn: photosynthesis responds within seconds but the stomata lag behind by several minutes — compare the solid and dashed curves in the day chart. In the sun, stomata close slightly in the afternoon when the air is warmest and driest.
  6. Try the presets. Tipburn risk shows a leaf in humid, still air that transpires very little — the calcium it needs travels in that water. Clear, calm night shows a leaf radiating heat to a cold sky and cooling below the air, until dew forms on it.
Key idea

Transpiration is driven by the difference in water-vapour pressure between the saturated air inside the leaf and the air outside, and resisted by two conductances in series — the stomata, which the plant controls, and the boundary layer, which the air flow and leaf size control. Because evaporation consumes energy, transpiration and leaf temperature can only be computed together, from the leaf's energy balance.

Why leaves lose water

Inside a leaf, the walls of the mesophyll cells are wet, so the air in the intercellular spaces is practically saturated with water vapour at leaf temperature. Outside, the air is usually much drier. Whenever the stomata are open to let CO₂ in, water vapour diffuses out down this concentration gradient. The ratio is lopsided: CO₂ makes up about 0.04 % of air, whereas the vapour-pressure difference between leaf and air is typically 1–2 % of atmospheric pressure, and water molecules diffuse 1.6 times faster than CO₂. A well-watered C3 crop therefore loses a few hundred water molecules for every CO₂ molecule it fixes. Transpiration is not a design flaw, though: it cools the leaves, and the transpiration stream carries nutrients such as calcium from the roots to the shoot.

The energy balance links water and temperature

Evaporating one mole of water takes about 44 kJ. A leaf transpiring 4 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ therefore removes about 180 W m⁻² — similar to the solar radiation it absorbs at midday in a greenhouse. The absorbed radiation (short-wave from the sun or lamps, long-wave from the surroundings) must leave the leaf again as emitted long-wave radiation, as sensible heat H conducted and convected to the air, and as latent heat λE carried away by water vapour (Figure 2). The leaf settles at the one temperature at which the books balance (Eq. S1). Because the vapour pressure inside the leaf depends steeply on that temperature, transpiration and leaf temperature must be solved together — this laboratory does so numerically, and also with the classical linearised solution, the Penman–Monteith equation for a leaf (Eq. S9), which agrees within about 1 %.

The boundary layer: why wind and leaf size matter

Air sticks to surfaces. Close to the leaf the wind slows down to zero, and heat and water vapour have to cross this layer of slow air by molecular diffusion. For a flat plate in laminar flow, the theory of heat transfer gives a conductance that grows with the square root of wind speed and shrinks with the square root of the plate's size in the direction of the flow; Campbell & Norman (1998) turned this into the practical rule of Eq. S5, with a characteristic dimension of 0.72 times the leaf width. The layer starts thin at the leading edge and thickens downstream, which is why the upwind margin of the leaf in the thermal view stays closest to air temperature. In still air, the leaf's own warmth drives buoyant (free) convection, which the model adds to the forced convection. Kitaya et al. (2003) showed experimentally that leaf photosynthesis and transpiration rise steeply as air speed increases from almost still air to a few tenths of a metre per second — one reason why plant factories and vertical farms invest in horizontal air flow over each tier.

How stomata decide

Guard cells swell when they accumulate potassium, chloride and malate and draw in water; the kidney shape and the radial orientation of cellulose in their walls turn this swelling into an opening of the pore. They respond to many signals at once. Light (blue light directly, and photosynthesis indirectly) opens them; high CO₂ closes them; dry air closes them; and abscisic acid (ABA), synthesised when roots sense drying soil and transported in the xylem, closes them even before the leaf itself loses turgor (Tardieu & Davies, 1993). The Medlyn et al. (2011) model captures the first three responses with a single idea: stomata behave as if they maximise carbon gain minus the “cost” of the water spent (Eq. S7). Light and CO₂ act through the photosynthesis rate A and the ratio A/Cₐ; dry air acts through the square root of the VPD. The root signal is represented, as in land-surface models (De Kauwe et al., 2015), by a factor β that scales the stomatal slope g₁ and falls once the root zone has lost 60 % of its extractable water (Granier et al., 1999). Stomata are slow: a change of light is followed within seconds by photosynthesis but within minutes to tens of minutes by the stomata (Lawson & Blatt, 2014; McAusland et al., 2016), which the model represents with a single time constant τ (Eq. S8).

Common misconception

“If the stomata close to half their conductance, transpiration halves.” Only when the leaf is well coupled to the air (large boundary-layer conductance, Ω ≈ 0). Closing stomata warms the leaf, which raises the vapour pressure inside it and partly compensates; and when the boundary layer is thick (still air, large leaves, dense canopies) it limits the flux anyway. The decoupling coefficient Ω (Eq. S10) measures how much the stomata are really in control (Jarvis & McNaughton, 1986).

Psychrometrics at the leaf surface

Growers read humidity as relative humidity, but plants respond to the vapour-pressure deficit, and for transpiration the relevant deficit is the one between the leaf interior at leaf temperature and the air: the leaf-to-air VPD. The live figure below places the current air and leaf on a psychrometric diagram (vapour pressure against temperature). The air is a point below the saturation curve; the leaf interior is a point on the curve at leaf temperature. Warming the leaf moves its point up the steep saturation curve and increases the driving force; if the leaf cools below the dew point of the air — as on a clear night — the difference reverses and dew condenses. Transpiration, calcium and tipburn

Calcium moves from the roots to the shoot almost exclusively in the xylem, with the transpiration stream, and once deposited in a leaf it is hardly redistributed. Young, rapidly expanding leaves need a lot of it for their cell walls and membranes, but the inner leaves of a lettuce head are enclosed by older leaves, sit in humid, still air and transpire very little. When growth is fast — high light, high CO₂, warm temperatures — calcium supply at the margins of these leaves falls behind, cells collapse and the leaf tips turn brown: tipburn (Saure, 1998). The humidity and air movement around the leaf, not the calcium concentration of the nutrient solution, are usually decisive. Blowing air into the heads prevented tipburn in classic experiments (Goto & Takakura, 1992), and at Cornell, lettuce could only be grown at a daily light integral of 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ with fans blowing air down onto the crop; without them the limit was about 12 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (Brechner & Both, 2013). Tipburn is one of the main factors that limits lettuce productivity in plant factories (Frantz et al., 2004). Try the Tipburn risk preset and increase the wind speed: transpiration of the exposed leaf roughly doubles.

Research frontier

Faster stomata could save water: because stomata lag behind fluctuating light, leaves waste water after shade flecks and lose carbon after sun flecks. Breeding and engineering for faster guard-cell responses, and for fewer or smaller stomata, are active routes to better water-use efficiency (Lawson & Blatt, 2014). In controlled environments, the same understanding is used in reverse: VPD, air speed and lighting schedules are managed to keep transpiration high enough for calcium transport without wasting water or energy on dehumidification.

What it means for controlled-environment agriculture

  • VPD targets. Recommended VPD windows for greenhouse crops lie between about 0.3 and 1.0 kPa, with commercial guidelines up to about 1.2 kPa (Lesson 2.2, Table 1). Within them stomata stay open and transpiration is moderate; below about 0.3 kPa young leaves are starved of calcium, and above about 1.5 kPa stomata close and the crop is stressed.
  • Air movement of a few tenths of a metre per second across the leaves raises the boundary-layer conductance, couples leaf temperature to air temperature and prevents humid pockets in the canopy.
  • LEDs versus sun. At the same PPFD, LED light carries less than half the radiant energy of sunlight, because more than half of the sun's energy is near-infrared. Leaves absorb near-infrared poorly, so the absorbed heat load is only about a quarter lower — but LED-lit leaves are still cooler and transpire less. The electrical energy of the lamps ends up as heat that the HVAC system has to remove.
  • Transpiration is the largest latent heat load in a closed plant factory: every litre transpired must be condensed again by the dehumidifier (see the plant-factory energy balance laboratory).

The equations behind the simulation

The model solves, for given air temperature, humidity, wind, radiation, CO₂ and root-zone water, the leaf temperature TL, the stomatal conductance gₛ, the transpiration rate E and the photosynthesis rate A that satisfy the energy balance (S1–S6) and the stomatal model (S7) at the same time. All fluxes are per square metre of (one-sided) leaf area; conductances in mol m⁻² s⁻¹; vapour pressures in kPa; P = 101.3 kPa.

Leaf energy balance(S1)
\[ R_{\text{abs}} - 2\varepsilon\sigma T_L^4 \;=\; R_n \;=\; H + \lambda E \]

The radiation a leaf absorbs, minus the long-wave radiation it emits from both faces, is the net radiation; in steady state all of it must leave as sensible heat and as latent heat of evaporation.

Follow the derivation
  1. First law of thermodynamics for a thin leaf: heat storage = gains − losses. A leaf is only about 0.3 mm thick; its heat capacity (≈ 1 kJ m⁻² K⁻¹) lets it adjust within a minute, so over longer times storage ≈ 0.
  2. Gains: absorbed radiation. Losses: emitted radiation (a grey body emits εσT⁴ from each of its two faces), sensible heat and latent heat. Metabolic terms (photosynthesis stores ≈ 0.5 % of the energy) are neglected.
  3. The equation is non-linear in TL (T⁴ and the exponential saturation vapour pressure in λE). The laboratory finds TL numerically: the residual \(R_{\text{abs}} - 2\varepsilon\sigma T_L^4 - H - \lambda E\) decreases monotonically with TL, so its root is bracketed between Ta − 25 and Ta + 30 °C and found by regula falsi to 0.002 °C.
Assumptions and validity

Steady state; a single, flat, isolated leaf of uniform temperature (the thermal image in the whole-leaf view relaxes the uniform-temperature assumption); no heat conduction to the petiole; no dew on the surfaces unless the leaf falls below the dew point.

Where the numbers come from
  1. Sunlight: 1 W m⁻² of global radiation carries about 2.0 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of PAR photons (45 % PAR × 4.57 µmol J⁻¹). Leaves absorb about 85 % of PAR but only about 20 % of the near-infrared (Campbell & Norman, 1998), so a sunlit leaf absorbs 0.85 × 0.45 + 0.20 × 0.55 ≈ 0.49 of the short-wave energy.
  2. Horticultural LEDs emit almost only 400–700 nm light; 4.6 µmol J⁻¹ corresponds to a white/red mixture. The same PPFD therefore delivers only about 45 % of the radiant energy of sunlight — but the leaf absorbs a larger fraction of it.
  3. The clear-sky formula is Brutsaert's (1975) derivation expressed in kPa (C&N 1998, eq. 10.10). At 10 °C and 85 % RH it gives εsky ≈ 0.77: the sky radiates like a black body at about −8 °C, which is why leaves and lawns cool below air temperature on clear nights.

Transpiration through stomata and boundary layer in series(S4)
\[ E = g_{tv}\,\frac{e_s(T_L) - e_a}{P}, \qquad g_{tv} = \begin{cases} \left(\dfrac{1}{g_s} + \dfrac{1}{g_{bv}}\right)^{-1} & \text{stomata on one face} \\[2ex] 2\left(\dfrac{2}{g_s} + \dfrac{1}{g_{bv}}\right)^{-1} & \text{stomata on both faces} \end{cases} \]

The transpiration rate is the total conductance for water vapour times the vapour-pressure difference between the leaf interior and the air, expressed as a mole fraction. The total conductance combines the stomata and the boundary layer like resistors in series; with stomata on both faces each face carries half of gₛ in series with its own boundary layer, and the two faces act in parallel.

Follow the derivation
  1. Fick's law in molar units: flux = conductance × difference in mole fraction; the mole fraction of water vapour is e/P.
  2. In series, the same flux crosses both steps: E = gs(ei − es,surf)/P = gbv(es,surf − ea)/P. Eliminating the unknown vapour pressure at the leaf surface gives 1/gtv = 1/gs + 1/gbv.
  3. Two faces each with gs/2 in series with gbv, in parallel: gtv = 2/(2/gs + 1/gbv).
  4. The latent heat flux is λE. If the leaf is colder than the dew point of the air, the difference becomes negative and water condenses on both surfaces, through the boundary layer only.
Assumptions and validity

The intercellular air is saturated at leaf temperature (true for well-watered leaves to within a few per cent); cuticular transpiration is included only through the small residual conductance g₀.

\[ g_{Ha} = 0.135\sqrt{\frac{u}{d}} + 0.05\left(\frac{\left|T_L - T_a\right|}{d}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad g_{bv} = 0.147\sqrt{\frac{u}{d}} + 0.055\left(\frac{\left|T_L - T_a\right|}{d}\right)^{1/4}, \qquad d = 0.72\,w \]

Follow the derivation (from heat-transfer theory)
  1. For laminar flow along a flat plate of length d, the mean Nusselt number is Nu = 0.66 Re½ Pr⅓, with Re = ud/ν. In molar units the conductance is g = ĉ kH Nu/d, where ĉ ≈ 41.6 mol m⁻³ is the molar density of air and kH = 21.5 × 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹ its thermal diffusivity.
  2. Substituting ν = 15.1 × 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹ and Pr = 0.71: gHa = 41.6 × 21.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.66 × (15.1 × 10⁻⁶)−½ × 0.71⅓ √(u/d) = 0.135 √(u/d) mol m⁻² s⁻¹ — Campbell & Norman's (1998) coefficient.
  3. For water vapour, replace the thermal diffusivity by the vapour diffusivity (24.2 × 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹) and Pr by the Schmidt number: the coefficient becomes 0.147.
  4. Local conductance along the plate falls as x−½ from the leading edge; averaging over a leaf of irregular outline gives the effective dimension d = 0.72 w for flow across the leaf width (C&N 1998).
  5. In free convection Nu = 0.5 Gr¼ (Gr the Grashof number, ∝ ΔT d³), which gives 0.05 (ΔT/d)¼. The two mechanisms are added, as is common in leaf models (Leuning et al., 1995).

How light, CO₂, VPD and drought act
  1. Light raises A, and gₛ follows A — so stomata open in light and close in the dark (to g₀).
  2. CO₂ enters as A/Cs: when CO₂ rises, A rises less than in proportion, so A/Cs and gₛ fall. Stomata close partially at high CO₂, which is why CO₂ enrichment raises water-use efficiency.
  3. VPD: gₛ falls with 1/√D, the optimal response derived by Medlyn et al. (2011).
  4. Drought: β multiplies g₁ as in land-surface models using the Medlyn equation (De Kauwe et al., 2015); the threshold REW = 0.4, below which transpiration becomes water-limited, follows Granier et al. (1999). Physiologically it stands for the ABA signal from drying roots (Tardieu & Davies, 1993).
  5. A, gₛ and the intercellular CO₂ are solved together with the diffusion equation A = (Ca − Cᵢ)/(1.6/gₛ + 1.37/gbv,tot), exactly as in the photosynthesis laboratory, but now at the leaf temperature from the energy balance.
Assumptions and validity

β acts only on stomata; real drought also reduces photosynthetic capacity (non-stomatal limitation) and involves hydraulic signals. The linear β(REW) is an empirical representation of the chemical signal, not a model of ABA synthesis.

  2. The balance becomes Rni = cpgHrΔT + (λgtv/P)(Da + sΔT).
  3. Solve for ΔT, substitute into λE = (λgtv/P)(Da + sΔT) and use λγ/P = cp: the result is S9.
  4. Worked example (Sunny greenhouse preset): Rni = 219 W m⁻², gHr = 1.146, gtv = 0.295 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, s = 0.179 kPa K⁻¹, γ* = 0.262 kPa K⁻¹, Da = 1.19 kPa → λE = (39.3 + 40.1)/0.441 = 180 W m⁻² and ΔT = +1.17 K. The full numerical solution gives 180 W m⁻² and +1.16 K.
Assumptions and validity

The linearisation is excellent for |TL − Ta| of a few kelvin. Applied to a whole canopy with a single “big leaf”, the same equation is the FAO Penman–Monteith equation for crop evapotranspiration (see the water-relations lesson).

(kPa K⁻¹ · W m⁻² + J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ · mol m⁻² s⁻¹ · kPa)/(kPa K⁻¹) = W m⁻² ✓
Decoupling coefficient(S10)
\[ \Omega = \frac{\epsilon + 1}{\epsilon + 1 + g_{bv,\text{tot}}/g_s}, \qquad \epsilon = \frac{s}{\gamma} \]

Ω tells how far transpiration is controlled by the stomata (Ω near 0: a small change in gₛ changes E in proportion) or by the energy supply (Ω near 1: E follows the absorbed radiation and hardly depends on gₛ).

Where does it come from?

From the Penman–Monteith equation, the relative sensitivity of E to gₛ is (dE/E)/(dgₛ/gₛ) = 1 − Ω (Jarvis & McNaughton, 1986). Tall, aerodynamically rough canopies such as forests, and small leaves in wind, are well coupled (Ω ≈ 0.1–0.3); short, smooth grass swards are much less so (Ω ≈ 0.8), and large leaves in still air, and whole greenhouse crops, are poorly coupled (Ω → 1). The single-surface form used here treats both faces with one boundary-layer conductance — a simplification.

all ratios dimensionless ✓
From conductance to pore width (drives the 3D guard cells)(S11)
\[ g_{\text{side}} = \frac{D_v}{v}\,\frac{n\,a}{\,l + \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{a/\pi}\,}, \qquad \text{width} = \frac{4a}{\pi\,\ell_p} \]

The conductance of one leaf surface equals the diffusivity of water vapour divided by the molar volume of air, times the pore area per unit leaf area, divided by the diffusion path: the pore depth plus an end correction for the vapour shell that spreads out from each pore.

What the numbers show

For an amphistomatous lettuce leaf each surface carries half of gₛ. At gₛ = 0.4 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ (0.2 per face) the pores need to be only 1.7 µm wide — a small fraction of their maximum of about 9 µm. Stomata are extremely effective diffusers: pores covering well under 1 % of the leaf surface let water vapour escape almost as fast as from a wet surface of the same area (Franks & Beerling, 2009). Invert the equation for a (a quadratic in √a) to obtain the widths shown in 3D.

Model assumptions

  • A single flat leaf in steady state (except for the stomatal time lag in the day simulation); no heat storage, no heat conduction through the petiole, photosynthetic energy storage neglected.
  • Radiation: short-wave absorptance 0.85 (PAR) and 0.20 (near-infrared); sunlight PPFD/2.02 W m⁻²; LEDs PPFD/4.6 W m⁻². Surroundings are either at air temperature (room, greenhouse) or a clear sky above and ground at air temperature below. Reflections from neighbouring leaves are ignored.
  • Boundary layer: flat-plate laminar forced convection plus free convection (Campbell & Norman, 1998); turbulence, leaf flutter and canopy sheltering are not modelled. The thermal image uses a local conductance proportional to x−½ from the upwind edge, scaled so that its mean equals the whole-leaf value; leaf temperature at each point is computed from a local energy balance with the same stomatal conductance everywhere.
  • Stomata: Medlyn et al. (2011) coupled to the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model with Bernacchi temperature functions — the same leaf (Vcmax,25 = 80, Jmax,25 = 135 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) as in the photosynthesis laboratory; g₀ = 0.01 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The drought factor β = min(1, REW/0.4) acts on g₁ only (no non-stomatal limitation). Mesophyll conductance infinite; air pressure 101.3 kPa.
  • Stomatal dynamics: one first-order time constant for opening and closing. Day forcing: sinusoidal sunlight (or a rectangular LED photoperiod with 10-minute ramps), sinusoidal air temperature peaking at 15:00 (or warming and cooling with the lights), constant vapour pressure, wind and CO₂.
  • 3D: the epidermis anatomy (cell sizes, 128 stomata per mm², guard cells 30 µm long, pore depth 10 µm, maximum pore width 9 µm) is typical of a dicot leaf, not measured on lettuce. Pore widths are computed from gₛ (Eq. S11); particle rates are proportional to E and A, particle speeds and flow tracers are illustrative.
