Sensor anomaly detection

A greenhouse climate computer trusts its sensors blindly. When a humidity probe drifts, a CO₂ sensor freezes or a pH electrode starts to crackle, the controller keeps acting on wrong numbers — and nobody notices until the crop does. This laboratory streams three days of six coupled greenhouse sensors (air temperature, relative humidity, CO₂, EC, pH and dissolved oxygen) into seven automatic detectors, from a simple range check to a small isolation forest. You know the truth: every fault was injected on purpose. Tune the thresholds, inject your own faults by clicking on the data, and watch precision, recall and detection delay pull against each other.

What to look at first

A coloured ring on the data marks a sample that raised an alarm, in the colour of the first detector that fired. The thin strip along the bottom of each panel is the verdict: green = fault caught (true positive), amber = false alarm, red = fault missed.

  1. Let it stream. Watch the confusion matrix fill. Most samples are true negatives; the interesting numbers are the three small ones. Hover over any point to see its value, the truth and which detectors fired.
  2. The frozen CO₂ sensor (day 2, 08:00). In the morning, CO₂ should climb with enrichment and fall when the vents open — but the line is flat. Only the stuck detector sees it, and only after its window (30 min) has filled with identical values. Shorten the flat-line window and the delay shrinks; make it too short and quiet periods start to look stuck.
  3. The drifting humidity probe (day 2, 18:00). The drift adds about 1.7 %RH per hour — far less than normal changes, so the z-score, Hampel and rate detectors never notice. Hours later, on the humid night, the probe claims more water vapour than the air can hold — its dew point rises above its own temperature (RH above 100 %) — and the physical-consistency and range checks fire.
  4. Spikes and the two statistics. Select Rolling z-score for the score chart and focus on T. The spike on day 2 at 14:20 stands out — but the z-score window also contains the spike for the next hour, which inflates its standard deviation and hides smaller faults. The Hampel filter's median and MAD barely notice a single spike.
  5. Trade-offs. Try the presets Strict and Sensitive. Strict thresholds give high precision but miss events; sensitive ones catch everything and bury the grower in false alarms. Read the result off the precision–recall chart.
  6. Inject your own. Choose a fault type, sensor, magnitude and duration, then click on a panel at the time you want it. Which fault types are easy, and which are nearly invisible?
Key idea

No single detector catches every fault. Range checks catch the impossible, rate and robust statistics catch the sudden, variance tests catch the frozen, and only physical knowledge — or a second, independent sensor — catches the slow drift. Real quality-control systems therefore layer several simple tests and attach a flag to every value instead of deleting anything (Campbell et al., 2013).

Why sensors lie

Sensor networks in agriculture and environmental monitoring produce far more data than anyone can inspect, and faults are common: in large field deployments, a substantial share of all readings can be affected (Ni et al., 2009; Sharma et al., 2010). The faults fall into a small number of recognisable types, each with a physical cause and a typical signature (Figure 1, Table 1). Automatic detection is the first line of defence; it is required before the data drive a controller, an alarm or a digital twin.

Four families of detectors

Rules encode what is possible: a value outside the sensor's plausible range, a missing value, or a change faster than the physics of the room allows. They are transparent and cheap, and they form the core of operational quality-control schemes such as the U.S. IOOS QARTOD manuals (Lesson 9.3, Table 3). They catch only gross errors.

Local statistics compare each value with its recent past. The rolling z-score uses the mean and standard deviation, which a single spike can inflate enough to hide itself and its neighbours (masking). The Hampel filter (Hampel, 1974; Pearson, 2002) uses the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) instead: both ignore up to half the window being wild, so the robust z-score of a spike stays large. Leys et al. (2013) recommend thresholds of 2.5–3 robust standard deviations for outliers in general; for streams with a few rare faults among thousands of samples, higher thresholds keep false alarms tolerable. A persistence test looks at the variance itself: a working sensor always shows some noise, so a rolling standard deviation far below the sensor's noise level means the value has stopped changing.

Physical consistency uses knowledge that no statistic contains. Air cannot hold more water vapour than saturation, so the dew point computed from a humidity probe must stay below the air temperature measured next to it. Water cannot hold much more dissolved oxygen than its temperature-dependent saturation (unless pure oxygen is injected). These tests catch slow drift, which is invisible to local statistics, because they compare a sensor with something independent — the laws of physics or another sensor.

Machine learning learns what normal looks like. The isolation forest (Liu et al., 2008, 2012) builds many random trees, each splitting the training data on random features at random values until every point is alone. Anomalies are "few and different", so they are isolated after few splits; normal points sit in dense regions and need many (Figure 2). The average path length, normalised by that expected for random data, becomes an anomaly score between 0 and 1. Here each sensor has its own forest of 100 trees grown on 256-sample subsets of day 1, using four shape features: the residual from the recent median, the first difference, and the 15- and 60-min standard deviations.

Judging a detector: the confusion matrix

Every sample ends up in one of four boxes (the live matrix above the tabs). Precision is the share of alarms that were real faults: low precision means alarm fatigue, and operators who start ignoring the system. Recall is the share of faulty samples that raised an alarm: low recall means wrong data reach the controller. F1 is their harmonic mean. For a grower, the detection delay of each fault event often matters more than sample-level recall: a drift caught after 30 minutes is harmless, one caught after two days has already cost a crop.

Common misconception

"The detector is 97 % accurate, so it works." Faults are rare: in the default scenario only about one sample in ten is faulty, so a detector that never raises an alarm is already about 90 % accurate. With imbalanced classes, accuracy and even ROC curves flatter a detector; precision and recall do not, which is why this laboratory plots precision against recall (Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015).

Frontier — anomaly detection as part of the digital twin

Commercial greenhouse platforms increasingly run anomaly detection continuously, combining physics-based checks, redundancy and machine-learning models, and flag or replace suspicious values before they reach the climate controller or a digital twin. The hard problems are the same as in this laboratory: labelled faults are scarce, what is "normal" changes with season and crop stage, and every false alarm costs trust. The QARTOD principle — flag, never delete — keeps the raw data available for re-analysis when the detector improves.

For the statistics of spikes, filters and flags, see Lesson 9.3 (Calibration, uncertainty and data quality); for the network that delivers the data, the IoT network designer; for filtering noisy signals, Sampling, noise and filtering.

The equations behind the detectors

The streams are sampled every Δt = 1 min for three days. Sensor j has a normal noise standard deviation σj (0.05 °C, 0.3 %RH, 6 ppm, 0.008 mS cm⁻¹, 0.015 pH, 0.04 mg L⁻¹), a resolution and a typical fault size mj. All detectors are causal: they use only the current and earlier samples.

Fault models(A1)
\[ \begin{gathered} \tilde x_k = \begin{cases} x_k + v_k + 2a\,[k = k_0] & \text{spike} \\ x_k + v_k + a\,\dfrac{k - k_0 + 1}{D} & \text{drift} \\ \tilde x_{k_0} & \text{stuck} \\ x_k + v_k + a & \text{offset} \\ \text{missing} & \text{dropout} \\ x_k + v_k + 0.35\,|a|\,\varepsilon_k & \text{noise burst} \end{cases} \\[8pt] k_0 \le k < k_0 + D, \qquad a = \mu\,m_j \end{gathered} \]

during a fault that starts at sample k₀ and lasts D samples, the measured value is the true value plus normal noise plus a fault term: a single jump of twice the fault size for a spike, a linear ramp to the full fault size for drift, a constant for an offset and extra random noise for a burst; a stuck sensor repeats its reading at k₀ and a dropout delivers nothing. The fault size is the chosen magnitude μ times the sensor's typical fault size.

Where the models come from

The taxonomy follows field studies of sensor-network data (Ni et al., 2009; Sharma et al., 2010), which distinguish short faults (spikes), noise faults (increased variance), constant or stuck faults, and calibration faults (offset and drift). A dropout is a data-delivery fault rather than a sensing fault, but it must be flagged in the same way (QARTOD flag 9, "missing"). Every sample inside a fault interval is labelled faulty — including the first minutes of a drift, when the error is still smaller than the noise. That is honest (the sensor is already faulty) and it is why recall for drift is low for every detector.

Assumptions and validity

Faults are additive or replace the value; real faults can also be multiplicative (a gain error that grows with the reading) or intermittent. The true signals come from a deliberately simple coupled greenhouse model (solar forcing for 25 September at Västerås, heating set points, vent opening, vapour pressure, CO₂ enrichment, evapo-concentration and dosing of the nutrient solution, temperature-dependent oxygen saturation); they are illustrative, not measurements.

Range and rate-of-change tests(A2)
\[ \text{range: } \tilde x_k \notin [x_{\min}, x_{\max}] \ \text{or missing}, \qquad \text{rate: } \frac{|\tilde x_k - \tilde x_{k'}|}{r_{\max}} > \kappa \]

raise an alarm if a value is missing or outside the plausible range, or if its change since the last valid value k′ exceeds κ times the fastest physically plausible change per minute.

Choosing the limits from physics

A greenhouse of several hundred cubic metres of air cannot warm by several degrees in a minute; a nutrient tank with a few hundred litres cannot change its EC by 0.1 mS cm⁻¹ in a minute unless a dosing pump runs. Physical limits like these, not statistics, set the rate threshold. The range check additionally catches the "error codes" that many digital sensors output when disconnected (for example −127 °C or 85 °C for the DS18B20).

Assumptions and validity

A rate test fires twice for a spike: on the way up and on the way down, so the sample after a spike is a false alarm. Legitimate fast events (a dosing pulse, a vent opening, a door) must be excluded by setting the threshold above them — or by telling the detector about the actuators.

Rolling z-score(A3)
\[ \bar x_{k} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=k-w}^{k-1} \tilde x_i, \qquad s_{k} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=k-w}^{k-1}\left(\tilde x_i - \bar x_k\right)^2}, \qquad z_k = \frac{|\tilde x_k - \bar x_k|}{\max(s_k, \sigma_j)} > z^{*} \]

compare each value with the mean of the previous w samples, in units of their standard deviation (never smaller than the normal sensor noise); alarm if the distance exceeds z*.

\(w\), \(n\)window length and number of valid samples in itsamples
\(\bar x_k\), \(s_k\)rolling mean and standard deviation (current sample excluded)sensor unit
\(z^*\)threshold–
Why the z-score is fragile
  1. For Gaussian noise, \(P(|z| > 3) = 0.27\,\%\): with 6 sensors × 1440 samples a day, even a perfect sensor would produce about 23 alarms per day at \(z^* = 3\) — thresholds must be higher, or alarms must be combined over several samples.
  2. A spike of size A inside the window raises \(s_k\) to about \(\sqrt{\sigma^2 + A^2/w}\): with A = 4 °C, w = 60 and σ = 0.05 °C, \(s \approx 0.52\) °C — ten times the noise. For the next hour, only faults larger than \(z^*\times 0.52\) °C can be seen. This is masking.
  3. The z-score also reacts to real, fast but normal changes (a vent opening): the rolling mean lags behind, as in any moving average.

Hampel identifier (median and MAD)(A4)
\[ \begin{gathered} m_k = \operatorname{median}_{i \in W_k}\tilde x_i, \qquad \text{MAD}_k = \operatorname{median}_{i \in W_k}\left|\tilde x_i - m_k\right| \\[6pt] \text{alarm if}\quad \frac{|\tilde x_k - m_k|}{\max(1.4826\,\text{MAD}_k,\ \sigma_j)} > k^{*} \end{gathered} \]

replace the mean by the median and the standard deviation by 1.4826 times the median absolute deviation, both over the previous window W; alarm if the robust distance exceeds k*.

Follow the derivation of 1.4826
  1. For Gaussian data with standard deviation σ, \(|X - \mu|\) exceeds \(c\) with probability \(2(1 - \Phi(c/\sigma))\).
  2. The MAD is the median of \(|X - \mu|\): the value exceeded with probability ½. So \(2(1 - \Phi(\text{MAD}/\sigma)) = \tfrac12\), i.e. \(\Phi(\text{MAD}/\sigma) = \tfrac34\).
  3. Hence \(\text{MAD} = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.75) = 0.6745\,\sigma\) and \(\sigma = \text{MAD}/0.6745 = 1.4826\,\text{MAD}\).
  4. Because the median and the MAD have a 50 % breakdown point, up to half the window can be faulty before they are pulled away — the reason the Hampel identifier does not suffer from masking (Hampel, 1974; Pearson, 2002; Leys et al., 2013).
Assumptions and validity

On a signal that is changing steadily (a ramp), the trailing median lags behind the current value, which raises the robust distance; windows must be short compared with the natural time scales. On a quantised, almost constant signal, the MAD can be zero — hence the floor at the sensor noise σj.

Persistence (stuck-sensor) test(A5)
\[ s_k^{(w_s)} = \operatorname{SD}\left\{\tilde x_{k-w_s+1}, \dots, \tilde x_k\right\}, \qquad \text{alarm if}\quad s_k^{(w_s)} < \frac{\sigma_j}{4} \]

compute the standard deviation of the last ws samples, including the current one; alarm if it is less than a quarter of the normal sensor noise.

\(w_s\)flat-line windowsamples (min)

Why a working sensor is never perfectly flat

Even when the measured quantity is constant, a working sensor shows noise of about σj (and, if σj is small compared with the resolution, occasional changes of one step). The sample standard deviation of w values of independent noise has a relative standard error of about \(1/\sqrt{2(w-1)}\): for w = 30 it is 0.13, so a value below σj/4 is practically impossible for a live sensor. A frozen value has an SD of exactly zero once the window contains only repeated values — the detection delay is therefore about ws.

Physical-consistency tests: dew point and oxygen saturation(A6)
\[ \begin{gathered} e_s(T) = 0.6108\exp\!\left(\frac{17.27\,T}{T + 237.3}\right) \\[6pt] \alpha = \ln\frac{(\text{RH}/100)\,e_s(T_p)}{0.6108}, \qquad T_d = \frac{237.3\,\alpha}{17.27 - \alpha} \\[6pt] \text{RH alarm if } T_d - T_p > \delta; \quad \text{otherwise T alarm if } T_d - \tilde T_{\text{air}} > \delta \\[6pt] \text{DO alarm if } \widetilde{\text{DO}} > \left(1.1 + \frac{\delta}{10}\right)\text{DO}_{\text{sat}}(T_w) \end{gathered} \]

compute the saturation vapour pressure from temperature, the actual vapour pressure from the humidity reading and the probe's own temperature, and invert the saturation curve to get the dew point; if the dew point exceeds the probe's own temperature by more than the tolerance δ, the humidity reading is impossible (RH above 100 %); if the humidity reading is plausible but its dew point exceeds the separately measured air temperature, the air-temperature sensor is suspect; the oxygen reading is inconsistent if it exceeds 1.1 + δ/10 times the saturation concentration at the water temperature.

\(\tilde T_{\text{air}}\)reading of the separate air-temperature sensor°C

Follow the derivation of the dew point
  1. Relative humidity is the ratio of the actual to the saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature: \(e = (\text{RH}/100)\,e_s(T_p)\).
  2. The dew point is the temperature at which this vapour pressure would saturate the air: \(e_s(T_d) = e\).
  3. Take logarithms of the Tetens-type formula: \(\ln(e/0.6108) = 17.27\,T_d/(T_d + 237.3) = \alpha\).
  4. Solve for \(T_d\): \(\alpha T_d + 237.3\alpha = 17.27\,T_d\), so \(T_d = 237.3\,\alpha/(17.27 - \alpha)\).
  5. Since \(\text{RH} \le 100\,\%\) implies \(T_d \le T\), a dew point clearly above the probe's own temperature \(T_p\) means the humidity element is wrong. If the humidity reading is plausible but its dew point lies above the separate air-temperature reading, the two instruments disagree about physics; the laboratory then blames the air-temperature sensor, because the probe passed its own check. With only two instruments this attribution is a judgement, not a proof — a third, redundant sensor would settle it.
Assumptions and validity

The humidity probe and the air-temperature sensor must see the same air (same height, same radiation shield); in a well-mixed greenhouse the difference is a few tenths of a degree, hence the tolerance. Near saturation (fog, dew on leaves at night) the test becomes sensitive and the tolerance matters. The DO test assumes no pure-oxygen injection; supersaturation of a few per cent is common in aerated tanks and in the light with algae, hence the factor 1.1.

\(17.27T/(T+237.3)\): °C/°C = dimensionless ✓; \(\alpha\) dimensionless, \(T_d\): °C ✓
Isolation-forest anomaly score(A7)
\[ \begin{gathered} s(x, \psi) = 2^{-\,\mathrm{E}[h(x)]/c(\psi)} \\[6pt] c(\psi) = 2H(\psi - 1) - \frac{2(\psi - 1)}{\psi}, \qquad H(i) \approx \ln i + 0.5772 \end{gathered} \]

the score is two to the power of minus the average path length of x over all trees, divided by the average path length expected for a random point in a tree grown on ψ samples; c(ψ) uses the harmonic number H.

SymbolMeaningHere
\(h(x)\)path length: number of splits from the root to the leaf where x ends, plus \(c(n)\) for a leaf still holding n training points–
\(\psi\)subsample size per tree256
trees, height limitnumber of trees; maximum depth \(\lceil\log_2\psi\rceil\)100; 8

Follow the reasoning
  1. Growing a tree on ψ points with random splits is equivalent to building a random binary search tree; the average depth of an unsuccessful search in such a tree is \(c(\psi)\) (Liu et al., 2008).
  2. If \(\mathrm{E}[h] = c(\psi)\), the point is as hard to isolate as a typical point: \(s = 2^{-1} = 0.5\).
  3. If \(\mathrm{E}[h] \to 0\), \(s \to 1\): isolated immediately, a clear anomaly. If \(\mathrm{E}[h] \to \psi - 1\), \(s \to 0\): deep inside a dense region.
  4. Example: ψ = 256 gives \(c = 2(\ln 255 + 0.5772) - 2\times255/256 = 10.24\); a point isolated after 3 splits on average scores \(2^{-3/10.24} = 0.82\).
Assumptions and validity

The forest learns "normal" from the training data (day 1); faults present during training are learned as normal, and conditions never seen in training (a cloudy day after a sunny one) can look anomalous. The score says that a sample is unusual, not why; attributing it to a cause still needs the simpler tests or an expert.

\(h\) and \(c\) are counts of splits: the exponent is dimensionless and \(0 < s < 1\) ✓
Precision, recall, F1 and detection delay(A8)
\[ P = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad R = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P + R}, \qquad \tau_e = t_{\text{first alarm}} - t_{0,e} \]

precision is the fraction of alarms that were faults, recall the fraction of faulty samples that raised an alarm, F1 their harmonic mean, and the detection delay of a fault event is the time from its start to the first alarm on that sensor while the fault lasts.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(TP, FP, FN, TN\)counts of sensor samples: faulty and alarmed; normal and alarmed; faulty and not alarmed; normal and not alarmed–

Why the harmonic mean?

The harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller of the two numbers: a detector with P = 1.0 and R = 0.1 has an arithmetic mean of 0.55 but F1 = 0.18. F1 is high only if both are high. Where one kind of error is more costly (a missed pump failure versus a false alarm at night), a weighted version \(F_\beta = (1+\beta^2)PR/(\beta^2P + R)\) is used.

Model assumptions

  • The six true signals come from a simple, deterministic-plus-random greenhouse model driven by clear-sky irradiance for 25 September at Västerås (59.6° N) with day-specific cloudiness (sunny, overcast, partly cloudy). Heating set points, vent opening, CO₂ enrichment, evapo-concentration, dosing and oxygen saturation are represented by first-order responses and simple rules; the values are plausible but illustrative, not measured.
  • Normal measurement noise is white and Gaussian with the stated σj, then rounded to the sensor resolution; the RH probe carries its own temperature sensor (noise 0.08 °C) used for the dew-point test.
  • Faults follow Eq. A1 and are labelled faulty for their whole duration; overlapping faults on the same sensor are applied in order, with stuck and dropout faults last.
  • All detectors are causal and are evaluated from 60 min after the start. A fault event counts as detected if the ensemble (or, in the coverage table, the single detector) raises an alarm on the faulty sensor while the fault lasts. Alarms in the 10 min after a fault ends (the recovery jump) are counted neither as detections nor as false alarms.
  • The consistency test first checks the humidity probe against its own temperature (RH above 100 % flags RH); only if that passes does it compare the dew point with the separate air-temperature sensor (flags T). The DO test uses fresh-water saturation at 1 atm.
  • The isolation forests (one per sensor, 100 trees, ψ = 256, height limit 8) are trained on day 1 only, which contains one dropout; features with missing values are skipped.
  • Sample-level metrics weight long faults more than short ones; event-level recall and delay complement them.
