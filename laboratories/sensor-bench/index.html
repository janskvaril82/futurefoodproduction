Sensor bench

Five sensors that every modern growing system depends on lie on an electronics bench next to an ESP32 microcontroller: an NTC thermistor probe, a capacitive humidity sensor, a quantum (PAR) sensor, an NDIR CO₂ module and a capacitive substrate-moisture probe. Click one and it opens up: watch heat flow into a thermistor bead, water molecules soak into a polymer, photons turn into electrons, CO₂ molecules swallow infrared light and an electric field reach into wet coir. Then follow its signal link by link — physical quantity, transducer output, conditioning circuit, ADC codes, firmware — to a number with an honest uncertainty.

Inside the quantum sensor. Photons from the LED panel scatter in the white diffuser dome (cosine correction), the filter absorbs ultraviolet and far-red photons (they flash and vanish) and passes 400–700 nm, and each photon absorbed in the silicon photodiode releases at most one electron: the photocurrent counts photons.
Inside the NDIR cell. The pulsed lamp sends broadband infrared along the gold-coated cavity to two thermopiles. Photons heading for the 4.26 µm (active) window can be absorbed by CO₂ molecules, which light up as they start to vibrate; the 3.91 µm reference window sees the same lamp but no absorption. The fraction absorbed follows the Beer–Lambert law.
Inside the moisture probe. The pot is cut in half. The copper electrode under the solder mask and the ground plane form a capacitor whose fringing field (moving dots) reaches about a centimetre into the substrate. Water (blue, filling the finest pores first) has a permittivity of ≈ 80 against ≈ 1 for air and ≈ 4 for fibres, so the capacitance — and the output voltage — tracks the water content.

A guided tour of the bench

The bench is a faithful model of a student logger: an ESP32 development board on a solderless breadboard, a small OLED display that shows the latest reading, a 10 kΩ resistor and a 100 nF capacitor forming the thermistor input, and a multimeter whose red lead sits on the analogue input node, so its display always shows the voltage the ADC is converting. Everything you see is driven by the physical model in the Derive tab.

  1. Start with the NTC probe. Click the probe in the beaker (or choose NTC in the controls). The camera flies in and the sheath opens. Press Run a step test: the probe is lifted into the 22 °C air and plunged into the water. In the time-response chart the green curve (bead temperature) climbs exponentially; the amber point marks one time constant τ, where 63.2 % of the step is complete.
  2. Change the medium. Switch from Stirred water to Still air and repeat the step test. τ grows from about 5 s to about 5 min — the same probe, the same thermistor. The time constant belongs to the probe in its medium, not to the sensor alone.
  3. Compare the firmware equations. Set the water to 2 °C and switch between β model and Steinhart–Hart. In the error chart the β model is exact at 25 °C and 50 °C (where β was defined) but 0.3 K high at 0 °C; the calibrated Steinhart–Hart equation removes that model error.
  4. Starve the ADC. Reduce the resolution to 8 bits with no noise: the error chart becomes a staircase, and the resolution readout shows each step. Now add 3 mV of noise and average ×64 — the staircase dissolves (dither) and the random error shrinks by √64 = 8.
  5. Open the humidity module. A magnified cross-section appears. Raise the relative humidity and watch water molecules migrate from the air into the polymer; then set Sensor warmer than the air to 2 K and see the molecules leave — a warm sensor reads a lower relative humidity.
  6. Open the quantum sensor. The diffuser and filter lift off. Violet and dark-red photons (ultraviolet and far-red) are stopped by the filter; the others reach the photodiode and release electrons. Lower the transimpedance resistor and the voltage drops; raise it to 1 MΩ and the ADC saturates above ≈ 440 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  7. Open the NDIR cell. Increase CO₂ from 400 to 5000 ppm: more molecules, more absorption flashes, and the active detector bar shrinks while the reference bar stays put. Then reduce the lamp output to 85 % with Divide by the reference channel switched off — the reading jumps by more than 1000 ppm.
  8. Open the moisture probe. The pot is cut in half: water fills the finest pores first. Move θ from 0.1 to 0.7 and watch the output voltage flatten at the wet end. Switch the firmware to Air–water % — the popular two-point method — and read the error chart.
Key idea

Every reading is the end of a chain: measurand → sensing element → transducer output → conditioning → ADC → firmware. Each link adds its own error, lag or noise, and the weakest link decides what the number is worth. Usually that link is not the ADC but the physics of the sensor, its calibration and its dynamics.

One chain, five kinds of physics

Whatever it measures, an electronic sensor turns a physical quantity into a small electrical change, and the rest of the chain converts that change into a trustworthy number. The bench makes each stage visible, with the notation used throughout the Derive tab.

The thermistor: a resistance that falls 4 % per kelvin

An NTC thermistor is a sintered metal-oxide semiconductor whose resistance falls roughly exponentially with temperature. The β equation captures the exponential with one constant; real parts deviate slightly, which the three-constant Steinhart–Hart equation absorbs (Steinhart & Hart, 1968). The probe on the bench is the thermistor calibrated in Lesson 9.1 (Worked example 2): 33.1 kΩ at 0 °C, 10.0 kΩ at 25 °C and 3.59 kΩ at 50 °C. Its β between 25 and 50 °C is exactly 3950 K, but between 0 and 25 °C it is 3899 K — hence the 0.3 K error of the nominal β model near freezing.

Two thermal effects matter. First, the probe must itself change temperature: its heat capacity (steel sheath plus epoxy, ≈ 1.5 J K⁻¹) is charged through the thermal conductance \(hA\) to the medium, so \(\tau = mc/(hA)\). Second, the divider current heats the bead: the power \(P = V^2/R_T\) raises it by \(P/\delta\) above the medium. In this single-node model the dissipation constant \(\delta\) is the same conductance \(hA\) that sets τ, so a probe that responds slowly also self-heats more.

The humidity sensor: counting water molecules with a capacitor

A thin hygroscopic polymer between two electrodes absorbs water until its water content is in equilibrium with the relative humidity at its own temperature. Water (\(\varepsilon_r \approx 80\)) raises the permittivity of the dry polymer (\(\varepsilon_r \approx 3\text{--}4\)), so the capacitance \(C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d\) rises with RH (Farahani et al., 2014). The element modelled here follows the published polynomial of the HS1101LF: 161.6 pF at 0 %RH, 180 pF at 55 %RH and 193.1 pF at 100 %RH, about 0.31 pF per %RH (Measurement Specialties, 2012). A charge amplifier turns the capacitance difference from a reference capacitor into a voltage.

Because the polymer responds to relative humidity at its own temperature, a sensor that is 2 K warmer than the air sees the same water vapour pressure but a 13 % higher saturation vapour pressure, and at 80 %RH reads about 71 %RH — a 9 %RH error that no amount of ADC resolution can fix.

The quantum sensor: a photon counter

In a silicon photodiode each absorbed photon with enough energy can create one electron–hole pair; the short-circuit current therefore counts photons, not joules — exactly what a photon flux density needs. A diffuser makes the response follow the cosine law, and a filter flattens the spectral response over 400–700 nm (Barnes et al., 1993; Ross & Sulev, 2000). The model uses a sensitivity of 7.5 µA per 1000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, in the 5–10 µA range specified for the LI-190R (LI-COR, n.d.). A transimpedance amplifier holds the photodiode at zero volts and converts the current to \(V = I R_f\).

The NDIR cell: CO₂ casts an infrared shadow

CO₂ absorbs strongly at 4.26 µm (asymmetric stretch). A pulsed lamp shines through a reflective cavity onto two thermopiles behind narrow filters: one at 4.26 µm, one at 3.91 µm where nothing absorbs (Hodgkinson & Tatam, 2013). The active signal falls as \(e^{-kcL}\); dividing by the reference cancels lamp ageing, dirt and detector drift. Because absorption depends on the number of molecules per volume, not on the mole fraction, a sensor calibrated at sea level reads low at altitude or on a low-pressure day unless it is compensated. The model uses the SCD30 class of performance: dual channel, τ₆₃ = 20 s, accuracy ±(30 ppm + 3 %) (Sensirion, 2020). Its absorption coefficient and path length are illustrative effective values that give about 5 % absorption at 1000 ppm.

The capacitive moisture probe: a fringing field in the root zone

The popular v1.2 probe is a printed-circuit board: a copper electrode next to a ground plane, both under solder mask. Their electric field fringes about a centimetre into the medium, so the probe capacitance depends on the bulk permittivity \(\varepsilon_a\) of the substrate, which the complex refractive index model (CRIM) builds from the volume fractions of water, solids and air (Birchak et al., 1974; Roth et al., 1990). On the board a TLC555 timer oscillates at about 1.5 MHz with a 34 % duty cycle (Mallon, 2020); the square wave charges the probe through 10 kΩ, and a diode peak detector outputs a DC voltage that falls as the capacitance rises — about 3.0 V in air and 1.5 V in water at 3.3 V supply. Two features make the calibration strongly non-linear: the solder mask acts as a capacitor in series with the medium, and the RC charging saturates when the probe capacitance is large.

The ADC and the firmware

A successive-approximation ADC divides its reference into \(2^N\) steps. Rounding to a step adds an error spread uniformly over one LSB (standard deviation LSB/√12). The ESP32's converter is nominally 12-bit but has a data-sheet integral nonlinearity of up to ±12 LSB (Espressif Systems, 2025), which the ESP32-like nonlinearity switch represents as a smooth bow. Noise larger than about half an LSB dithers the converter: averaging many conversions then recovers resolution finer than one LSB. Finally the firmware inverts the chain; if it uses the wrong equation or the wrong constants, the error is systematic — it will be the same tomorrow and cannot be averaged away.

Common misconception

“A 16-bit ADC makes my sensor accurate.” Resolution is the smallest change the chain can register, not the closeness of the reading to the truth. On the bench, the PAR sensor's ±5 % calibration dwarfs the quantisation error of even an 8-bit converter, and a warm humidity sensor is 9 %RH wrong whatever the ADC.

Systematic versus random

The budget chart follows the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (JCGM, 2008): random effects and effects known only as limits (tolerances, calibration uncertainty) are expressed as standard uncertainties and added in quadrature; known systematic errors should be corrected. The lab shows them separately so that you can see what calibration or a better equation would remove.

The equations behind the bench

Each sensor is modelled as element state \(y\) → transducer output \(q\) → ADC input voltage \(V\) → code → reading \(\hat x\). Temperatures in the equations are absolute (K) unless the symbol table says °C.

Thermistor: Steinhart–Hart truth, β-model firmware and the exact inverse(SB1)
\[ \frac{1}{T} = A + B\ln R + C(\ln R)^3,\qquad \frac{1}{T_\beta} = \frac{1}{T_{25}} + \frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{R}{R_{25}},\qquad \ln R = \sqrt[3]{y - \tfrac{x}{2}} - \sqrt[3]{y + \tfrac{x}{2}} \]

the calibrated probe obeys the Steinhart–Hart equation; the data-sheet firmware uses the β model instead; to find the resistance at a given temperature, the cubic in the logarithm of R is solved exactly with Cardano's formula, where x equals (A minus 1/T) divided by C and y equals the square root of (B/3C) cubed plus x squared over four.

Follow the derivation of the inverse
  1. Write \(L = \ln R\). Steinhart–Hart becomes \(C L^3 + B L + (A - 1/T) = 0\), a cubic with no square term.
  2. Divide by \(C\): \(L^3 + pL + q = 0\) with \(p = B/C > 0\) and \(q = x = (A - 1/T)/C\).
  3. Because \(p > 0\) the cubic has exactly one real root. Cardano's formula gives \(L = \sqrt[3]{-q/2 + \sqrt{q^2/4 + p^3/27}} + \sqrt[3]{-q/2 - \sqrt{q^2/4 + p^3/27}}\).
  4. With \(y = \sqrt{(B/3C)^3 + x^2/4} = \sqrt{p^3/27 + q^2/4}\) this is \(L = \sqrt[3]{y - x/2} - \sqrt[3]{y + x/2}\), and \(R = e^L\). Check: \(T = 298.15\) K returns 10 000.0 Ω.
  5. The β-model error is then \(T_\beta(R(T)) - T\): zero at 25 °C and 50 °C, +0.30 K at 0 °C and +0.14 K at 10 °C for this probe.
Assumptions and validity

The three calibration points (0, 25, 50 °C) define the "true" curve; between and slightly beyond them Steinhart–Hart is accurate to millikelvins (Steinhart & Hart, 1968). Below 0 °C and above 50 °C the model extrapolates. Interchangeability of a nominal part is taken as ±1 % in \(R_{25}\) and ±1 % in β, a common tolerance class.

\(\ln R\) is dimensionless, so \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) all carry K⁻¹ and \(1/T\) is in K⁻¹ ✓; in the β model \(\ln(R/R_{25})/\beta\) is in K⁻¹ ✓
Voltage divider and the ratiometric ADC code(SB2)
\[ V = V_s\,\frac{R_T}{R_f + R_T},\qquad \text{code} = \left\lfloor 2^N\,\frac{V}{V_{\text{ref}}}\right\rfloor \overset{V_{\text{ref}} = V_s}{=} \left\lfloor 2^N \frac{R_T}{R_f + R_T}\right\rfloor,\qquad \hat R_T = R_f\,\frac{\hat V}{V_{s,\text{nom}} - \hat V} \]

the voltage across the thermistor is the supply times the thermistor's share of the total resistance; if the ADC uses the same supply as its reference, the code depends only on the resistance ratio; the firmware inverts the divider using the nominal supply voltage.

First-order dynamics and self-heating of the probe(SB3)
\[ mc\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = hA\,(x - y) + P \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \tau = \frac{mc}{hA},\qquad y_\infty = x + \frac{P}{\delta},\quad \delta = hA,\quad P = \frac{V^2}{R_T} \]

the heat stored in the probe changes by the heat convected from the medium plus the electrical power dissipated in the thermistor; hence the time constant is heat capacity over thermal conductance, and in the steady state the bead sits P over delta above the medium, where delta equals hA in this single-node model.

Assumptions and validity

A real probe has internal thermal resistance between sheath and bead, so its response is S-shaped at first (two or more first-order stages) and its self-heating is larger than \(P/(hA)\). The convection coefficients sit inside the textbook ranges for free and forced convection of gases and liquids (Bergman et al., 2011); the true value depends on flow speed and geometry. The other sensors use data-sheet time constants: 3 s for the humidity element, 20 s for the NDIR cell, < 1 µs for the photodiode, and an assumed 1 s electronic filter for the moisture probe.

Follow the derivation
  1. Beer–Lambert (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.8): transmission \(e^{-kcL}\); absorbance \(A = kcL\) is proportional to the number of molecules per volume.
  2. The ratio \(r = V_{\text{act}}/V_{\text{ref}} = (V_{0,\text{act}}/V_{0,\text{ref}})e^{-kcL}\) no longer contains \(\lambda\) — lamp ageing and dirt cancel.
  3. Take the logarithm: \(kcL = \ln(r_0/r)\) with \(r_0 = V_{0,\text{act}}/V_{0,\text{ref}}\) from the zero calibration; solve for \(c\).
  4. Convert to mole fraction with the ideal-gas law, \(x = cRT/P\). A single-beam sensor must assume \(V_{\text{ref}} = V_{0,\text{ref}}\); a 5 % weaker lamp then adds \(\ln(1/0.95)/(kL) = 0.043\) mol m⁻³ ≈ 1000 ppm.
  5. Fractional absorbance at 1000 ppm, 25 °C, 1013 hPa: \(c = 0.0409\) mol m⁻³, \(kcL = 0.049\), so 4.8 % of the active-channel photons are absorbed — the electronics must resolve changes of a few hundredths of a percent of the signal.
Assumptions and validity

Real NDIR sensors do not follow a single exponential because the filter band covers many absorption lines; manufacturers use empirical linearisation (Hodgkinson & Tatam, 2013). The effective \(k\) and \(L\) are illustrative values, not those of a specific product; the accuracy, temperature stability and τ₆₃ follow the SCD30 data sheet (Sensirion, 2020).

\(kcL\): m² mol⁻¹ × mol m⁻³ × m = 1 ✓; \(cRT/P\): mol m⁻³ × J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ × K ÷ Pa = J m⁻³ Pa⁻¹ = 1 ✓
Bulk permittivity of a moist substrate and the probe capacitance(SB8)
\[ \sqrt{\varepsilon_a} = \theta\sqrt{\varepsilon_w(T)} + (1-\phi)\sqrt{\varepsilon_s} + (\phi - \theta),\qquad \varepsilon_w = 87.740 - 0.40008\,t + 9.398\times10^{-4}t^2 - 1.410\times10^{-6}t^3,\qquad C = C_0 + \frac{C_c\,C_g\varepsilon_a}{C_c + C_g\varepsilon_a} \]

the square root of the bulk permittivity is the volume-weighted sum of the square roots of the permittivities of water, solid and air; water's permittivity falls with temperature; the probe capacitance is a stray part plus the coating capacitance in series with the medium's capacitance.

\(\theta, \phi\)volumetric water content; total porositym³ m⁻³φ: coir 0.94, stone wool 0.96, peat 0.90, loam 0.48
\(\varepsilon_w, \varepsilon_s\)relative permittivity of water (80.1 at 20 °C); of the solids (assumed)–εs = 3–5

Follow the derivation
  1. For a wave travelling through a layered mixture, travel times add: \(\sqrt{\varepsilon_a}\) (proportional to the refractive index) is the volume-weighted mean of the constituents' \(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\) — the CRIM mixing rule with exponent ½ (Birchak et al., 1974; Roth et al., 1990). Air has \(\varepsilon = 1\).
  2. Water's permittivity follows Malmberg and Maryott (1956): 80.1 at 20 °C, 78.3 at 25 °C — so a warmer substrate reads slightly drier.
  3. The medium sees the fringing field as a capacitor \(C_g\varepsilon_a\). The thin solder mask over the electrode is a capacitor \(C_c\) in series with it, and series capacitances combine as \(C_cC_m/(C_c + C_m)\), which approaches \(C_c\) in a very wet medium — the first reason the probe saturates.
  4. Example: coir at θ = 0.45 and 20 °C: \(\sqrt{\varepsilon_a} = 0.45\times8.95 + 0.06\times2 + 0.49 = 4.64\), \(\varepsilon_a = 21.5\), \(C = 2 + 45\times68.9/113.9 = 29.2\) pF.
Assumptions and validity

CRIM ignores bound water, dielectric losses and salinity, all of which matter in fertigated organic substrates; capacitance probes are known to over-read at high pore-water EC (Blonquist et al., 2005). \(C_0, C_g, C_c\) are chosen so that the probe gives about 3.0 V in air and 1.5 V in water, as reported for v1.2 probes (Mallon, 2020). Porosities are typical of the substrate classes (Raviv et al., 2019).

\(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\) terms are dimensionless volume-weighted sums ✓; \(C_cC_m/(C_c + C_m)\): pF²/pF = pF ✓
Square wave, RC charging and the peak detector of the moisture probe(SB9)
\[ V = V_s\,\frac{1 - q_1}{1 - q_0 q_1} - V_D,\qquad q_1 = \exp\!\left(-\frac{D}{fRC}\right),\quad q_0 = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1-D}{fRC}\right) \]

the output is the peak voltage that the probe capacitor reaches in the periodic steady state of a square wave, minus the diode drop; q one and q zero are the exponential decays during the high and the low part of each period.

\(V_s\)oscillator amplitude (regulated supply)V3.3
\(f, D\)oscillator frequency and duty cycle (TLC555)Hz, –1.5 × 10⁶, 0.34
\(R\)series resistorΩ10 000

Follow the derivation
  1. While the oscillator is high (duration \(D/f\)) the capacitor charges towards \(V_s\): \(v_H = V_s + (v_L - V_s)q_1\).
  2. While it is low (duration \((1-D)/f\)) it discharges towards 0: \(v_L = v_H q_0\).
  3. Substitute: \(v_H = V_s(1 - q_1) + v_H q_0 q_1\), so \(v_H = V_s(1 - q_1)/(1 - q_0q_1)\). The diode and hold capacitor keep the peak, minus \(V_D\).
  4. Limits: \(C \to 0\): \(q \to 0\), \(v_H \to V_s\) (air: ≈ 3.0 V). \(C \to \infty\): \(v_H \to DV_s\), the average of the square wave (1.12 V + wiring) — the second reason for the flat wet end.
Assumptions and validity

Ideal square wave and diode, no load on the hold capacitor. The frequency and duty cycle follow measurements on v1.2 boards (Mallon, 2020); the diode drop is an assumption. Temperature drift of the oscillator and diode is not modelled; field loggers show a daily "thermal wobble" in exactly these probes (Mallon, 2020).

\(D/(fRC)\): 1 ÷ (s⁻¹ × Ω × F) = 1 ÷ (s⁻¹ × s) = 1 ✓; V × (1 − q)/(1 − q q) = V ✓
Quantisation, noise and averaging(SB10)
\[ \text{LSB} = \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{2^N},\qquad u_q = \frac{\text{LSB}}{\sqrt{12}},\qquad u_{\bar V} = \frac{\sigma_V}{\sqrt{n}}\ \ (\text{and } \frac{u_q}{\sqrt n}\ \text{if}\ \sigma_V \gtrsim \tfrac12\text{LSB}),\qquad u_x = \frac{u_V}{|S|},\ S = \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x} \]

one least-significant bit is the reference divided by two to the power N; rounding adds a uniform error with standard deviation LSB over root twelve; averaging n noisy conversions divides the random part by root n — and also the quantisation part if the noise is large enough to dither; a voltage uncertainty becomes a measurand uncertainty through the sensitivity of the chain.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(N, V_{\text{ref}}\)ADC bits and full scale–, V
\(\sigma_V\)rms noise at the ADC input (slider)V
\(n\)conversions averaged per reading–
\(S\)sensitivity of the ADC input to the measurandV per unit
Follow the derivation
  1. Without noise, a slowly varying input is always rounded the same way: the error is deterministic and bounded by ±½ LSB (the staircase in the error chart).
  2. Treated as uniformly distributed over one LSB, its variance is \(\text{LSB}^2/12\) (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.4).
  3. With Gaussian noise \(\sigma_V\), each conversion lands on neighbouring codes with probabilities that depend on where the true voltage lies between them; the mean code of many conversions therefore interpolates between codes (dithering), and both noise and quantisation fall as \(1/\sqrt n\).
  4. Divide by the local sensitivity to express everything in measurand units, e.g. 0.806 mV ÷ 37.4 mV K⁻¹ = 0.022 K per LSB for the thermistor at 20 °C.
Assumptions and validity

Ideal converter apart from an optional smooth integral-nonlinearity bow of ±12 LSB (at 12 bit, 3.3 V), the data-sheet figure for the ESP32 (Espressif Systems, 2025); real INL curves are irregular and differ between chips, and the ESP32 also has ±7 LSB differential nonlinearity. Averaging assumes independent (white) noise.

LSB/|S|: V ÷ (V per unit) = unit of the measurand ✓
Error budget: propagation through the firmware(SB11)
\[ u_c(\hat x) = \sqrt{\sum_i u_{B,i}^2 + \sum_j \left(\frac{\partial \hat x}{\partial V_j}\right)^{2}\!\left(u_{q,j}^2 + u_{\bar V,j}^2\right)},\qquad U = 2\,u_c,\qquad b = \sum_k b_k \]

the combined standard uncertainty is the root-sum-square of the Type B components from data sheets and of the ADC-channel uncertainties multiplied by the sensitivity of the firmware result to each channel voltage; the expanded uncertainty uses a coverage factor of two; the known but uncorrected systematic errors are summed separately.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(u_{B,i}\)Type B standard uncertainties: tolerance or calibration limit \(a\) as \(a/\sqrt3\) (rectangular)measurand unit
\(\partial\hat x/\partial V_j\)sensitivity coefficient of the firmware to channel \(j\) (numerical derivative)unit per V
\(b_k\)systematic errors: model error, self-heating, warm sensor, uncompensated pressure, lamp ageing, INL…measurand unit
\(U\)expanded uncertainty, ≈ 95 % coverage for a near-normal resultmeasurand unit
Follow the derivation
  1. The reading is a function of the channel voltages, \(\hat x = f(V_1, V_2, \dots)\). A small error \(\delta V_j\) causes \(\delta\hat x \approx (\partial f/\partial V_j)\delta V_j\) (first-order Taylor expansion).
  2. For independent errors the variances add: \(u^2(\hat x) = \sum_j (\partial f/\partial V_j)^2u^2(V_j)\) — the law of propagation of uncertainty (JCGM, 2008; Lesson 9.3, Eq. 9.3.3).
  3. A data-sheet limit \(\pm a\) with no further information is a rectangular distribution with standard deviation \(a/\sqrt3\).
  4. The NDIR sensor has two channels: noise on each is propagated through the ratio, which is why the noise contribution is \(\sqrt2\) larger than for one channel with the same signal.
Assumptions and validity

Components are treated as uncorrelated; the budget is evaluated in the steady state (the dynamic error of a changing measurand is shown separately in the readouts). Systematic errors are listed with their sign because a well-designed measurement corrects them rather than widening the uncertainty.

(unit per V × V)² + unit² → unit² under the root ✓

Model assumptions

  • Dynamics. Every sensing element is a single first-order lag (Eq. SB3), integrated exactly. The NTC time constant follows from the probe's heat capacity and a convection coefficient per medium; the others use data-sheet values (humidity element 3 s, NDIR τ₆₃ 20 s, photodiode < 1 µs). The 1 s time constant of the moisture probe's output filter is an assumption. Real probes show S-shaped (higher-order) responses.
  • NTC probe. The "true" R(T) is the Steinhart–Hart curve of the probe calibrated in Lesson 9.1; the nominal part is 10 kΩ / β 3950 K with ±1 % tolerances. Self-heating uses δ = hA, which underestimates the self-heating of a potted bead. The supply is assumed to be 3.27 V against a nominal 3.30 V.
  • Humidity element. HS1101LF polynomial and tolerances; "this unit" has C₅₅ = 181.2 pF (inside the 177–183 pF range). Hysteresis is treated as an uncertainty, not simulated as a history.
  • Quantum sensor. Ideal linear photodiode with LI-190R-type specifications; spectral mismatch and cosine errors are not simulated; the photon animation shows a sampled stream, not the true photon rate (≈ 10¹⁵ s⁻¹ on the detector).
  • NDIR module. Pure Beer–Lambert absorption with an illustrative effective absorption coefficient (20 m² mol⁻¹) and path (6 cm) — about 5 % absorption at 1000 ppm; ideal-gas conversion; dual-channel ratio. The absorption flashes in the animation occur with the model's true fractional absorbance.
  • Moisture probe. CRIM mixing, Malmberg–Maryott permittivity of water, a series coating capacitance and an ideal square-wave RC peak detector with parameters tuned to the reported 3.0 V (air) and 1.5 V (water). Salinity, dielectric losses, bound water and air gaps around the probe are ignored. The "substrate curve" firmware is the exact inverse of the model at 20 °C, i.e. a perfect calibration apart from its assumed ±0.02 m³ m⁻³ uncertainty.
  • ADC. Ideal N-bit converter with Gaussian input noise, optional smooth INL bow (±12 LSB at 12 bit), averaging of independent conversions, and reconstruction at code centres. Reference voltages other than the ratiometric supply are assumed exact.
  • Uncertainty. Data-sheet limits are converted to standard uncertainties with a rectangular distribution; components are uncorrelated; systematic errors are reported separately and not included in uc.
  • The 3D models are faithful in layout and proportion but simplified; the physics animations are driven by the model (molecule numbers, photon rates, currents, field-line density) and scaled for visibility where stated.
