Aeroponic root chamber

A high-pressure aeroponic unit, cut open. A pump charges an accumulator, a timer opens a solenoid for a few seconds every few minutes, and four stainless nozzles fill a dark chamber with mist. Whether the mist actually reaches the roots is decided by droplet physics — settling, evaporation and inertial impaction — and whether the roots stay alive between pulses is decided by a film of water only a few hundredths of a millimetre thick. Tune the spray, starve the roots, clog a nozzle or cut the power, and watch the root curtain respond.

What to look at first

  1. Watch one misting cycle in real time. Set Simulated time per real second to 1 s and press Root zone. When the amber LED on the timer lights, the solenoid opens: jets of mist rise from the four nozzles, the chamber fogs up and the root film jumps to 100 %. For a few seconds water drips from the root tips — that is drainage, the part of the deposit the root surface cannot hold. Then the film declines slowly as the plant drinks. Switch back to 2 min per second to see many cycles as a saw-tooth in the first chart.
  2. Change the droplet size. Raise the pressure to 10 bar and shrink the orifice to 0.1 mm: the Sauter mean diameter falls below 15 µm, the mist becomes a fog that hangs in the chamber, and in the second chart the magenta deposition curve shows that the finest droplets are simply not captured. Now try 2 bar and 0.8 mm: coarse droplets rain onto the roots and the chamber floor within a second, but the large orifice sprays more than four times as much solution per plant (Solution sprayed per plant). Shorten the pulse to 1 s so that it sprays about as much as before, and Root surface hit per pulse falls — fewer, bigger droplets wet fewer spots.
  3. Aim the spray. The two rows of plants hang their roots on either side of the nozzle line. With a 40° cone most of the mist shoots up the gap between the two root curtains; with a 150° cone it wets the walls. Find the cone angle that maximises Spray deposited on roots.
  4. Clog a nozzle. Choose the preset Clogged nozzle and switch the root colours to Film map. The two plants above nozzle 2 turn red: with a narrow cone their neighbours' nozzles cannot help them. Widen the cone and watch the overlap rescue them.
  5. Cut the power. Press Cut the power. The pump and the timer stop, the solenoid closes, the chamber humidity drifts towards 85 % and the root film evaporates. Within minutes the fine roots lose their sheen, turn beige and — when their water content falls below 70 % — brown; the leaves wilt. Restart, tick Controller on a UPS and cut the power again: now the accumulator keeps spraying, pulse after pulse, and the pressure gauge steps down until the gas pre-charge is reached.
  6. Compare with other systems. In the third chart, the water the plant takes up is the same in aeroponics, NFT and deep-water culture. What aeroponics really changes is how little water it keeps around the roots — millilitres instead of litres.
Key idea

An aeroponic system delivers water in two steps that can each fail: droplets must reach and stick to the roots (droplet size, air speed, cone geometry), and the resulting film must last until the next pulse (film capacity versus uptake and evaporation). The engineering is a balance between a spray fine enough to cover every root hair and coarse enough to arrive, and a timer that pulses often enough for a buffer of a few millilitres per plant.

The route from nozzle to root hair

This laboratory turns the theory of Lesson 7.1 into a working machine. A diaphragm pump pressurises nutrient solution into a bladder accumulator; a pressure switch restarts the pump when the pressure falls below the set value and stops it 1.5 bar higher. A timer opens a normally-closed solenoid valve for \(t_{\text{on}}\) seconds every \(t_{\text{off}}\) minutes, and four pressure-swirl nozzles atomise the solution into a hollow cone of droplets. Every droplet then has three possible fates (Figure 1).

Why there is a best droplet size

Two opposite requirements set the window of high-pressure aeroponics. Arrival favours large droplets: a droplet is captured by a root only if its inertia keeps it moving straight when the air bends around the root — a large Stokes number, which grows with the square of the diameter. Droplets below about 5 µm follow the air, settle at a fraction of a millimetre per second and, unless the chamber is saturated, evaporate within a second. Coverage favours small droplets: the same litre of solution divided into 10 µm droplets contains a thousand times more droplets than in 100 µm droplets, so it touches far more of the root surface. The simulator counts the droplets that land on each root system in one pulse and uses Poisson statistics to find the fraction of the root surface they hit; only that fraction can hold a water film. With the default nozzle (D₃₂ ≈ 30 µm) nearly every square millimetre of root is hit in each pulse; with 100 µm droplets carrying the same volume only about two thirds is.

The root film is the water tank

Between pulses the plant lives on the film of solution that clings to its roots and root hairs — about 2 mL per lettuce plant in this model (0.08 m² of root surface × 25 µm). The film is drained by the plant's own uptake (≈ 80 mL per day, 0.93 µL per second) and, if the chamber air is not saturated, by evaporation from the root surface. Water above the retention capacity drips off within tens of seconds. As long as the film stays above a quarter of its capacity the plant can take up water freely; below that, uptake is limited and the root water-stress index (the fraction of the plant's demand that is not met) rises above zero. When the film has gone, the plant starts to draw water out of its own fine roots, and root tissue exposed to unsaturated air dries out. Fine roots that lose more than 30 % of their water are assumed to be irreversibly damaged.

A buffer measured in minutes

A lettuce plant in soil or in deep-water culture has days of water around its roots; in an aeroponic chamber it has minutes. That is why the Pump failure: roots damaged after readout matters as much as any growth figure. Redundancy — an accumulator, a UPS on the controller, a second pump, an alarm that reaches a person — is part of the physiology of an aeroponic crop.

Nutrients travel with the water

Each deposited millilitre carries nutrients at the solution concentration. The plant takes them up mostly by mass flow — with the water it absorbs — so the nitrogen uptake is the water uptake times the concentration. Most of the nitrogen sprayed onto the roots drains back to the reservoir. Evaporation from the root film removes water but leaves the salts behind, so in a leaky, unsaturated chamber the film becomes more concentrated between pulses — one reason why long pauses and dry chamber air are a bad combination.

How much water does aeroponics really save?

The third chart answers the popular claim that aeroponics uses a tiny fraction of the water of other systems (Lesson 7.1, misconception box). In any closed, recirculating system nearly all the water leaves through the plant. Aeroponics can save the water thrown away at solution changes because it needs little solution in circulation, and it loses almost nothing by evaporation in a sealed chamber — but it gives up almost all of the water buffer around the roots.

Log-normal spray and its mean diameters(A1)
\[ \begin{gathered} f_N(\ln d) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\ln\sigma_g}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\ln d-\ln \text{CMD})^2}{2\ln^2\sigma_g}\right] \\ D_{32}=\text{CMD}\,e^{2.5\ln^2\sigma_g},\qquad D_{30}=\text{CMD}\,e^{1.5\ln^2\sigma_g} \end{gathered} \]

the logarithm of the droplet diameter is normally distributed around the logarithm of the count median diameter with standard deviation \(\ln\sigma_g\); every mean diameter follows from these two numbers. The simulator is given \(D_{32}\) by the nozzle and \(\sigma_g\) by you, and back-calculates the count median.

Terminal velocity with inertia correction and time in the air(A3)
\[ v_t = \frac{(\rho_p-\rho_a)\,g\,d^2}{18\,\mu\,\left(1+0.15\,\text{Re}^{0.687}\right)},\qquad \text{Re}=\frac{\rho_a v_t d}{\mu},\qquad t_{\text{fall}} = \frac{H}{v_t} \]

the settling velocity is Stokes' law divided by a drag-correction factor that grows with the droplet Reynolds number; because Re itself contains the velocity, the equation is solved by iteration. A droplet that is not captured spends a time equal to the chamber height divided by its settling velocity in the air.

Model assumptions

  • Eight butterhead lettuce plants in two rows; four identical nozzles on the centre line, one under each pair. Each nozzle's deposit is shared between plants according to a Gaussian footprint whose width grows with the cone angle; losses at the chamber ends are neglected.
  • Droplet sizes are log-normal. D₃₂ follows the pressure-swirl scaling of Eq. A2, calibrated to 30 µm for a 0.3 mm nozzle at 6 bar; the spray air speed at the roots (0.6 m s⁻¹ for the reference nozzle) scales with the square root of the jet momentum flux. Both calibrations are assumptions chosen to reproduce the 5–50 µm range and the 20–50 % deposition used in Lesson 7.1.
  • Capture by the roots uses the potential-flow impaction efficiency of a single cylinder (Israel & Rosner, 1982) for a curtain of 1.5 root layers of 0.5 mm roots; settling droplets that were not captured land on roots with probability 0.35. Diffusion, interception and electrostatic effects are neglected (they matter below about 1 µm).
  • Evaporation follows the d²-law at the chamber humidity with the droplet at air temperature (no wet-bulb cooling), which slightly overestimates evaporation.
  • The root film holds 25 µm on 0.08 m² of roots per plant (Lesson 7.1), but only on the fraction of the surface hit by droplets in a pulse (Poisson coverage with a spread factor of 2). Uptake is unrestricted above 25 % of capacity; water above capacity drains with a 30 s time constant.
  • Fine-root desiccation is a bucket model (9 g of tissue water per plant, irreversible damage below 70 % relative water content). It illustrates time scales and is not a physiological prediction.
  • After a power failure the chamber humidity relaxes to the chosen value with a 5 min time constant. The accumulator is isothermal (Boyle); rapid discharge is closer to adiabatic and delivers somewhat less.
  • NFT and deep-water culture in the water chart are illustrative designs: 3 and 10 L of solution per plant, 5 and 3 mL d⁻¹ of evaporation, a 1.5 L min⁻¹ NFT channel serving 20 plants; all systems replace their solution every 21 days.
  • The 3D chamber has a transparent front for teaching; real root chambers are opaque, because light on roots and solution promotes algae and pathogens.
