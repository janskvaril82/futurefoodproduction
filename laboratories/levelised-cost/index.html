Levelised cost of food

What does a kilogram of lettuce really cost? Not what the till says, but what the producer must earn on every kilogram, for the whole life of the farm, to pay for the building, the lamps, the electricity, the people, the seeds and the packaging — and to give the investors the return they expect. This laboratory builds the complete discounted cash-flow model behind that number, the levelised cost of food, for a vertical farm, a heated greenhouse and an open field, with literature defaults, a Swedish wage option, sensitivity and Monte Carlo analyses — the same definition as Lesson 15.3.

What to look at first

  1. Read the stage from left to right. Six streams of money — capital, energy, labour, inputs, packaging and logistics, and overheads — flow into a crate that holds one kilogram of lettuce. Their widths are the euros per kilogram they contribute; together they make the levelised cost of food (LCOF) printed on the crate. The price tag underneath shows whether the market pays enough.
  2. Watch the timeline. In year 0 the farm is built: a large red bar. Each year then brings revenue (green) and operating costs (amber); every ten years the lamps and the automation are replaced. The light ghost bars are the money as it is paid, the solid bars what it is worth today, shrunk by the discount factor \((1+r)^{-t}\). The magenta line — cumulative discounted cash flow — crosses zero at the discounted payback time and ends at the net present value.
  3. Apply the preset Lesson 15.3 check. The laboratory reproduces Worked example 3 of Lesson 15.3 (Zhuang et al., 2022): €7.16 kg⁻¹, which is $7.54 kg⁻¹ at the 2022 exchange rate. Then choose Swedish vertical farm: with Swedish labour costs, ramp-up years, 5 % unsold produce and replacement of LEDs and automation, the cost rises to about €8.0 kg⁻¹ and labour becomes the largest item.
  4. Switch the system. A heated Nordic greenhouse makes lettuce for about €6 kg⁻¹, the California field for about €0.92 kg⁻¹. In the field, harvest, packing and transport are more than half of the cost; in the vertical farm, capital, electricity and labour dominate. The comparison chart shows all three side by side.
  5. Read the tornado. For the Swedish vertical farm the most influential assumptions are the labour hours, the capital cost, the electricity price and the share of unsold produce — not the water, which costs less than one cent per kilogram.
  6. Look at the Monte Carlo. The distribution is skewed to the right: most of the published uncertainty ranges extend further towards higher costs than lower ones, so the median LCOF is higher than the base case. For the Swedish vertical farm only about half of the draws beat the Japanese market price used as default.
Key idea

The levelised cost of food is the constant price per kilogram at which the project exactly recovers all its costs, including the return investors require. It turns a building paid today, lamps paid every ten years and wages paid every month into one comparable number per kilogram — the same logic as the levelised cost of electricity used to compare power plants.

Why "levelised"?

A euro today is worth more than a euro in ten years: it could be invested, and there is a risk that the future euro never arrives. Investors therefore discount future money with a rate \(r\): €1 in year \(t\) is worth \((1+r)^{-t}\) euros today. At 5 % a euro in year 10 is worth €0.61, in year 15 €0.48. Discounting explains two features of food-system economics. First, capital-intensive systems — plant factories, heated greenhouses — are much more sensitive to the cost of capital than open fields. Second, output that arrives late (slow ramp-up) is worth less than output that arrives early.

The LCOF divides the present value of all costs by the present value of all kilograms sold. Discounting the kilograms may look odd — lettuce does not lose value by waiting — but it follows directly from asking "which constant price makes the net present value zero?": the revenue earned by a kilogram sold in year \(t\) is discounted, so the kilogram is too (Eq. LC2).

The difference between the vertical farm and the field is roughly a factor of nine. Much of it is the price of replacing sunlight and soil with electricity, buildings and technology; some of it reflects different products (small, clean, pesticide-free heads harvested with roots versus wrapped iceberg heads packed in the field). Nicholson et al. (2020) found that even after transport to the East Coast of the USA, field lettuce from California cost less than half as much as lettuce from local greenhouses and plant factories. Eaves and Eaves (2018), on the other hand, simulated a greenhouse and a vertical farm near Quebec City and found similar costs to equip and run — in a cold, dark climate the greenhouse also needs heat and lamps.

Handle the defaults with care

The capital costs of Nordic high-tech greenhouses and the split of plant-factory investment into building, racks, lamps, climate system and automation are not published consistently. The totals of the vertical farm come from Zhuang et al. (2022); the split, the greenhouse investment and the greenhouse price are teaching assumptions. For your own project, replace them with quotations and market prices.

Labour: the Swedish question

In Japanese plant factories labour is already about 40 % of the operating costs (Zhuang et al., 2022), and van Delden et al. (2021) put it at 25–30 % of total costs. Kozai (2013) described a factory producing a million lettuce heads a year with one manager and ten part-time workers — about 1.5 minutes per head. In Sweden a grower (trädgårdsodlare) earned on average 30 200 SEK per month in 2024 (Statistics Sweden); with employer contributions of 31.42 % and other labour costs the employer pays about €26 per working hour, more than in Japan. That is why the Swedish vertical farm in this laboratory spends more on people than on electricity, and why automation — robots that transplant, space and harvest — is the most important cost lever in high-wage countries.

Common misconception

"Vertical farms are expensive because of electricity." Electricity matters — at €0.30 kWh⁻¹, as in parts of Europe in 2022, it raises the LCOF of the default farm by more than 30 % — but at normal prices capital and labour together cost more than twice as much. Investors: NPV, IRR and payback

The LCOF answers "what must a kilogram sell for?". An investor asks "given the price, does it pay?". The net present value adds all discounted cash flows; if it is positive, the project earns more than the discount rate. The internal rate of return is the discount rate at which the NPV is zero — the project's own yield. The payback time is the year in which the cumulative cash flow turns positive; the discounted payback uses discounted flows. Payback is popular because it is simple, and misleading because it ignores everything after the payback year. Try the preset Venture capital (12 %): the same farm, a different investor, a very different verdict.

Research frontier

After a wave of bankruptcies of large vertical farms in 2022–2025, techno-economic studies focus on the few levers that move the LCOF substantially: yields per photon (plant science), automation (engineering), cheaper and flexible electricity (energy systems) and higher-value products. Probabilistic cost models — like the Monte Carlo of this laboratory — are increasingly used because a single "base case" hides how likely a project is to fail.

The equations behind the model

All quantities are per m² of growing area (cultivation area of the trays for the vertical farm, floor area for the greenhouse, land for the field). Years run from \(t = 0\) (construction) to \(t = n\).

Present value and the discount factor(LC1)
\[ \text{PV} = \frac{X_t}{(1+r)^{t}} = X_t\,d_t, \qquad d_t = (1+r)^{-t} \]

an amount of money paid or received in year t is worth that amount times the discount factor today, where the discount factor is one plus the real discount rate raised to the power minus t.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(X_t\)cash flow in year \(t\) (in constant, "real" euros)€ m⁻²
\(r\)real discount rate: the return investors require above inflationyr⁻¹0.05
\(d_t\)discount factor–0.61 at \(t\) = 10
Follow the derivation
  1. €1 invested today at rate \(r\) grows to \((1+r)^t\) after \(t\) years.
  2. Conversely, the amount you must invest today to have \(X_t\) in year \(t\) is \(X_t/(1+r)^t\) — its present value.
  3. Because all prices are in constant euros, \(r\) is a real rate; mixing nominal prices with a real rate double-counts inflation.
€ × (–) = € ✓
Levelised cost of food(LC2)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{LCOF} = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{n} C_t\,(1+r)^{-t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} Q_t\,(1+r)^{-t}} \\[6pt] = \frac{I\,(\text{CRF}+m) + F}{Y}\quad\text{(single investment, identical years)} \end{gathered} \]

the levelised cost of food is the discounted sum of all costs divided by the discounted sum of all kilograms sold; for one investment at the start and identical years it reduces to the annualised investment plus maintenance plus the annual operating costs, divided by the annual yield — Eq. 15.3.4 of Lesson 15.3.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(C_t\)all costs in year \(t\): capital (LC3) and operating (LC5)€ m⁻²
\(Q_t\)kilograms sold in year \(t\) (LC4)kg m⁻²
CRFcapital recovery factor \(r(1+r)^n/[(1+r)^n-1]\)yr⁻¹
\(I, m, F, Y\)investment, maintenance share, operating costs, yield€ m⁻², yr⁻¹, € m⁻² yr⁻¹, kg m⁻² yr⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Define the LCOF as the constant price \(P\) at which the NPV is zero: \(\sum_t (P\,Q_t - C_t)(1+r)^{-t} = 0\).
  2. Solve for \(P\): numerator = PV of costs, denominator = PV of kilograms.
  3. For \(C_0 = I\), \(C_t = mI + F\) and \(Q_t = Y\) (\(t\ge1\)): divide numerator and denominator by the annuity factor \(\text{AF} = [1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r = 1/\text{CRF}\) to obtain the right-hand form.
  4. Check with Zhuang et al. (2022) as in Lesson 15.3: \(I\) = $1500, CRF(5 %, 15) = 0.0963, \(m\) = 0.015, \(F\) = $700, \(Y\) = 115 → $7.54 kg⁻¹. The preset Lesson 15.3 check gives €7.16 = $7.54 × 0.95.
Assumptions and validity

Real prices constant; no taxes, subsidies, working capital or financing structure (debt versus equity); all costs occur at the end of the year in which they are incurred. The LCOF is a cost, not a price forecast.

\(\dfrac{\text{EUR m}^{-2}\,\text{yr}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-2}\,\text{yr}^{-1}} = \text{EUR kg}^{-1}\) ✓
Capital with replacements and residual value(LC3)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{PV}_{\text{cap}} = \sum_{i} I_i\left[1 + \sum_{k\ge1,\;kL_i<n}(1+r)^{-kL_i} - \rho_i\,(1+r)^{-n}\right] \\[6pt] \rho_i = \frac{t_{\text{last},i} + L_i - n}{L_i} \end{gathered} \]

the present value of the capital is, for each item, its cost at the start plus the discounted cost of each replacement within the project, minus the discounted residual value at the end, which is the fraction of its last life that remains.

\(I_i\)cost of item \(i\): building, growing system, lighting, climate, automation€ m⁻²428, 285, 356, 214, 142

Follow the derivation
  1. An item with life \(L\) must be bought at \(t = 0, L, 2L, \dots\) as long as \(t < n\).
  2. At the end of the project the item bought last at \(t_{\text{last}}\) still has \(t_{\text{last}} + L - n\) years left; with straight-line depreciation its residual value is that fraction of its cost.
  3. Example (LED fixtures, €356, 10 yr, \(n\) = 15, 5 %): \(356 + 356\times1.05^{-10} - 0.5\times356\times1.05^{-15} = 356 + 218.6 - 85.6 = 489\) € m⁻².
  4. The equivalent annual cost multiplies the PV by CRF(r, n) — the mini-calculator.
Assumptions and validity

Replacement at the same real price (in reality LED prices fall and efficacy rises); straight-line residual values; buildings may be worth more (land) or less (specialised use) at the end.

Why ramp-up raises the LCOF so much

Most operating inputs — lamps, climate, staff — are sized for full production from day one, so in the ramp-up years costs are nearly full while revenue is not. Those early years also carry the largest discount factors. The mini-calculator shows the resulting increase in LCOF when all costs are at full capacity.

Net present value, internal rate of return and payback(LC7)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{n}\frac{P\,Q_t - C_t}{(1+r)^{t}}, \qquad \text{NPV}(\text{IRR}) = 0 \\[6pt] \sum_{t\le T_p}\left(P\,Q_t - C_t\right) = 0 \end{gathered} \]

the net present value is the discounted sum of revenue minus costs; the internal rate of return is the discount rate that makes it zero; the payback time is the moment when the cumulative, undiscounted cash flow reaches zero.

  2. The IRR is found where the NPV changes sign between −95 % and 300 %; if it never does, there is no IRR.

Decomposition, sensitivity and Monte Carlo(LC8)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{LCOF} = \sum_{k}\frac{\text{PV}_k}{\text{PV}_Q}, \qquad \Delta_j^{\pm} = \text{LCOF}(\theta_j^{\pm}) - \text{LCOF}(\theta) \\[6pt] x = \begin{cases} a + \sqrt{U(b-a)(c-a)} & U < \frac{c-a}{b-a} \\ b - \sqrt{(1-U)(b-a)(b-c)} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{gathered} \]

the LCOF is the sum of the present values of the cost components divided by the present value of the output; the tornado shows how much it changes when one parameter moves to its low or high value; the Monte Carlo draws each parameter from a triangular distribution with minimum a, mode c and maximum b, using a uniform random number U.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\text{PV}_k\)present value of cost component \(k\) (capital, labour, …)€ m⁻²
\(\theta_j^{\pm}\)parameter \(j\) at the end of its range, all others unchanged
\(a, c, b\)low value, mode (your setting), high value
Why triangular distributions?

For most techno-economic parameters we know a plausible range and a "best guess", but not a full distribution. The triangular distribution uses exactly that information. Its asymmetry matters: when the range extends further above the mode than below it — as for the capital cost of plant factories ($1159–4167 m⁻² around $1500; Zhuang et al., 2022) — the mean and the median of the LCOF lie above the base case. Parameters are drawn independently; in reality some are correlated (for example yield and labour).

Model assumptions

  • Discounted cash flows in constant euros, yearly, with the investment at \(t = 0\) and all other flows at the end of each year; real discount rate; no taxes, subsidies, inflation, financing structure or working capital. The LCOF definition is identical to Lesson 15.3 (Eq. 15.3.4).
  • Capital in five items with their own lifetimes, replaced at the same real price; straight-line residual value at the end of the project (optional). Maintenance is a fixed share of the initial investment.
  • Operations. Energy, labour, seeds, nutrients, crop protection, water and other costs are sized for full production from year 1; packaging, harvest and logistics scale with the harvest. Ramp-up affects years 1 and 2; losses are the share of the harvest that is not sold.
  • Uncertainty. One-at-a-time ranges and independent triangular distributions; correlations between parameters are ignored.
  • Currencies. US dollars converted at the ECB annual average rates: 0.95 € per $ for 2022 (Zhuang et al.) and 0.925 € per $ for 2023 (Tourte et al.). SEK at 11.0 SEK per € (editable).
