A guided tour in six steps

  1. LED spectra (one-way ANOVA). The page opens with fresh weights of lettuce under three spectra. Each dot is one independent hydroponic unit; the box shows the quartiles, the black diamond the mean with its 95 % confidence interval. Read the report sentence: the spectra differ, F(2, 21) = 14.25, p < .001. The letters above the groups summarise Tukey's HSD: groups that share a letter do not differ significantly.
  2. Switch to a t-test (Analysis → t-test, Welch) and compare Red–blue with White + far-red. The third chart shows the estimated difference (≈ 42 g) with its confidence interval — far from zero. Now compare Red–blue with White: this single t-test gives p = .027, yet Tukey's HSD after the ANOVA gave p = .089 for the same pair. Tukey accounts for the fact that three pairs were compared; picking the one pair that “worked” after looking at the data is exactly the multiple-testing trap it protects you from.
  3. Transform the response with ln(y). The test statistic hardly changes because these data are close to normal with similar spreads — transformations matter for skewed data such as the fresh weights of very uneven crops.
  4. Regression: choose the EC regression example. The line looks convincing (R² = .87), but the residuals form an arch — positive in the middle, negative at both ends — and the pure-error lack-of-fit test flags it (p = .039): growth levels off at high EC. A straight line is a description over this range only, not a law of nature.
  5. Blocks help: load the Tasting panel. Each panellist rated all three samples, so panellists are blocks. The checklist shows that a one-way ANOVA ignoring blocks gives F = 8.35, but the block design gives F = 46.0 — blocking made the experiment about five times as efficient.
  6. Plan your own experiment: choose Plan: n for Δ = 20 g. With a between-plant SD of 22 g, you need 21 plants per treatment for 80 % power. With 6 per treatment — a typical student project — power is only about 30 %.
Key idea

A statistical result has three parts that belong together: an estimate (how big is the difference?), its uncertainty (the confidence interval) and a test (how surprising would this be if there were no effect?). Report all three — and the assumption checks that justify them.

Choosing the right analysis

The analysis follows from two questions: what kind of variables do you have (a number such as fresh weight, or a category such as “tipburn yes/no”), and how was the experiment designed (independent groups, the same units measured twice, or blocks)?

What a p-value is — and is not

A p-value is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as yours if the null hypothesis were true (for example, if all three spectra gave the same mean fresh weight). A small p means your data would be surprising under the null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and it says nothing about how large or important the effect is (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). That is why the workbench always reports an effect size and a confidence interval with every test. Watch p-values “dance” from one replication to the next in the Sampling distributions laboratory.

Common misconception

“p = .20, so the treatment has no effect.” A non-significant result only means the data are compatible with no effect — and usually also with a sizeable effect. Look at the confidence interval: with six plants per treatment it is often ±30 g wide. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The experimental unit decides the n

The single most common error in student growth experiments is counting plants as replicates when the treatment was applied to a whole tray, reservoir or light compartment. The plants in one tray share the same solution, the same position and the same accidents — they are subsamples, not replicates (Hurlbert, 1984). Average them first, then analyse one value per experimental unit. The same applies to repeated measurements over time in your grow log: the plant, not the measurement day, is the unit. The Experiment designer shows what goes wrong otherwise.

Formal tests of assumptions have little power in small samples and too much power in large ones. Use them together with the plots, and remember that t-tests and ANOVA are fairly robust to mild non-normality when groups have similar sizes (Quinn & Keough, 2002).

Effect sizes

Cohen's d expresses a difference in units of the standard deviation (0.2 is conventionally “small”, 0.5 “medium”, 0.8 “large”; Cohen, 1988). η² is the share of the total variation explained by the treatments; ω² is its less biased cousin. For block designs the workbench reports partial η², which leaves the block variation out of the denominator. Effect sizes allow comparison between studies and are needed for power analysis and meta-analysis (Lakens, 2013).

Writing it up

The workbench writes sentences following APA 7 conventions: italic test symbols, degrees of freedom in brackets, two decimals for test statistics, exact p-values to three decimals (p < .001 below that) without a leading zero, and an effect size with its confidence interval. Treat the sentence as a draft: add the biological meaning (“far-red supplementation increased fresh weight by about a quarter”) and cite the figure. See the lesson Writing the laboratory report.

The equations behind the workbench

Notation: \(y_{ij}\) is observation \(j\) in group \(i\); \(\bar y_i\), \(s_i\) and \(n_i\) are the group mean, standard deviation and size; \(k\) groups and \(N\) observations in total.

Standard error and confidence interval of a mean(W1)
\[ \mathrm{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt n}, \qquad \bar y \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n} \]

the standard error of a mean is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size; the confidence interval extends a t-quantile number of standard errors on either side of the mean.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(s\)sample standard deviation, \(\sqrt{\sum(y-\bar y)^2/(n-1)}\)as y (e.g. g)
\(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\)quantile of Student's t with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom (2.57 for n = 6, 1.96 as n → ∞)–
Follow the derivation
  1. For independent observations with variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar y) = \operatorname{Var}(\tfrac1n\sum y_j) = \tfrac{1}{n^2}\,n\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n\).
  2. If the \(y\) are normal, \((\bar y-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n)\) follows Student's t with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom (Student, 1908) — s is estimated, which makes the tails heavier than the normal.
  3. So \(P(-t_c \le (\bar y-\mu)/(s/\sqrt n) \le t_c) = 1-\alpha\); rearranging for \(\mu\) gives the interval.
Assumptions and validity

Independent observations; normality of \(\bar y\) (exact for normal data, approximately true for n ≳ 15–30 by the central limit theorem unless the data are very skewed). With n = 3 the multiplier is 4.30 — small samples give wide intervals.

\(\mathrm{SE}\) has the unit of y: \(\text{g}/\sqrt{1} = \text{g}\). ✓
Welch's t-test and the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom(W2)
\[ t = \frac{\bar y_2 - \bar y_1}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}, \qquad \nu = \frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\dfrac{(s_1^2/n_1)^2}{n_1-1}+\dfrac{(s_2^2/n_2)^2}{n_2-1}} \]

the difference between two means divided by its standard error, where each group contributes its own variance; the degrees of freedom are reduced when the variances or sample sizes differ.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(s_1^2, s_2^2\)sample variances of the two groupsg²
\(\nu\)approximate degrees of freedom (between \(\min(n_i)-1\) and \(n_1+n_2-2\))–
Follow the derivation
  1. \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar y_2-\bar y_1) = \sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2\) for independent groups — no need to assume \(\sigma_1=\sigma_2\).
  2. The estimated variance \(V = s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2\) is a weighted sum of scaled χ² variables. Satterthwaite matched its mean and variance to a single scaled χ² with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom: \(\nu = 2E(V)^2/\operatorname{Var}(V)\), which gives the formula (Welch, 1947).
  3. \(t\) is then compared with Student's t with \(\nu\) (usually non-integer) degrees of freedom.
Assumptions and validity

Independent units, approximately normal groups. Welch's test keeps its false-positive rate close to α when variances differ, and loses almost nothing when they are equal — which is why it is the default in R and in this workbench.

\(t = \text{g}/\sqrt{\text{g}^2} \) is dimensionless. ✓
Pooled-variance and paired t-tests(W3)
\[ t = \frac{\bar y_2-\bar y_1}{s_p\sqrt{\tfrac1{n_1}+\tfrac1{n_2}}},\;\; s_p^2=\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}; \qquad t_{\text{paired}} = \frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt n} \]

Student's two-sample test uses one pooled variance with \(n_1+n_2-2\) degrees of freedom; the paired test is a one-sample test on the differences \(d = y_{\text{after}} - y_{\text{before}}\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(s_p\)pooled standard deviationas y
\(\bar d, s_d\)mean and SD of the within-unit differencesas y
Why pairing helps
  1. \(\operatorname{Var}(d) = \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B\).
  2. When the two measurements on the same plant are positively correlated (\(\rho>0\)) — big plants stay big — the variance of the difference is much smaller than the variance of either measurement.
  3. In the nitrate example ρ ≈ 0.9: the paired t is 13.4, the (wrong) independent-groups Welch t on the same numbers only 4.7.
Assumptions and validity

Pooled test: equal variances in both groups (check the variance ratio). Paired test: differences approximately normal; pairs independent of each other.

Both statistics are ratios of g to g: dimensionless. ✓
Standardised effect size (Cohen's d, Hedges' g)(W4)
\[ d = \frac{\bar y_2-\bar y_1}{s_p}, \qquad g = d\left(1-\frac{3}{4(n_1+n_2-2)-1}\right), \qquad \mathrm{SE}(d)\approx\sqrt{\frac{n_1+n_2}{n_1n_2}+\frac{d^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}} \]

the difference in means measured in pooled standard deviations; Hedges' correction removes the small-sample upward bias; the standard error gives an approximate confidence interval.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(d, g\)standardised mean difference–
Assumptions and validity

The CI uses a large-sample normal approximation (Hedges & Olkin, 1985); it is approximate for n below about 10 per group. For paired data the workbench reports \(d_z = \bar d/s_d\).

g divided by g — dimensionless, which is what makes d comparable across studies and variables. ✓
One-way ANOVA: partitioning the variation(W5)
\[ \underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij}-\bar y)^2}_{SS_T} = \underbrace{\sum_i n_i(\bar y_i-\bar y)^2}_{SS_B} + \underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij}-\bar y_i)^2}_{SS_W}, \qquad F = \frac{SS_B/(k-1)}{SS_W/(N-k)}, \quad \eta^2 = \frac{SS_B}{SS_T} \]

the total variation splits exactly into variation between group means and variation within groups; F compares the between-group mean square with the within-group (error) mean square.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(SS_B, SS_W\)between- and within-group sums of squaresg²
\(MS_W = SS_W/(N-k)\)error mean square = pooled varianceg²
\(F\)compared with the F distribution with \(k-1\) and \(N-k\) df–
Follow the derivation
  1. Write \(y_{ij}-\bar y = (\bar y_i-\bar y) + (y_{ij}-\bar y_i)\) and square. The cross-term \(2\sum_i(\bar y_i-\bar y)\sum_j(y_{ij}-\bar y_i)\) vanishes because deviations from a group mean sum to zero.
  2. Under H₀ (equal means) both \(MS_B\) and \(MS_W\) estimate σ², so F ≈ 1. Real differences inflate \(MS_B\): \(E(MS_B) = \sigma^2 + \sum n_i\tau_i^2/(k-1)\).
  3. With normal errors, \(SS_W/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{N-k}\) and, under H₀, \(SS_B/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{k-1}\), independent — their scaled ratio is F-distributed (Fisher's analysis of variance).
Assumptions and validity

Independent units, normal residuals, equal variances. ω² = (SS_B − (k−1)MS_W)/(SS_T + MS_W) corrects η² for its upward bias in small samples.

\(F = \text{g}^2/\text{g}^2\): dimensionless. ✓
Tukey's honestly significant difference(W6)
\[ q = \frac{|\bar y_i-\bar y_j|}{\sqrt{\dfrac{MS_W}{2}\left(\dfrac1{n_i}+\dfrac1{n_j}\right)}}, \qquad (\bar y_j-\bar y_i) \pm q_{1-\alpha;\,k,\,N-k}\sqrt{\frac{MS_W}{2}\left(\frac1{n_i}+\frac1{n_j}\right)} \]

each pairwise difference is divided by its standard error and compared with the studentised range distribution, which accounts for the fact that the largest of many differences is being tested.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(q_{1-\alpha;k,\nu}\)quantile of the studentised range for k means and ν error df (3.56 for k = 3, ν = 21)–
Why not several t-tests?
  1. With k = 4 groups there are 6 pairs. If each t-test has α = 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive can reach 1 − 0.95⁶ ≈ 0.26.
  2. The studentised range \(Q = (\max\bar y - \min\bar y)/\sqrt{MS_W/n}\) has a known distribution under H₀; using its quantile keeps the family-wise error rate at α for all pairs together (Tukey, 1949; Kramer's extension for unequal n).
  3. The workbench evaluates the studentised range distribution by numerical integration (stats.js) and checks it against published tables.
Assumptions and validity

Same as ANOVA. With unequal n the Tukey–Kramer intervals are slightly conservative.

q is a ratio of g to g — dimensionless; the interval has the unit of y. ✓
Brown–Forsythe test of equal variances(W7)
\[ z_{ij} = \left|y_{ij} - \tilde y_i\right|, \qquad F_{BF} = \text{one-way ANOVA } F \text{ computed on the } z_{ij} \]

replace every observation by its absolute distance from its group median, then test whether these distances have the same mean in all groups.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\tilde y_i\)median of group ias y
Assumptions and validity

Using the median (instead of the mean in Levene's original test) makes the test robust to skewed data (Brown & Forsythe, 1974). If it rejects, use Welch's ANOVA: \(F^* = \frac{\sum w_i(\bar y_i-\hat\mu)^2/(k-1)}{1+\frac{2(k-2)}{k^2-1}\sum\frac{(1-w_i/\sum w)^2}{n_i-1}}\) with \(w_i=n_i/s_i^2\) (Welch, 1951).

F on distances measured in g: dimensionless. ✓
Randomised complete block design(W8)
\[ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad SS_T = SS_{\text{trt}} + SS_{\text{block}} + SS_E, \qquad F = \frac{SS_{\text{trt}}/(t-1)}{SS_E/\big((t-1)(b-1)\big)} \]

each observation is the overall mean plus a treatment effect plus a block effect plus error; the block sum of squares is removed from the error before testing treatments.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\tau_i\)effect of treatment i (t treatments)as y
\(\beta_j\)effect of block j (b blocks: shelves, panellists, days)as y
\(SS_{\text{block}} = t\sum_j(\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y)^2\)variation between blocksg²
Follow the derivation
  1. Least squares gives \(\hat\tau_i = \bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y\), \(\hat\beta_j = \bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y\), residual \(e_{ij} = y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot j}+\bar y\).
  2. Orthogonality of the complete, balanced layout makes the three sums of squares add up exactly.
  3. Relative efficiency versus a completely randomised design: \(RE = \frac{(b-1)MS_{\text{block}} + b(t-1)MS_E}{(bt-1)MS_E}\). RE = 5 means blocking saved the equivalent of four out of five replicates.
Assumptions and validity

Additivity: the treatment effect is the same in every block (checked with Tukey's one-degree-of-freedom test for non-additivity). Complete blocks: every treatment once per block — incomplete blocks are dropped. Randomisation within blocks.

All sums of squares in g², F dimensionless. ✓
Least-squares line, confidence and prediction bands(W9)
\[ b=\frac{\sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)}{\sum(x-\bar x)^2},\;\; a=\bar y-b\bar x,\;\; \hat y_0 \pm t_{n-2}\,s\sqrt{\underbrace{1}_{\text{PI only}}+\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\bar x)^2}{S_{xx}}} \]

the slope is the covariance of x and y divided by the variance of x; the confidence band for the mean line widens away from the mean of x, and the prediction band for a single new plant adds the scatter of individual plants.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(b\)slopeunit y per unit x (g per mS cm⁻¹)
\(s = \sqrt{SSE/(n-2)}\)residual standard erroras y
\(S_{xx}=\sum(x-\bar x)^2\)spread of the predictorunit x²
Follow the derivation
  1. Minimise \(SSE=\sum(y-a-bx)^2\): setting \(\partial SSE/\partial a = 0\) and \(\partial SSE/\partial b=0\) gives the two normal equations, solved by the formulas above.
  2. \(\operatorname{Var}(b)=\sigma^2/S_{xx}\): spreading the x values (a wide EC range) makes the slope more precise.
  3. \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat y_0)=\sigma^2(1/n + (x_0-\bar x)^2/S_{xx})\); a new observation adds its own σ², hence the “1+”.
  4. Lack of fit (with replicated x): split SSE into pure error \(\sum\sum(y-\bar y_x)^2\) and lack of fit; \(F=\frac{SS_{LOF}/(m-2)}{SS_{PE}/(n-m)}\) for m distinct x values (Kutner et al., 2005).
Assumptions and validity

Linear mean, independent residuals, constant variance (Koenker–Breusch–Pagan test), normal residuals for exact CIs. Cook's distance \(D_i = \frac{e_i^2}{2s^2}\frac{h_i}{(1-h_i)^2}\) flags influential points (Cook, 1977).

\(b\): g ÷ (mS cm⁻¹); the bands have the unit of y. ✓
Correlation and its confidence interval(W10)
\[ r = \frac{\sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum(x-\bar x)^2\sum(y-\bar y)^2}},\quad t=r\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r^2}},\quad \tanh\!\left(\operatorname{artanh} r \pm \frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n-3}}\right) \]

Pearson's r is the standardised covariance; it is tested with a t statistic on n − 2 df, and its confidence interval is built on Fisher's z scale, where the sampling distribution is nearly normal.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(r\)correlation (−1 to 1); Spearman's ρ is r of the ranks–
Assumptions and validity

Pearson: linear relationship, roughly bivariate normal, no dominant outlier. Spearman: any monotonic relationship. r² equals the R² of the simple regression.

Units of x and y cancel: r is dimensionless. ✓
χ² test of independence and Cramér's V(W11)
\[ E_{ij}=\frac{R_iC_j}{N},\qquad \chi^2=\sum_{i,j}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}},\;\; df=(r-1)(c-1),\qquad V=\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N(\min(r,c)-1)}} \]

expected counts follow from the row and column totals if the variables are independent; χ² adds up the squared, scaled deviations of observed from expected counts; Cramér's V rescales χ² to a 0–1 effect size.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(O_{ij}, E_{ij}\)observed and expected countscounts
\(R_i, C_j, N\)row totals, column totals, grand totalcounts
Assumptions and validity

Each individual counted once; expected counts ≥ 5 in at least 80 % of cells and none below 1 (Cochran's rule). The adjusted residual \((O-E)/\sqrt{E(1-R_i/N)(1-C_j/N)}\) is approximately standard normal and shows where the association lies.

counts²/counts summed → dimensionless χ². ✓
Power of the two-sample t-test(W12)
\[ 1-\beta = P\big(|T'| > t_{1-\alpha/2,\,2n-2}\big),\quad T'\sim t'_{2n-2}(\delta),\;\; \delta = \frac{\Delta}{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac n2}; \qquad n \approx 2\left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}}{\Delta/\sigma}\right)^2 \]

power is the probability that the test statistic, which follows a noncentral t distribution when the true difference is Δ, falls beyond the critical value; the right-hand formula is the normal approximation for the n per group.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\Delta\)smallest difference worth detectingas y
\(\delta\)noncentrality parameter–
\(1-\beta\)power; β = probability of missing a real effect (type II error)–
Follow the derivation
  1. If the true difference is Δ, then \(T = (\bar y_2-\bar y_1)/(s_p\sqrt{2/n})\) has a noncentral t distribution with noncentrality \(\delta=\Delta/(\sigma\sqrt{2/n})\).
  2. The workbench integrates this distribution exactly (Lenth, 1989). The normal approximation replaces t′ by N(δ, 1) and solves \(\delta = z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\) for n.
  3. Example: Δ = 20 g, σ = 22 g, α = .05, power .80: the approximation gives n ≈ 19.0; the exact answer is 21 per group — small samples need more than the normal formula suggests.
Assumptions and validity

σ must be the SD between experimental units, from a pilot study or earlier data. For k groups the workbench uses Cohen's f = (Δ/σ)√(1/(2k)) (two extreme means Δ apart, others in the middle) with the noncentral F distribution.

Δ/σ is dimensionless; n is a count. ✓

    Paste your own project data (or your grow log). Run the analysis that matches your design, go through every item in the checklist, and write a results paragraph of 3–5 sentences that includes an estimate, a confidence interval, a test and an effect size.

How the workbench computes

  • Distributions. t, F and χ² probabilities come from the regularised incomplete beta and gamma functions (continued fractions); the studentised range (Tukey) from numerical integration; all in the course library stats.js.
  • Normality. Shapiro–Wilk W with Royston's (1995) approximation of the coefficients and p-value — the algorithm used by R's shapiro.test, valid for 3 ≤ n ≤ 5000.
  • Rank tests follow R's conventions: exact distributions for Mann–Whitney and Wilcoxon signed-rank when n < 50 and there are no ties; otherwise a normal approximation with tie and continuity corrections. Kruskal–Wallis and Friedman use tie-corrected χ² approximations.
  • Power uses the exact noncentral t (Lenth, 1989) and noncentral F (Poisson mixture) distributions. The shared library's powerTwoSample uses a shifted central-t approximation that underestimates power by up to 8 percentage points at n ≤ 5, so it is not used here.
  • Parsing. Tab, semicolon, comma or space separated; decimal commas converted when the separator is not a comma; quoted fields; NA, blank, “-” and “.” as missing values. Check the preview table: a column read as text instead of numbers usually contains a typing error.
  • Limitations. Only one-way and randomised-block designs; no factorial ANOVA, repeated-measures models or mixed models (use R's lme4 for tray-within-treatment designs). Tests are two-sided. The report sentence is a draft — you are responsible for its content.
