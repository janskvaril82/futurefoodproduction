Cultivated meat techno-economics

What would a kilogram of cultivated meat cost to make — and what would have to be true for it to compete with chicken or beef? This laboratory is a transparent techno-economic model built on the structure of Humbird's (2021) analysis: cells that double every day or two, reactors that can hold only so many of them, a culture medium whose growth factors cost millions of dollars per gram, a sterile plant that must be paid for, staffed and powered. Watch the plant run, then take it apart with a cost waterfall, a tornado chart, a Monte Carlo simulation and a price-parity map.

What to look at first

  1. Watch one batch. The default is Humbird's fed-batch plant: 24 reactors of 20 m³ (16 m³ working volume) making 6.8 kt of wet cells a year. Follow the timeline under the reactors: each batch grows through about two doublings (the medium turns from straw to salmon as the cells multiply and the O₂ bubbles thicken), is harvested, cleaned in place and refilled — a cycle of about two days. The reactors are staggered, so harvests flow continuously to the centrifuge.
  2. Read the waterfall. At Humbird's assumptions the medium (amino acids and glucose, plus a little for growth factors) is the largest single item, about $22 of $37 kg⁻¹; the capital charge and the capital-linked maintenance and insurance together add another $11. Labour, electricity and consumables are small.
  3. Switch to the early-commercial preset. Bought at list prices (the lowest found in the last systematic public survey, December 2020; CE Delft, 2021), recombinant proteins and growth factors make the medium cost $16.6 per litre and the plant $150 kg⁻¹ — almost 90 % of it growth factors and recombinant proteins. Now open the tornado: the price of those proteins, the medium price and the cell density (which sets the litres needed per kilogram) dwarf every other assumption.
  4. Try the CE Delft optimistic preset. Growth factors are 1,000× cheaper, recombinant proteins 500× cheaper in use, the plant is food-grade and financed over 30 years: $6.5 kg⁻¹. Look at the waterfall again — no single item dominates any more, and electricity has become one of the larger bars.
  5. Switch fed-batch to perfusion (Humbird perfusion preset). The reactors stay full and cells are bled off continuously at the growth rate, reaching 195 g L⁻¹ — yet the cost rises to about $51 kg⁻¹, because small perfusion reactors, cell-retention filters and their membranes are expensive.
  6. Move the parity target. Choose ground beef, then whole chicken. With a mark-up of 2 (processing, distribution, retail), wet cells must cost about $7.6 kg⁻¹ to match ground beef and $2.2 kg⁻¹ to match whole chicken at US retail prices of August 2026. The parity panel and the map show which combinations of medium price and cell density could get there — and which cannot.
Key idea

Cultivated meat is a bioprocess in which slow-growing, fragile animal cells are grown in expensive, sterile equipment. Its cost is set by four levers: the price of the medium (especially growth factors and recombinant proteins), the mass of cells a litre can hold, the capital cost of sterile capacity and the cost of money. Every published analysis that reaches meat-like prices assumes large improvements in all four at once.

From a vial of cells to a kilogram of meat

In the bulk-production concept analysed by Humbird (2021), cells from a cell bank are expanded through a seed train of ever larger bioreactors and finally inoculate a production bioreactor. There they grow in suspension until a density limit is reached; the cell slurry is then harvested and dewatered by centrifugation into a wet cell paste (about 70 % water) that can be formulated into minced or nugget-type products, alone or blended with plant proteins. Structured cuts need scaffolds and further differentiation, which this model represents only as an optional maturation hold.

Why cells are expensive to grow

They are slow. Animal cells double roughly once a day (Humbird assumed 24 h, CE Delft 30 h), bacteria every half hour. A production reactor therefore spends days, not hours, on each batch, and needs sterile protection throughout: a contaminant that doubles every 30 minutes wins any race.

They cannot be packed densely. A reactor becomes too viscous to mix once cells occupy about a quarter of its volume, about 86 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹ or 258 g L⁻¹ for 3,000 pg cells; in fed-batch culture, ammonia and CO₂ accumulation stop growth much earlier — at about 110 g L⁻¹ for Humbird's metabolically enhanced cells in a 20 m³ reactor, and only 7 g L⁻¹ for an unoptimised "wild-type" metabolism. Perfusion removes inhibitors continuously and reaches 195 g L⁻¹, but needs cell-retention filters that limit reactor size to about 2 m³.

Their medium is a pharmaceutical. Besides glucose, amino acids, salts and vitamins, serum-free media contain proteins that tell cells to survive and divide: insulin, transferrin, albumin and growth factors such as FGF2 and TGF-β. In December 2020, CE Delft found list prices of $1.3–2.3 million per gram for FGF2 and $3.7–5.0 million per gram for TGF-β; recombinant proteins and growth factors made up more than 99 % of the medium cost. Humbird instead assumed that these proteins would be produced at food-industry scale and would add only $3 kg⁻¹, and that amino acids from a plant-protein hydrolysate could cost $2 kg⁻¹.

Where the money goes

A techno-economic analysis (TEA) adds up the cost of goods: variable costs that scale with every kilogram (medium, electricity, consumables), fixed operating costs (staff, maintenance, insurance) and the capital charge, the yearly payment that repays the investment with interest. Capital is converted to a yearly payment with the capital recovery factor (Eq. T7); Humbird used 7.5 % over 10 years (15 % of the investment each year), CE Delft simple payback times of 4 years ("commercial") or 30 years ("social").

Economies of scale enter twice. Larger plants spread fixed staff and shared infrastructure (media preparation, O₂ supply, CIP, buildings) over more kilograms; that part of the capital follows the classic six-tenths rule, cost ∝ capacity0.6. Larger reactors are cheaper per cubic metre — Humbird's installed cost of a sterile reactor system is $0.80 million plus $30.7 k per m³, because piping, instrumentation and sterile fittings cost almost the same for a small and a large vessel. But reactor size is capped: above about 20–50 m³, CO₂ removal limits the cell density in fed-batch culture (Humbird, 2021).

Humbird versus CE Delft

The two most cited analyses differ less in their arithmetic than in their assumptions. Humbird (2021) designed a plant like a biopharmaceutical factory — clean rooms, a 1.84× factor for engineering and contingency, 7.5 % interest — and concluded that metabolic efficiency and cheap hydrolysate media are "necessary but insufficient" for affordability. CE Delft (Vergeer et al., 2021), with data from more than 16 companies, assumed a food-grade plant without clean rooms, 1,000-fold cheaper growth factors, far less albumin and 30-year financing, and reached about $6 kg⁻¹. A scoping review of published TEAs concluded that under the current technological paradigm cultivated meat is unlikely to be competitive with conventional meat (Goodwin et al., 2024).

Energy and climate

Sinke et al. (2023) estimated about 22.3 kWh of energy per kg of cells for a 2030 commercial plant, three quarters of it for cooling the reactors. The carbon footprint then depends mostly on the electricity: over 14 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ with global-average energy, about 4 with renewable energy at the plant and 2.8 when the medium suppliers also use renewable energy — below beef and pork, comparable to an efficient 2030 chicken (2.7). The model reproduces these numbers with a two-term footprint (Eq. T9).

An honest look at price parity

Retail and production costs must not be confused. A wet cell paste still has to be formulated, packaged, distributed and sold; Humbird estimated that $25 kg⁻¹ of wet cells becomes at least $50 kg⁻¹ at the supermarket. In August 2026 whole chickens cost $2.01 per pound ($4.44 kg⁻¹) and ground beef $6.92 per pound ($15.26 kg⁻¹) in US cities (BLS). With a mark-up of 2, parity with ground beef requires wet cells at about $7.6 kg⁻¹, and with whole chicken at about $2.2 kg⁻¹. In this model that means, simultaneously:

  • growth factors and recombinant proteins at industrial-enzyme prices — at least a thousand-fold below 2020 list prices — and a medium below roughly $0.1–0.3 per litre;
  • harvest densities of 50–100 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹ or more (100–300 g L⁻¹), close to the viscosity limit, with metabolically efficient cell lines that neither poison themselves with ammonia and lactate nor lose their ability to differentiate;
  • food-grade rather than pharmaceutical plants, very large scale (10 kt a⁻¹ and more per plant, as in CE Delft's scenarios) and patient, low-cost capital;
  • contamination-free operation, cheap low-carbon electricity and efficient cooling.

None of these is forbidden by physics, but no plant has demonstrated them together. Even the CE Delft optimistic scenario ($6.4 kg⁻¹) sits above the ≈ $2 kg⁻¹ production cost of conventional meat, and the same scenario with commercial 4-year payback costs about $13.5 kg⁻¹ in this model. Company-reported projections are lower — Pasitka et al. (2024) reported continuous perfusion at up to 130 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹ in a $0.63 L⁻¹ animal-free medium and projected $6.2 per pound ($13.7 kg⁻¹) for a hypothetical 50,000 L facility — but such projections have not yet been confirmed at commercial scale. See Lesson 12.4 Cultivated meat and cellular agriculture for the biology, bioreactor physics, regulation and ethics.

Assumptions and validity

Balanced, uninhibited exponential growth until the harvest density; no lag phase or cell death. Humbird argued that any inhibition is economically unacceptable at such slow growth: it is cheaper to stop the batch and start a new one.

Perfusion: harvest by bleeding at the growth rate(T4)
\[ Q_R = \mu\,X\,V_w\cdot 8760\,a\,(1-f_{\text{fail}})\,y, \qquad v_m = \frac{1000\,D}{24\,\mu\,X}\,(1 + f_x) \]

at steady state the cells must be removed as fast as they grow, so a perfused reactor delivers the growth rate times the cell concentration times its volume; the medium used per kilogram is the perfusion rate divided by the daily harvest per unit volume.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical
\(D\)perfusion rate (reactor volumes per day)d⁻¹1.0 (dual ATF, 2 m³)
\(v_m\)medium used per kg of cellsL kg⁻¹7–11
\(f_x\)extra medium: seed train, initial fill, losses–0.2–0.6
Follow the derivation
  1. Cell balance of a well-mixed perfused reactor with total cell retention and a bleed stream \(F_b\): \(V\,\mathrm dX/\mathrm dt = \mu X V - F_b X\).
  2. Steady state (\(\mathrm dX/\mathrm dt = 0\)) requires \(F_b = \mu V\): the bleed dilution rate equals the growth rate.
  3. The mass harvested per hour is \(F_b X = \mu X V\). For Humbird's case, \(0.0289\ \text{h}^{-1}\times195\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\times1.6\ \text{m}^3 = 9.0\) kg h⁻¹ per reactor — his reported value.
  4. Medium flows at \(D V\) per day while \(24\mu X V\) kg of cells leave, so \(v_m = D/(24\mu X)\) m³ kg⁻¹ = \(1000D/(24\mu X)\) L kg⁻¹ (7.4 L kg⁻¹ for Humbird's case, before extras).

Capital recovery factor(T7)
\[ \text{CRF} = \frac{i\,(1+i)^{n}}{(1+i)^{n} - 1} \quad\xrightarrow{\;i\,\to\,0\;}\quad \frac{1}{n} \]

the share of the investment that must be earned each year to repay it with interest \(i\) over \(n\) years; without interest it is simply one over the payback time.

SymbolMeaningUnitExamples
\(i\)interest or discount ratea⁻¹0.075 (Humbird)
\(n\)plant life or payback timea10 (Humbird); 4 or 30 (CE Delft)

Follow the derivation
  1. A loan \(I\) is repaid by \(n\) equal annual payments \(A\). Their present value must equal the loan: \(I = \sum_{k=1}^{n} A(1+i)^{-k}\).
  2. Geometric series: \(\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1+i)^{-k} = [1-(1+i)^{-n}]/i\).
  3. So \(A = I\,i/[1-(1+i)^{-n}] = I\,i(1+i)^n/[(1+i)^n-1] = \text{CRF}\cdot I\). Humbird wrote the same factor as \(i/(1-(1+i)^{-n})\) = 14.6 % for 7.5 % over 10 years.
  4. As \(i\to0\), \((1+i)^n \approx 1 + ni\) and CRF → \(1/n\): CE Delft's payback criterion is the zero-interest case.

Sensitivity and uncertainty(T10)
\[ \Delta_j = \left|\,c(\theta_j^{\text{high}}) - c(\theta_j^{\text{low}})\,\right| \] \[ \hat F(c) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf 1\!\left[c^{(k)} \le c\right], \qquad P_q = \inf\{\,c : \hat F(c) \ge q\,\} \]

the swing of input \(j\) is the difference in cost between its high and low value with all other inputs fixed (the tornado ranks inputs by swing); the Monte Carlo draws \(N\) plants with all uncertain inputs sampled together, and the percentile \(P_q\) is the cost below which a fraction \(q\) of the draws fall.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\theta_j\)input \(j\) (e.g., medium price, density, capital)various

Why the two methods can disagree

A tornado varies one input at a time and so ignores combinations: two moderately unfavourable assumptions can together cost more than the sum of their swings when effects multiply (density and medium price do: cost ∝ price ÷ density). The Monte Carlo varies everything together but depends entirely on the assumed distributions, which in early-stage TEA are expert judgement rather than data. Neither replaces a pilot plant.

Swing and percentiles carry the unit of the cost, $ kg⁻¹ ✓

Now read the cell-volume-fraction readout and the map: which physical limit discussed by Humbird does this operating point challenge, and what would you ask the authors?

  5. The price of money

    Load CE Delft optimistic and set the payback time to 4 years, then to 7.5 % interest over 10 years. How much of the gap between $6.4 kg⁻¹ and these results is due to finance alone? Who could provide cheap, patient capital, and would that be a good use of public money?

  6. Climate check on your grid

    For your preferred scenario, compare the carbon footprint on a world-average grid (0.46) and on a Swedish-like grid (0.04 kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹). Which conventional meats does it beat in each case (Sinke et al., 2023 benchmarks)? What would you need to change in the medium supply chain to reach the chicken benchmark?

Model assumptions

  • The product is bulk wet cell mass (≈ 70 % water) leaving the dewatering centrifuge, as in Humbird (2021) and CE Delft (2021); formulation, scaffolds for structured products, packaging, distribution and profit are outside the model.
  • Growth is exponential and uninhibited up to the harvest density, which the user sets; the physical limits Humbird computed (NH₃ and CO₂ inhibition, oxygen transfer, viscosity at a cell volume fraction of ≈ 0.25) are shown as warnings, not enforced.
  • All production reactors are identical, fully utilised and staggered; the number of reactors is treated as a continuous number for costing (the stage shows the rounded count). Seed-train and differentiation reactors are represented by a fraction of the production-reactor cost.
  • Medium is priced per litre with a fixed composition. Humbird priced nutrients by consumption (stoichiometry), not by volume; the per-litre price in his presets is therefore a model-equivalent value chosen to reproduce his cost per kilogram.
  • Capital: Humbird's installed-reactor correlation or a power law, the rest of the plant as a six-tenths-rule term plus a per-reactor term fitted in this laboratory to Humbird's two plants, and a single indirect-cost factor. Estimates at this stage have an accuracy of roughly −20 % / +40 % (CE Delft, 2021).
  • Finance: a constant annual capital charge (Eq. T7); no taxes, inflation, working capital, start-up costs, learning curves or plant ramp-up. Money values are in the price years of the sources (2018–2020 US dollars) and have not been inflation-adjusted.
  • Staff is an input (it does not scale automatically with the number of reactors). Electricity use per kilogram is an input taken from the literature rather than computed from heat and mass balances.
  • Failed (contaminated) batches and harvest losses consume medium and energy without producing product and increase the number of reactors needed.
  • The carbon footprint is a two-term calibration to the scenarios of Sinke et al. (2023) (Eq. T9); it ignores land use, water and other impact categories and the difference between CO₂ and methane warming dynamics.
  • Retail benchmarks are US city-average prices (BLS, August 2026); the mark-up from wet-cell cost to retail is an assumption (Humbird suggested at least 2×).
  • The "early commercial (2024–2026)" preset uses the December 2020 list prices of CE Delft (2021), the most recent systematic public survey of medium prices. Companies report much cheaper animal-free media (e.g., $0.63 L⁻¹, Pasitka et al., 2024), but independent, commercial-scale data for 2024–2026 are not public; treat the preset as an upper bound for plants that buy their proteins on the market.
