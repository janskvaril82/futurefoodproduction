Agrivoltaic field designer

Can one hectare give both food and electricity? Build an agrivoltaic field in 3D — tall tilted panel rows that tractors drive under, or vertical bifacial "solar fences" like Sweden's first agrivoltaic research site at Kärrbo prästgård near Västerås. Let the sun run through the day, watch the real shadows sweep over the crop, and see what the model calculates: the light map on the crop, the crop yield, the electricity and the land equivalent ratio.

What to look at first

  1. Run a summer day at Kärrbo. The laboratory opens with vertical bifacial modules in north–south rows 10 m apart over ley grass, the design principle of the research site outside Västerås. Press Play the day. In the morning the east faces are lit, in the afternoon the west faces; around noon the fences stand edge-on to the sun and cast almost no shadow. The PV output now readout rises twice: a "camel-back" power curve with peaks in the morning and afternoon, when electricity is often worth more than at midday.
  2. Follow the shadows. Set Map shows to PPFD now and drag the solar time. Shadow bands sweep across the whole row gap during the day, so no spot is shaded for long; then switch back to Day %: the darkest strips lie right beside the fences, where both the morning and the evening shadow fall and the sky is partly hidden.
  3. Lift the panels. Apply the preset Heggelbach-like overhead: modules on a 5 m high table facing south-west, like those of the German research farm near Lake Constance (the 20° tilt is our assumption). The shadows are now broad rectangles that travel across the crop. Lower the clearance to 3 m and then raise it to 7 m, keeping everything else fixed: the mean light hardly changes, but the stripes in the Day % map become sharper or smoother. Height spreads shade; it does not remove it.
  4. Change the crop, not the array. With the same design, switch between ley grass, lettuce, potato, cereal and maize. The light reduction is identical — the yield is not. Shade-tolerant crops lose much less than the light; maize loses more than the light.
  5. Find the best compromise. The last chart varies only the row spacing. The land equivalent ratio (LER) rises as rows get denser, because electricity gains more than the crop loses — until the crop falls below what a farmer (and the German standard DIN SPEC 91434, which requires at least 66 % of the reference yield) would accept. Watch the Crop yield readout turn red.
  6. Place a sensor. Click on the field (or on the map chart) to plant a virtual quantum sensor. Its PPFD, its daily light and its season yield appear in the scene and as a dashed curve in the day chart — this is how agrivoltaic research sites such as Kärrbo map the light under their modules.
Key idea

Panels do not destroy sunlight; they redistribute it between modules and crop, in space and in time. Geometry decides how much light the crop loses, how evenly it is shared and at which hours it is missing; physiology decides how much yield that costs; and the land equivalent ratio tells you whether sharing the land beats splitting it.

Sharing sunlight between crops and modules

The idea is older than most solar parks: Goetzberger and Zastrow (1982) proposed raising solar collectors about 2 m above a field and spacing them so that crops below still receive enough light. The name agrivoltaics and the land equivalent ratio as the yardstick came with the Montpellier experiments of Dupraz et al. (2011). Today two families of design dominate (DIN, 2021):

  • Overhead systems (category I in DIN SPEC 91434): modules on a table at least 2.1 m, often 4–6 m, above the ground; machinery drives underneath. The German research plant at Heggelbach (194 kWp on one third of a hectare, 5 m clearance, bifacial modules facing south-west) is the best-documented example (Trommsdorff et al., 2021).
  • Interspace systems (category II), here vertical bifacial "fences": modules stand upright in rows far apart, crops grow between the rows. Facing east and west, both sides of a bifacial module collect light. Sweden's first agrivoltaic research facility, at Kärrbo prästgård outside Västerås (59.55° N), was established in 2021 by Mälardalen University researchers with vertically mounted bifacial modules in north–south rows and a conventional ground-mounted system for comparison (Campana et al., 2024; Ma Lu et al., 2024).

Direct and diffuse light need different treatment

Sunlight reaches the crop in two forms. The direct beam comes from one direction, so a crop point is either in the shadow of a panel or not — the laboratory decides this with a ray cast: a straight line from the crop point towards the sun either hits a module rectangle or passes by. The diffuse light comes from the whole sky; a point receives the fraction of it that corresponds to the part of the sky it can "see", the sky view factor. On an overcast Swedish day almost all light is diffuse, so shading is weak but unavoidable; on a clear day in Tucson the beam dominates and the shadows are sharp. The laboratory splits the global irradiance into beam and diffuse with the Erbs et al. (1982) correlation and uses NASA POWER's monthly cloudiness for each site.

The geometry explains most design rules. The ground-coverage ratio (GCR, module width over row spacing) fixes, to first order, what fraction of the beam the modules intercept. Height does not change how much light is intercepted, but it spreads the shadow: a high table's shadow travels far across the field during the day, a low one's hardly moves. Orientation decides when the shade falls: Trommsdorff et al. (2021) found that turning the Heggelbach rows about 30° from south distributed the ground radiation more evenly.

How crops respond to less light

Leaves do not use extra light at noon on a sunny day efficiently: photosynthesis saturates. A crop therefore loses less than proportionally when some of the brightest hours are shaded. How much less depends on the crop. A meta-analysis of shading experiments (Laub et al., 2022) found that berries, fruits and fruit vegetables even gained yield with up to 30 % less radiation, that forages, leafy vegetables, tuber crops and C₃ cereals lost less than proportionally at moderate shade, and that maize and grain legumes lost more than proportionally — maize kept only about 45 % of its yield at 40 % shade. Lettuce, for example, compensates by growing larger, thinner leaves that intercept more of the light that remains (Marrou et al., 2013). In hot, dry climates shade can also save water and cool the crop (Barron-Gafford et al., 2019), effects that this laboratory does not model; a review of agrivoltaic applications discusses these microclimate effects and which crops are likely to benefit (Weselek et al., 2019).

The laboratory uses a saturating canopy light-response curve (a non-rectangular hyperbola) applied every half hour at every point of the crop, integrated over the growing season. Its saturation parameter was calibrated so that uniform shading reproduces the published responses: this is why the same light map gives different yields for different crops.

Electricity from both sides of the module

Photovoltaic output follows the irradiance on the module plane. It depends on the angle between sun and module (the cosine law), on how much of the module is shaded by the neighbouring row, on the diffuse sky the module can see and on the light reflected from the ground. Bifacial modules also convert light on their rear side, with an efficiency relative to the front given by the bifaciality factor φ (0.7–0.9 for current cell types). Vertical east–west modules turn this into a design principle: both faces are "fronts". Cell temperature matters as well — crystalline modules lose about 0.3–0.4 % of their output per kelvin (Skoplaki & Palyvos, 2009) — and inverters, cables and soiling take about 14 % more (the PVWatts default; Dobos, 2014).

The land equivalent ratio

Dupraz et al. (2011) borrowed a yardstick from intercropping research: the land equivalent ratio, LER = (crop yield in the agrivoltaic system / crop yield of an open field) + (electricity of the agrivoltaic system / electricity of a conventional PV park on the same area). An LER of 1.35 means that growing the same crop and the same electricity on separate fields would need 1.35 ha for every hectare of agrivoltaics. Measured values include 1.56–1.70 in 2017, depending on the crop, and about 1.86 for potatoes in the hot, dry summer of 2018 at Heggelbach (Trommsdorff et al., 2021; Fraunhofer ISE, 2019), and 1.27 (2021) and 1.39 (2022) for ley grass at Kärrbo, where the light for the crop was about 25 % lower on average without a statistically significant yield difference (Campana et al., 2024).

Common misconception

"An LER above 1 means the farm produces more food." It does not: the crop term is below 1 in almost every design, so each agrivoltaic hectare produces less crop than an open field. LER > 1 means that the combination of food and electricity needs less land than producing them apart. Whether that is desirable depends on what the land is needed for, on prices and on the choice of reference — change the reference PV park or the reference crop yield and the LER changes too.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Research is moving from single demonstration plots to crop rotations under the same array (at Kärrbo, barley grown between the vertical rows in 2023 yielded at least as much as the open-field control; Ma Lu et al., 2024), to tracking systems that can turn the modules away when the crop needs light, and to regulations that define what counts as agrivoltaics — Germany's DIN SPEC 91434 limits the land lost to the structure to 10 % (overhead) or 15 % (interspace) and requires at least 66 % of the reference crop yield.

The equations behind the simulation

Symbols: \(\alpha\) solar elevation, \(\gamma_s\) solar azimuth (clockwise from north), \(\beta\) module tilt, \(\gamma_m\) azimuth of the module front face, \(w\) collector width (slant height), \(p\) row pitch, \(h\) clearance. Local solar time is used throughout (12:00 = sun due south).

Position of the sun(A1)
\[ \begin{gathered} \sin\alpha = \sin\phi\,\sin\delta + \cos\phi\,\cos\delta\,\cos\omega \\[4pt] \delta = 0.409\,\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi J}{365} - 1.39\right), \qquad \omega = 15^{\circ}\,(t-12) \end{gathered} \]

the sine of the solar elevation equals the sine of the latitude times the sine of the declination plus the product of their cosines and the cosine of the hour angle; the declination follows the day of the year, and the hour angle turns by 15 degrees per hour away from solar noon.

Follow the derivation
  1. Put the sun on a unit sphere centred on the observer. In equatorial coordinates its direction is fixed by the declination \(\delta\) (its latitude on the celestial sphere) and the hour angle \(\omega\) (Earth's rotation since solar noon).
  2. Rotating from equatorial to horizontal coordinates is a rotation about the east–west axis by the co-latitude \(90^{\circ}-\phi\). The vertical component of the rotated unit vector is \(\sin\alpha\), which gives the formula above (spherical law of cosines).
  3. The azimuth follows from the horizontal components; physics.js returns it clockwise from north. The model then converts \((\alpha,\gamma_s)\) to a unit vector \(\mathbf{s}\) pointing to the sun.
Assumptions and validity

The declination formula is accurate to about 0.5°; atmospheric refraction and the equation of time are ignored, so the sun rises a few minutes early in the model. That is irrelevant for daily light integrals.

Global, beam and diffuse irradiance(A2)
\[ G_h = k_t\,G_{sc}\,E_0\sin\alpha,\qquad D_h = k_d(k_t)\,G_h,\qquad B_n = \frac{G_h - D_h}{\sin\alpha} \]

  2. Shape within the day. The clear-sky model of Haurwitz (1945), \(G_{cs} = 1098\sin\alpha\,e^{-0.059/\sin\alpha}\) W m⁻², is scaled so that its daily integral equals \(k_tH_0\).

Assumptions and validity

A "typical" day for each month: real days alternate between clear and cloudy, which changes the beam–diffuse mix; the monthly mean clearness index with one day shape smooths this out. The circumsolar and horizon brightening of the real sky is ignored (isotropic sky).

Ray casting the direct beam(A3)
\[ \begin{gathered} t^{*} = \frac{\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{C}_k-\mathbf{P})}{\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s}},\qquad \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{P}+t^{*}\mathbf{s} \\[4pt] \text{shaded} \iff t^{*}>0,\quad |(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{C}_k)\cdot\mathbf{e}_u|\le \tfrac{L}{2},\quad |(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{C}_k)\cdot\mathbf{e}_w|\le \tfrac{w}{2} \end{gathered} \]

follow the ray from the crop point P towards the sun until it meets the plane of panel row k; the crop point is shaded if the meeting point Q lies in front of the crop, within the row length L and within the collector width w.

Follow the derivation
  1. A point on the ray is \(\mathbf{P}+t\mathbf{s}\). It lies in the plane of the row when \(\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{P}+t\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{C}_k)=0\), which gives \(t^{*}\).
  2. \(t^{*}\le 0\) means the plane is behind the point (the sun is on the other side): no shade from this row.
  3. Projecting \(\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{C}_k\) on the two in-plane axes gives the position within the rectangle; both must be within half the length and half the width.
  4. The model repeats this for every crop point (64 × 32 points), every row that the ray can reach and every quarter hour of the day. Summing gives the daily light map.
  5. Check with a closed form. For infinitely long rows the shadow on the ground is a band of width \(w\,|\cos\theta|/\sin\alpha\) per row, so the shaded fraction of the ground is \(f_g = \min\!\left(1,\ \text{GCR}\,|\cos\theta|/\sin\alpha\right)\), where \(\cos\theta=\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{s}\) is the cosine of the angle of incidence on the module. Averaged over the gaps, the ray caster reproduces this.
Assumptions and validity

Modules are opaque rectangles (optionally with a uniform transmission τ for gaps between cells); posts, beams and cables are not traced; light scattered by the modules and the ground onto the crop is ignored. Rows are finite, so the ends of the field receive more light — the crop averages use the interior (dashed box).

Sky view factor for diffuse light(A4)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{SVF}(\mathbf{P}) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Omega_{\text{sky}}}\cos\theta_z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[1-(1-\tau)\,b_i\big] \\[6pt] \text{SVF}_{\text{2D}} = \frac{p/2}{\sqrt{(p/2)^2+H^2}} \end{gathered} \]

the sky view factor is the cosine-weighted fraction of the hemisphere above a point that is open sky; the model estimates it by sending N rays in cosine-weighted directions and counting those that are not blocked; for a point midway between two infinitely long walls of height H the integral has a closed form.

Follow the derivation of the 2D formula
  1. For an isotropic sky, the diffuse irradiance on a horizontal element is \(\int L\cos\theta_z\,\mathrm{d}\Omega\); an unobstructed hemisphere gives \(\pi L\), which defines SVF = 1.
  2. For obstacles that are infinitely long, integrate first along their length: the view factor from a horizontal strip to a sky slice between cross-section angles \(\varphi_1\) and \(\varphi_2\) (measured from the zenith) is \(\tfrac12(\sin\varphi_2-\sin\varphi_1)\).
  3. Midway between two walls at distance \(p/2\), the open sky spans \(-\varphi_w \dots \varphi_w\) with \(\tan\varphi_w = (p/2)/H\), so \(\text{SVF} = \sin\varphi_w = (p/2)/\sqrt{(p/2)^2+H^2}\).
  4. Stratified cosine-weighted sampling (rings of equal \(\sin^2\theta_z\)) gives every ray the same weight, so the integral becomes a simple average.
Assumptions and validity

Isotropic sky: the bright circumsolar region is treated as part of the beam. Reflection from the undersides of the modules would add a little light under overhead systems.

Crop light response and relative yield(A8)
\[ \begin{gathered} A(I) = \frac{I + I_k - \sqrt{(I+I_k)^2 - 4\theta I I_k}}{2\theta} \\[6pt] y(\mathbf{P}) = \left(\frac{\sum_{d,t} A\big(I(\mathbf{P},t)\big)}{\sum_{d,t} A\big(I_{\text{open}}(t)\big)}\right)^{\gamma} \end{gathered} \]

Follow the reasoning and the calibration
  1. A leaf (and, less sharply, a canopy) converts light efficiently at low PPFD and ever less efficiently at high PPFD. The non-rectangular hyperbola is the standard empirical form; \(\theta\to 0\) gives a rectangular hyperbola, \(\theta\to1\) a sharp corner at \(I_k\).
  2. Because \(A\) is concave, removing light at bright hours costs little: a crop that saturates early (small \(I_k\)) loses much less yield than light.
  3. Canopy acclimation (larger, thinner leaves, more leaf area) is not modelled explicitly; it is absorbed into \(I_k\), which was calibrated so that a uniform shade reproduces published responses: forages 93 % and leafy vegetables 86 % of the unshaded yield at 40 % shade (Laub et al., 2022); potato 82 % and winter wheat 81 % with about 30 % less radiation at Heggelbach in 2017 (Fraunhofer ISE, 2017).
  4. No value of \(I_k\) can make yield fall faster than light. Maize grain does (45 % at 40 % shade; Laub et al., 2022) because shade reduces kernel set and the harvest index; \(\gamma = 1.75\) on a near-linear C₄ response reproduces this.
Assumptions and validity

Light is the only factor: water, temperature, nutrients and diseases are equal under and outside the panels. In dry or hot climates this underestimates the benefit of shade. Yield gains from shade (berries, fruit) cannot be produced by this model.

\(E_{\text{AV}}, E_{\text{PV,ref}}\)annual electricity per hectare of the agrivoltaic system and of the reference PV park (monofacial, facing south, tilt 30°, GCR 0.45)MWh ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
Follow the reasoning
  1. To produce the agrivoltaic hectare's crop on an open field you need \(Y_{\text{AV}}/Y_{\text{ref}}\) ha; to produce its electricity in a PV park you need \(E_{\text{AV}}/E_{\text{PV,ref}}\) ha.
  2. The sum is the land that separate single-use systems would need to match one hectare of agrivoltaics (Dupraz et al., 2011). LER > 1 means sharing saves land.
  3. Example (Kärrbo-like defaults): \(\bar y = 0.95\), \(\lambda = 0.1\), electricity 0.49 → LER = 0.855 + 0.49 ≈ 1.35.
Assumptions and validity

LER weights a tonne of forage and a megawatt-hour equally in land terms; it says nothing about economic value, food security, landscape or biodiversity. It is sensitive to the references: an optimally tilted, denser PV park lowers the electricity term, a better-managed reference field lowers the crop term.

Why GCR is the master variable

For rows that are long compared with the field width, module area per land area is exactly \(w\,L/(p\,L) = w/p\). The same ratio sets, to first order, the share of the beam intercepted by modules (Eq. A3), so GCR simultaneously raises electricity and lowers crop light — the central trade-off drawn in the last chart.

Where the coefficients come from
  1. Laub et al. (2022) regressed \(\log_{10}\) of relative yield on the reduction in solar radiation and its square, forced through 100 % at zero shade, with a crop-type-specific linear term and a common quadratic term.
  2. The coefficients here were reconstructed from their reported point estimates: berries 114 % at both 20 and 40 % shade, forages 103 % and 93 %, leafy vegetables 86 % at 40 %, maize 45 % and grain legumes 50 % at 40 %; the C₃-cereal slope from their statement that cereals are shade tolerant up to 50 % shade. Solving the berry and forage pairs gives the same curvature \(c\) within 5 %, a consistency check.

Model assumptions

  • Geometry. Each row is one opaque rectangle (length 40 m, width = collector width) with an optional uniform transmission; the modules, posts, beams and inverters in the 3D scene are drawn for realism, but only the module rectangles cast shadows in the light model. Gaps between modules along a row are ignored.
  • Light. Local solar time, no refraction or equation of time. One representative day per month (the 15th) with the NASA POWER monthly clearness index and a Haurwitz clear-sky shape; beam–diffuse split after Erbs et al. (1982); isotropic sky; no light reflected from panels or ground onto the crop. PAR = 2.02 µmol J⁻¹ × global irradiance.
  • Crop. Light at canopy height only; no water, temperature, nutrient or disease effects of the panels. The season is fixed per crop (Northern-hemisphere months). Yield is the season's canopy photosynthesis relative to the open field (Eq. A8) with parameters calibrated to published uniform-shade responses — a calibrated, not a validated, model. Averages use the interior of the field (dashed box) excluding the uncultivated strips.
  • PV. Front and rear irradiance with an isotropic sky, row-to-row beam shading for long rows (no partial-shading electrical losses), ASHRAE incidence-angle modifier, NOCT cell temperature, 14 % system losses, no snow, degradation or clipping. The PV energy is computed for a large array (no end or edge rows).
  • Reference systems. Crop: the same crop in the open field. PV: a conventional ground-mounted park with monofacial modules facing the equator at 30° tilt and GCR 0.45, computed with the same model.
  • Conventional PV park mode sets the crop yield to zero; grazing or mowing under such parks is not credited.
  • Kärrbo-like preset. The research site's exact dimensions are not reproduced; the row spacing of 10 m and the 2.3 m module height are illustrative choices.
