Technology diffusion model

Why do some new foods spread in a decade while others stall at a niche? The Bass diffusion model answers with two numbers: how strongly people respond to outside influence — advertising, media, a regulator's approval — and how strongly they copy each other. In this laboratory four hundred potential adopters take up an innovation in front of you, coloured by Rogers' adopter categories. Add falling prices and marketing with the generalised Bass model, delay the European launch by a novel-food procedure, fit the model to thirty years of real adoption data, and compare transparent scenarios for vertical farming, precision fermentation and cultivated meat.

What to look at first

  1. Watch the population. Each of the 400 circles on the right is 0.25 % of the market potential. When the time cursor passes an agent's adoption time it lights up in the colour of its Rogers category. A white ripple means the agent adopted because of external influence (the \(p\) term); a thin line means it copied an earlier adopter (the \(q\) term). Early on almost every adoption is a ripple; later almost every one is a line.
  2. Read the two curves. The coloured area is the adoption rate — new adopters per year — and the magenta line its integral, the cumulative S-curve. The hatched area at the bottom is the part of the rate caused by external influence. Key idea

Diffusion is a contagion process. Its speed is set by \(p + q\), its shape by \(q/p\), and its ceiling by the market potential \(m\) — which is an assumption about the future, not a fact. Regulation, price and marketing change \(p\), \(q\), \(m\) or the starting date, and because adoption compounds, a delay at the start is felt for decades.

From Rogers to Bass

Everett Rogers described diffusion as communication of an innovation through channels over time in a social system, and grouped adopters into innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards (2.5, 13.5, 34, 34 and 16 %, Lesson 14.4). Frank Bass (1969) turned that picture into a model. The probability that a person who has not yet adopted does so in the next short interval — the hazard — is the sum of a constant external influence \(p\) and an internal influence \(qF\) that grows with the fraction \(F\) who have already adopted. Nothing else is assumed: no network, no individual differences. The model is "mean-field": everyone meets everyone, which is why the agents on the stage are scattered at random.

Two numbers therefore describe a whole diffusion history. A meta-analysis of 213 applications found average values of about \(p = 0.03\) and \(q = 0.38\) per year (Sultan et al., 1990) — useful priors when no data exist yet, as for most new foods.

Reading the curves

The rate curve \(f(t)\) is bell-shaped when \(q > p\), and its peak comes at \(t^* = \ln(q/p)/(p+q)\), when \(\tfrac12 - p/(2q)\) of the potential has adopted (Eq. D3). Rogers' categories can be placed on it as quantiles of the adoption time: the first 2.5 % of eventual adopters are the innovators, the next 13.5 % the early adopters, and so on (Eq. D4). The boundary at 16 % is Moore's "chasm" between enthusiasts and the pragmatic majority. On a Bass curve the category sizes are fixed by definition, but their calendar dates are not: with \(p = 0.03,\ q = 0.38\) the chasm is reached 3.1 years after launch and half the potential after 6.6 years.

How much of the diffusion is "pushed" by external influence? Integrating \(p(1-F)\) over all time gives the share of adopters who responded to external influence rather than to other adopters: \(\tfrac{p}{q}\ln(1 + q/p)\) (Eq. D5) — 21 % for the meta-analytic averages, but 65 % for an advertising-driven product with \(p = 0.08,\ q = 0.1\).

What real data say

The adoption of genetically engineered crops by US farmers is one of the best-documented technology diffusions in agriculture: every June since 2000 the National Agricultural Statistics Service has asked farmers which varieties they planted, and the Economic Research Service publishes the shares. Price, marketing and the generalised Bass model

Bass, Krishnan and Jain (1994) multiplied the hazard by a factor \(x(t)\) that rises when prices fall or advertising grows: \(x = 1 + \beta_P\,\dot P/P + \beta_A \max(0, \dot A/A)\). Because \(x\) only rescales time, the solution is the familiar Bass curve evaluated at an "effective time" \(X(t) = \int_0^t x\,\mathrm d\tau\) (Eq. D6). The same paper explains a puzzle: the plain Bass model fits well even without marketing variables because prices and advertising usually change smoothly, so \(x(t)\) is nearly constant and is absorbed into \(p\) and \(q\). Price matters most when it changes fast — as in the experience curves of Lesson 14.4, where every doubling of cumulative production lowers cost by a learning rate.

Regulation as a delay

A novel-food procedure delays the start of diffusion in the EU; it does not change the curve that follows — unless experience from the first market spills over. The simulator shifts the EU launch by \(d\) years (on average about 3.3 years for an Article 10 application; see the Authorisation timeline simulator) and optionally adds cross-market word of mouth \(\sigma q F_{\text{first}}\) to the EU hazard (Eq. D7). Because the delay falls in the slow early phase, it postpones the whole imitation cascade: with \(p = 0.01,\ q = 0.35\), ten years after the first launch the delayed market has reached 22 % of its potential instead of 50 %. The cost can be summarised as adopter-years lost — the area between the two S-curves.

Common misconception

"The S-curve always ends at 100 %." The Bass curve ends at the market potential \(m\), and \(m\) is itself a forecast. Many food substitutions stall at a partial share — plant-based milks coexist with dairy. In the scenarios above, the choice of \(m\) matters more than \(p\) or \(q\) for the absolute size of each market in 2040.

S-curves versus hype cycles

The consultancy Gartner popularised the "hype cycle": after a technology trigger, expectations rise to a peak of inflated expectations, fall into a trough of disillusionment and recover along a slope of enlightenment to a plateau of productivity (Fenn & Raskino, 2008). A common explanation of the shape, used by Gartner's analysts, is the superposition of a short-lived wave of enthusiasm and a slower S-curve of technological maturity, which is how the last chart draws it. The investment history of alternative proteins looks like a hype cycle — about US$5 billion raised in 2021, US$1.1 billion in 2024 and US$881 million in 2025 (GFI, in Lesson 14.4) — but a systematic review found little empirical support for the hype cycle as a general law (Dedehayir & Steinert, 2016). The two curves measure different things: the S-curve counts adopters, the hype cycle describes attention and expectations. A boom-and-bust in investment is compatible with a perfectly ordinary Bass S-curve in adoption — investors simply extrapolated its steepest part, or mistook the first 16 % for the mass market.

Frontier

Diffusion research now uses agent-based and network models, in which who talks to whom matters, and models in which the market potential \(m\) grows as prices fall. For new foods, the open question is which parameter policy can move: approvals and bans move the launch date, procurement and tastings move \(p\), visible use in schools and restaurants moves \(q\), and cost learning moves \(m\).

The equations behind the simulation

The Bass model: hazard and rate equation(D1)
\[ \frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)} = p + q\,F(t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} = \big[p + qF(t)\big]\big[1-F(t)\big] \]\[ F(0)=0,\qquad N(t) = m\,F(t) \]

the rate at which non-adopters adopt equals the coefficient of innovation plus the coefficient of imitation times the adopted fraction; multiplied by the fraction still available, this gives the growth rate of adoption. This is Eq. 14.4.4 of Lesson 14.4.

Where does the model come from?
  1. Let \(h(t)\) be the hazard: the probability per unit time that a non-adopter adopts. Among the non-adopters \(1-F\), the rate is \(f = h(1-F)\).
  2. Bass assumed \(h\) linear in \(F\): \(h = p + qF\). With \(q = 0\) the model becomes \(F = 1 - e^{-pt}\); with \(p \to 0\) it becomes logistic growth, which needs a seed of adopters.
  3. The simulator integrates the equation numerically (fourth-order Runge–Kutta, 0.02-year steps) so that price effects and spillovers (D6, D7) can be added; without them the numerical solution matches the closed form (D2) to better than 10⁻⁶.
\([p + qF]\) in a⁻¹ times the dimensionless \([1-F]\) → a⁻¹ ✓
Closed-form solution(D2)
\[ F(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-(p+q)t}}{1 + \dfrac{q}{p}\,e^{-(p+q)t}}, \qquad f(t) = \frac{(p+q)^2}{p}\,\frac{e^{-(p+q)t}}{\Big(1 + \dfrac{q}{p}\,e^{-(p+q)t}\Big)^{2}} \]

the adopted fraction is one minus a decaying exponential, divided by one plus the imitation-to-innovation ratio times the same exponential; the rate is its derivative (Eq. 14.4.5).

Follow the derivation
  1. Separate variables: \(\mathrm dF/[(p+qF)(1-F)] = \mathrm dt\), and use partial fractions \(\frac{1}{(p+qF)(1-F)} = \frac{1}{p+q}\left[\frac{q}{p+qF} + \frac{1}{1-F}\right]\).
  2. Integrate: \(\frac{1}{p+q}\ln\frac{p+qF}{1-F} = t + K\); \(F(0) = 0\) gives \(K = \ln p/(p+q)\), so \(\frac{p+qF}{p(1-F)} = e^{(p+q)t}\).
  3. Solve for \(F\) and divide numerator and denominator by \(p\,e^{(p+q)t}\).
  4. Check: \(F(0) = 0\), \(F(\infty) = 1\), \(f(0) = p\).

Follow the derivation
  1. With \(x = e^{-(p+q)t}\) and \(a = q/p\), \(f \propto x/(1+ax)^2\). Setting \(\mathrm df/\mathrm dx = 0\) gives \(ax = 1\), i.e. \(t^* = \ln(q/p)/(p+q)\); substitution gives \(F(t^*)\) and \(f(t^*)\).
  2. Inverting D2 for \(F = s\): \(x = (1-s)/(1 + as)\), which gives \(t_s\).
  3. Setting \(\mathrm d^2 f/\mathrm dt^2 = 0\) gives \((ax)^2 - 4(ax) + 1 = 0\), so \(ax = 2 \pm \sqrt3\), i.e. \(T_{1,2}\). For \(p \ll q\), \(F(T_1) \approx 21\,\%\) and \(F(T_2) \approx 79\,\%\).
  4. Example \(p = 0.03,\ q = 0.38\): \(t^* = \ln 12.67/0.41 = 6.2\) a, \(F(t^*) = 46\) %, \(f(t^*) = 0.111\) a⁻¹; \(T_1 = 3.0\) a, \(T_2 = 9.4\) a.

Why quantiles?

Rogers defined his categories by standard deviations of a normal distribution of adoption times (2.28, 13.59, 34.13, 34.13, 15.87 %, rounded; Eq. 14.4.3). Applying the same shares as quantiles to the Bass distribution keeps the familiar sizes while letting the calendar dates follow the model. Mahajan, Muller and Srivastava (1990) proposed an alternative that derives the category boundaries from the Bass curve itself, using its peak and inflection points (D3), so that category sizes depend on \(p\) and \(q\).

Generalised Bass model (price and advertising)(D6)
\[ \frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)} = \big[p + qF(t)\big]\,x(t) \]\[ x(t) = 1 + \beta_P\,\frac{\dot P(t)}{P(t)} + \beta_A \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{\dot A(t)}{A(t)}\right) \]\[ F(t) = F_{\text{Bass}}\big(X(t)\big),\qquad X(t) = \int_0^t x(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau = t + \beta_P \ln\frac{P(t)}{P(0)} + \beta_A \ln\frac{A(t)}{A(0)} \]

Assumptions and validity

Prices here are exogenous. In reality prices fall because volume grows (experience curves, Eq. 14.4.8), which creates a feedback not included in the closed form. The market potential \(m\) is kept fixed although a lower price would also raise it.

\(\dot P/P\) and \(\dot A/A\) are rates in a⁻¹, so \(\beta_P\) and \(\beta_A\) carry the unit a and \(x\) is dimensionless; in \(X(t)\), a × ln(ratio) → a ✓

Fitting the model to data(D8)
\[ \min_{p,\,q,\,m\,(,\,\tau)}\ \text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i - m\,F(t_i - \tau;\ p, q)\Big]^2 \]\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2} \]

How the fit works
  1. Bass (1969) estimated his model by ordinary least squares on a discrete approximation. Srinivasan and Mason (1986) showed that fitting the continuous solution directly by nonlinear least squares gives unbiased parameters with valid standard errors.
  2. The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (fitLM in the course statistics library) alternates between gradient descent and Gauss–Newton steps. Standard errors come from \(s^2(J^\top J)^{-1}\), with \(J\) the Jacobian of the model at the optimum and \(s^2 = \text{SSE}/(n-k)\).
  3. With early data only, \(p\), \(q\) and \(m\) are strongly correlated: many combinations fit the first points equally well. Fix what you know (for example the launch year) and report the uncertainty.
\(y\) and \(mF\) in the same unit (%); SSE in %² ✓

Model assumptions

  • The Bass model is mean-field: every non-adopter feels the same external influence and the same word of mouth from all adopters. Networks, segments, repeat purchase and disadoption are not modelled; a food that is tried once but not bought again is not "adopted".
  • The market potential \(m\) is constant. In reality it grows as prices fall and products improve, and it can shrink after scandals or bans.
  • The generalised Bass model uses an exogenous exponential price decline until parity and a constant advertising growth during a campaign; the price coefficient \(\beta_P\) and the advertising term are illustrative, not estimated for food products.
  • The EU curve starts at \(t_0 + d\) with the same \(p\), \(q\) and \(m\) as the first market; spillover adds \(\sigma q F_1\) to its hazard. Markets are otherwise independent.
  • The 400 agents are the market potential in rank order; their adoption times are the quantiles of the model curve, and the cause of each adoption (external or imitation) is drawn with probability \(p/(p + qF)\) at its adoption time.
  • USDA data are shares of planted acreage in the United States; the launch year of stacked-trait maize (1999) is an assumption (the series starts in 2000). The three future scenarios are teaching assumptions, not forecasts.
  • The hype-cycle curve is conceptual; it is drawn as a Gaussian wave of expectations plus a scaled Bass S-curve.
