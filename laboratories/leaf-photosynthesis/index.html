Inside a photosynthesising leaf

Shrink to the size of a cell and enter a leaf. This laboratory couples a microscopic 3D leaf — cuticle, epidermis, palisade and spongy mesophyll, a vein and working stomata — to the model that plant physiologists use to predict photosynthesis: the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry biochemistry with Bernacchi temperature functions and the Medlyn stomatal model. Change light, CO₂, temperature, humidity and oxygen, and watch photons being absorbed, CO₂ diffusing to the chloroplasts and O₂ and water vapour leaving — every stream is driven by the computed fluxes. Then fly into a chloroplast.

A guided tour through the leaf

  1. Read the cross-section. The block is 440 × 220 µm and about 230 µm thick — roughly the thickness of two sheets of paper. From the top: a glossy cuticle, a clear upper epidermis, tall palisade cells lined with chloroplasts, loosely packed spongy mesophyll with large air spaces, a vein (xylem above, phloem below, surrounded by a bundle sheath) and a lower epidermis with stomata. Click any tissue to identify it; the 100 µm scale bar sits at the front corner.
  2. Follow the photons. Each streak is a photon of the colour it would have. Blue and red photons are absorbed within the first few cell layers; green photons penetrate deeper and some are reflected — the reason leaves look green. Turn PPFD down to 50 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and up to 2000 and watch the flux of streaks and absorption flashes change.
  3. Follow a CO₂ molecule. CO₂ glyphs (grey carbon, red oxygens) enter through the stomata, cross the substomatal cavity and the air spaces and disappear at chloroplasts, where Rubisco fixes them. O₂ leaves by the same route, and water vapour (blue) streams out much faster: for every CO₂ molecule a well-watered C3 leaf fixes, it loses a few hundred water molecules. Press Stoma to look at the pore from below and change the VPD: the guard cells close and the streams thin out.
  4. Watch the chloroplasts move. At low light the chloroplasts gather at the upper, light-facing end of the palisade cells; at high light they spread down the side walls. This avoidance response is real (it is driven by the blue-light receptor phototropin) and protects the photosynthetic machinery from excess light.
  5. Remove photorespiration. Switch O₂ to 2 %. Net assimilation of a C3 leaf jumps by roughly a third and the photorespiration readout collapses, because Rubisco no longer wastes energy fixing O₂. Now switch to C4: the O₂ effect disappears, because the C4 pump already keeps CO₂ high around Rubisco — and the bundle sheath turns green (Kranz anatomy).
  6. Fly into a chloroplast. Inside, grana (stacks of thylakoids) glow with the electron-transport rate J, the Calvin–Benson cycle turns at a rate set by carboxylation, CO₂ enters and sugar (triose phosphate) leaves at the net rate A, and O₂ bubbles away from the thylakoids at J/4.
Key idea

Photosynthesis is limited by whichever of three processes is slowest — Rubisco (the enzyme that fixes CO₂), RuBP regeneration (fed by electron transport, i.e. by light) or triose-phosphate use (sugar export) — and by how much CO₂ the stomata let in. Every grower's lever (light, CO₂, temperature, humidity) acts through one of these.

Scale

The leaf block uses 1 scene unit = 10 µm. Molecules are drawn as large glyphs so you can see them; each CO₂ glyph stands for about 4 × 10¹⁰ molecules (the legend in the 3D view updates the exact number), and each H₂O glyph for 125 times more.

From a photon and a CO₂ molecule to a sugar

A leaf is a gas-exchange organ built around a compromise. It must take in CO₂ from air that contains only about 0.04 % of it, while losing as little water as possible to air that is usually far drier than the leaf interior. Both gases share one route: the stomatal pores. The cuticle blocks almost everything else.

Anatomy is built for light and gas

The palisade cells are columns aligned with the incoming light. Light can travel down their vacuoles and between them, spreading absorption through the layer instead of saturating the top cells (the “sieve” and “light-guide” effects). The spongy mesophyll below is full of air spaces; these distribute CO₂ from the stomata to every cell and scatter light sideways, lengthening its path. Blue and red photons are absorbed strongly by chlorophyll and are used up in the upper palisade; green photons are absorbed more weakly and reach the lower chloroplasts, which is why, under strong white light, green light drives photosynthesis in the deeper layers (Terashima et al., 2009). Leaf anatomy therefore sets both how evenly light is used and how easily CO₂ reaches the chloroplasts (Terashima et al., 2011).

Three biochemical limits

Rubisco is a slow and indiscriminate enzyme: besides CO₂ it also reacts with O₂. Each oxygenation starts photorespiration, a salvage pathway that releases half a CO₂ per O₂ fixed and costs ATP and NADPH. The CO₂ concentration at which carboxylation gains exactly balance photorespiratory losses is the photorespiratory compensation point Γ* (≈ 43 µmol mol⁻¹ at 25 °C in air). Γ* is proportional to the O₂ concentration and rises steeply with temperature, because Rubisco's preference for CO₂ over O₂ and the solubility of CO₂ relative to O₂ both fall as the leaf warms (Bernacchi et al., 2001). That is why photorespiration is suppressed at 2 % O₂, why CO₂ enrichment helps most in warm greenhouses, and why the temperature optimum of a C3 leaf moves upwards at high CO₂.

Stomata: the supply side

The biochemistry sets a demand for CO₂ that rises with Cᵢ; diffusion through the stomata sets a supply, \(A = g_s (C_a - C_i)/1.6\), that falls as Cᵢ approaches Cₐ. The leaf operates where the two meet — the operating point in the A–Cᵢ chart (Farquhar & Sharkey, 1982). Stomata do not open at random: across hundreds of species their behaviour is close to an optimum that maximises carbon gain for a given water loss. The Medlyn et al. (2011) model derived from this theory predicts that gₛ scales with A/Cₐ and falls with the square root of the vapour-pressure deficit D; its single parameter g₁ reflects how “expensive” water is for the plant. Crops that evolved with ample water have high g₁ (≈ 5.8 kPa^½ for C3 crops), C4 grasses low g₁ (≈ 1.6) (Lin et al., 2015; De Kauwe et al., 2015).

Common misconception

“More light always means more photosynthesis.” Above a few hundred µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ a C3 leaf is often no longer limited by light but by Rubisco or TPU. Doubling light then barely helps, while raising CO₂ can help a lot. Use the light-response chart to find where the curve bends for different CO₂ levels.

The C4 alternative

C4 plants such as maize, sorghum and sugar cane first fix CO₂ in the mesophyll with PEP carboxylase (which does not react with O₂), then release it in the bundle sheath around the veins, where Rubisco sits at a CO₂ concentration several times that of the air. Photorespiration almost disappears, A saturates at low Cᵢ, and the plant can keep its stomata narrower (low g₁) — higher water-use efficiency. The price is extra ATP per CO₂ fixed, so at low temperature and low light C3 plants often do as well or better. In the 3D view, switching to C4 fills the bundle-sheath cells with chloroplasts — the “Kranz” (wreath) anatomy.

What it means for controlled-environment agriculture

  • CO₂ enrichment (typically to 800–1000 ppm in greenhouses and plant factories) raises A mainly by suppressing photorespiration and by moving the operating point up the steep part of the A–Cᵢ curve; the gain is largest in warm, bright conditions and small when the leaf is already TPU- or light-limited.
  • Light should be matched to CO₂ and temperature: at 1000 ppm the light-response curve saturates later, so more photons pay off.
  • Humidity (VPD) acts through the stomata: very dry air closes them and lowers Cᵢ and A; very humid air keeps them open but reduces transpiration, which has its own risks (see the stomata and transpiration laboratory).
  • Temperature has an optimum that depends on CO₂ and on the temperature the crop was grown at (acclimation — try the growth-temperature slider).
Research frontier

Engineering photorespiratory bypasses, faster recovery from photoprotection, and introducing C4-like or algal CO₂-concentrating mechanisms into C3 crops are among the most active routes to raising crop yield potential. The model in this laboratory is the standard tool used to estimate what such changes could gain.

The equations behind the simulation

The model computes, for given light, CO₂, O₂, leaf temperature and VPD, the net CO₂ assimilation rate A, the stomatal conductance gₛ and the intercellular CO₂ concentration Cᵢ that are simultaneously consistent with the biochemistry (Eqs. P1–P6) and with stomatal behaviour and diffusion (P7–P8). Symbols: CO₂ in µmol mol⁻¹, O₂ in mmol mol⁻¹, fluxes in µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, conductances in mol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Rubisco-limited assimilation(P1)
\[ A_c = V_{c\max}\,\frac{C_i-\Gamma^*}{C_i + K_c\left(1+O/K_o\right)} \;-\; R_d \]

When there is plenty of RuBP, the rate is set by how fast Rubisco can carboxylate: a Michaelis–Menten function of CO₂ with O₂ as a competitive inhibitor, reduced by the CO₂ lost in photorespiration (the Γ* term) and by day respiration.

Follow the derivation
  1. Rubisco carboxylation with O₂ as competitive inhibitor: \(V_c = V_{c\max} C_i/[C_i + K_c(1+O/K_o)]\).
  2. For every carboxylation there are \(\phi = V_o/V_c\) oxygenations; each oxygenation releases ½ CO₂ in photorespiration. Net carboxylation is \(V_c - \tfrac12 V_o = V_c(1-\tfrac{\phi}{2})\).
  3. Because both reactions compete for the same site, \(\phi = O/(S_{c/o} C_i)\), where \(S_{c/o}\) is Rubisco's specificity. Defining \(\Gamma^* = \tfrac12 O/S_{c/o}\) gives \(\tfrac{\phi}{2} = \Gamma^*/C_i\).
  4. So the net rate is \(V_c(1-\Gamma^*/C_i) - R_d = V_{c\max}(C_i-\Gamma^*)/[C_i + K_c(1+O/K_o)] - R_d\).
Assumptions and validity

Rubisco is fully activated and RuBP-saturated. Kc, Ko and Γ* are the in-vivo values of Bernacchi et al. (2001), expressed on a Cᵢ basis (i.e. assuming infinite mesophyll conductance). With a finite mesophyll conductance, the same equations apply with the chloroplast concentration Cc < Cᵢ (and Cc-based kinetic constants).

RuBP-regeneration (electron-transport) limited assimilation(P2)
\[ A_j = J\,\frac{C_i-\Gamma^*}{4\,C_i + 8\,\Gamma^*} \;-\; R_d \]

When RuBP regeneration is limiting, the rate is set by the supply of NADPH from electron transport: each carboxylation needs four electrons, each oxygenation also four, and photorespiration returns ½ CO₂ per oxygenation.

Follow the derivation
  1. Regenerating the RuBP consumed by one carboxylation and \(\phi\) oxygenations requires NADPH equivalent to \(4 + 4\phi\) electrons: \(J = V_c(4 + 4\phi)\).
  2. With \(\phi = 2\Gamma^*/C_i\): \(V_c = J/(4 + 8\Gamma^*/C_i) = J C_i/(4C_i + 8\Gamma^*)\).
  3. Net: \(A_j = V_c(1-\Gamma^*/C_i) - R_d = J(C_i-\Gamma^*)/(4C_i+8\Gamma^*) - R_d\).
Assumptions and validity

NADPH (not ATP) limits RuBP regeneration; von Caemmerer (2000) uses the coefficients 4 and 8, Farquhar et al. (1980) used 4.5 and 10.5 (ATP limitation). The difference is ≈ 10 %.

Electron transport from absorbed light(P3)
\[ \theta J^2 - \left(I_2 + J_{\max}\right)J + I_2 J_{\max} = 0,\qquad I_2 = I\,\frac{a\,(1-f)}{2} \]

Electron transport rises linearly with light at first (slope set by the light absorbed by photosystem II) and bends smoothly towards its maximum Jmax; θ sets how sharp the bend is. The smaller root of the quadratic is used.

Follow the derivation
  1. At low light, J = I₂ (every photon absorbed by PSII moves one electron); at high light J → Jmax.
  2. The non-rectangular hyperbola \(\theta J^2 - (I_2+J_{\max})J + I_2J_{\max}=0\) connects the two limits; θ = 0 gives a rectangular hyperbola, θ = 1 the sharp minimum \(J=\min(I_2, J_{\max})\).
  3. Solving: \(J = \dfrac{I_2 + J_{\max} - \sqrt{(I_2+J_{\max})^2 - 4\theta I_2 J_{\max}}}{2\theta}\).
  4. With a = 0.85, f = 0.15: I₂ = 0.361 I. The maximum quantum yield of Aⱼ at high Cᵢ is then 0.361/4 ≈ 0.09 CO₂ per incident photon, and at ambient Cᵢ ≈ 0.06 — close to measured values for C3 leaves.

Triose-phosphate-use limitation(P4)

If the chloroplast makes triose phosphate faster than the cell can turn it into sucrose and starch, phosphate is not recycled quickly enough and photosynthesis stalls at three times the triose-phosphate-use rate.

Optimal stomatal conductance (Medlyn model)(P7)
\[ g_s = g_0 + 1.6\left(1 + \frac{g_1}{\sqrt{D}}\right)\frac{A}{C_a} \]

Stomatal conductance to water vapour is a small residual g₀ plus a term proportional to assimilation divided by CO₂ at the leaf surface, reduced when the air is dry (large D). g₁ expresses how much water the plant is prepared to spend per unit carbon gained.

Diffusive supply and the coupled solution(P8)
\[ A = \frac{g_s}{1.6}\,\left(C_a - C_i\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{C_i}{C_a} = \frac{g_1}{g_1+\sqrt{D}}\quad(g_0 = 0) \]

How the laboratory solves it
  1. A depends on Cᵢ (P1–P5) and gₛ depends on A (P7), while Cᵢ depends on gₛ (P8): a circular problem.
  2. Define \(f(C_i) = A(C_i) - \tfrac{g_s(A(C_i))}{1.6}(C_a - C_i)\): demand minus supply.
  3. f is negative at Cᵢ = 0 and positive at Cᵢ ≫ Cₐ, so the root is bracketed; we halve the interval 48 times (bisection) until it is narrower than 0.02 µmol mol⁻¹.
  4. With g₀ = 0: substituting P7 in P8 gives \(A = (1+g_1/\sqrt D)(A/C_a)(C_a - C_i)\), so \(1 - C_i/C_a = 1/(1+g_1/\sqrt D)\) and \(C_i/C_a = g_1/(g_1+\sqrt D)\).

  3. Is CO₂ enrichment worth it in a plant factory?

    Choose the CO₂-enriched plant factory preset (300 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, 22 °C). Compare A at 425 and 1000 ppm, then repeat at 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Where is the relative gain larger and why (look at the limiting process and the photorespiration readout)? Would you rather buy more LEDs or more CO₂?

  4. Moving optimum

    From the temperature-response chart, find the temperature optimum of A at 425 ppm and at 1000 ppm (PPFD 1200). Explain the shift using Γ* and photorespiration. Then change the growth temperature from 15 to 30 °C: what happens to the optimum and to A at 38 °C?

  5. C3 versus C4 on a hot afternoon

    Apply Hot afternoon and note A, gₛ, E and WUE. Switch to C4 (defaults are applied) and compare. By what factor does WUE change? Now set 15 °C and 300 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹: which pathway wins, and why might northern European crops still be mostly C3?

  6. Hunting for TPU limitation

    Use the Cool, high CO₂ preset. Show that A no longer responds to O₂ or to more CO₂. Increase TPU₂₅ until another process takes over. Why can growers see no benefit from extra CO₂ on cool, bright days?

Model assumptions

  • Steady state: A, gₛ and Cᵢ are solved for instantaneous equilibrium; stomatal opening and closing kinetics (minutes) are not modelled here (see the stomata laboratory).
  • The leaf is well stirred (boundary-layer conductance infinite, Cₛ = Cₐ) and the leaf-to-air VPD is set directly, as in a leaf gas-exchange cuvette. Leaf temperature is an input; the leaf energy balance is treated in the stomata laboratory.
  • Mesophyll conductance is taken as infinite, so Rubisco sees Cᵢ; the Bernacchi et al. (2001) kinetic constants are the matching Cᵢ-based values. In real leaves the chloroplast concentration Cc is noticeably lower than Cᵢ.
  • C3 limitations are combined by a strict minimum; the TPU limitation assumes no glycolate carbon export (α = 0). Rd = 0.015 Vcmax,25 and is not reduced in the light.
  • J follows a non-rectangular hyperbola with a = 0.85, f = 0.15, θ = 0.7 for all temperatures; the spectrum of the light is not considered.
  • Temperature responses: Arrhenius energies from Bernacchi et al. (2001, 2003) with high-temperature deactivation of Vcmax and Jmax from Kattge & Knorr (2007) — this combination is a documented modelling choice, not a single published parameter set.
  • C4: simplified von Caemmerer (2000) model with the parameters and temperature functions of von Caemmerer (2021); Vpmax = 5 Vcmax; O₂ has no effect.
  • Stomata: Medlyn et al. (2011) with g₀ = 0.01 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The 3D pore width is derived from gₛ with the pore-diffusion equation (P11) for the 14 stomata shown (a hypostomatous leaf).
  • The 3D anatomy is a faithful but schematic reconstruction (cell sizes and layer thicknesses typical of a dicot leaf). Photon absorption depths and reflectances by colour are illustrative; particle streams are proportional to the computed fluxes but their paths are drawn, not simulated by diffusion. Chloroplast avoidance movement is represented qualitatively.
