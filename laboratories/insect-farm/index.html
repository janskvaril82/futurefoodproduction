Key idea

An insect farm is a bioreactor made of crates. Feed carbon is split three ways — into larval biomass, into CO₂ (with the metabolic heat that comes with it) and into frass. Temperature controls how fast larvae grow and how much they respire; stocking density controls how much heat each crate makes; ventilation must remove that heat, the CO₂ and the evaporated water. Good farms operate where growth is fast but the larval mass does not overheat.

What the numbers mean

FCR in this lab is kg feed dry matter per kg fresh larval gain; ECI is dry gain per dry feed. Literature values use different bases (feed as fed or dry, feed offered or eaten), so always check the basis before comparing — the lesson on the protein transition explains why.

Two insects, two production systems

The yellow mealworm (Tenebrio molitor) is the larva of a darkling beetle. It is reared on dry cereal by-products such as wheat bran, with carrots or other fresh vegetables as a source of water, and is authorised as a novel food in the EU. Its larval phase is long: 76–183 days depending on diet at 28 °C (Oonincx et al., 2015) and about 125–140 days to pupation at 25–30 °C on a bran-based feed (Eberle et al., 2022). The black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) is a fly whose larvae devour moist organic material; they are reared mainly as feed (meal and oil for fish, pigs and poultry) and to convert by-products. The larval phase lasts 2–3 weeks at 27–30 °C (Chia et al., 2018; Tomberlin et al., 2009).

Growth and temperature

Insects are ectotherms: their body temperature, and with it every metabolic rate, follows the temperature around them. Development rate rises almost linearly above a lower threshold, peaks, and collapses a few degrees above the optimum — the asymmetric shape captured by the Brière model (Eq. I2). Mealworms grew fastest at 31 °C in Bjørge et al. (2018), but development and fecundity were best at 27 °C (Morales-Ramos et al., 2025) and survival falls above about 35 °C. Black soldier fly larvae develop fastest at 31–36 °C, yet only 0.1 % of larvae reared at 36 °C became adults in Tomberlin et al. (2009): fastest is not the same as best.

Where does the feed go?

Follow the carbon. Of every kilogram of carbon a larva eats, a fraction is not digested and leaves as frass; the digested (assimilated) carbon either builds body mass or is respired to CO₂ to pay for growth and for maintenance (Eq. I3). The slower the larvae grow, the longer they pay maintenance, and the more carbon ends as CO₂ — which is why mealworms, with their long larval phase, convert feed less efficiently than black soldier fly larvae (Nielsen et al., 2025, found average carbon net growth efficiencies of 0.16–0.40 for mealworms and 0.33–0.56 for BSF larvae).

A crate is a heater

Every gram of CO₂ a larva breathes out releases about 11.4 kJ of heat (Eq. I6). Oonincx et al. (2010) measured 61 g CO₂ per kg of mealworm larvae per day at 25 °C — about 8 W per kilogram, the power of a small light bulb for every few kilograms of larvae. In a crate, that heat must cross the substrate by conduction and leave the surface by convection and by evaporation of water (Figure 2). If it cannot, the larval mass warms up, respires faster and warms up further. Black soldier fly larvae at high density raise their substrate at least 10 °C above the air (Li et al., 2023), and doubling the density in lab trials raised temperatures by 0.6–2.4 °C (Klammsteiner et al., 2025).

Designing the climate

Ventilation must satisfy three demands at once (Eq. I8): remove the sensible heat with supply air a few kelvin cooler than the room; keep CO₂ below the chosen limit (5000 ppm is the 8-hour occupational exposure limit value for workers in the EU); and remove the water vapour evaporated from the substrate. Whichever needs the most air sets the design. Try it: for mealworms the heat usually governs; for black soldier flies the moisture does — a BSF room is as much a drying plant as a farm.

What may insects eat? EU rules as of 2026

Since Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/893, insects reared to produce processed animal protein are farmed animals under the animal by-products regulation (EC) No 1069/2009. They are therefore subject to the same feed bans as pigs or chickens: they may be fed materials of vegetal origin and a short list of animal-derived materials (for example milk and eggs and their products, honey and rendered fat), but not manure, catering (kitchen) waste, or former foodstuffs containing meat or fish (EFSA, 2015 discusses the hazards behind these rules). Insect frass may be placed on the market as a fertiliser after heat treatment at 70 °C for 60 minutes (Commission Regulation (EU) 2021/1925). For human food, the EU has authorised four insect species as novel foods — Tenebrio molitor (dried, frozen and powder forms, and since January 2025 a UV-treated powder), Locusta migratoria, Acheta domesticus and Alphitobius diaperinus; the black soldier fly is authorised for feed but not as a novel food.

Common misconception

“Insects are always more sustainable than meat.” Per kilogram of edible protein, mealworms need far less land and emit fewer greenhouse gases than chicken, pork or beef — but they used more energy than chicken and about as much as pork in the first life-cycle assessment (173 MJ kg⁻¹, of which gas for heating the rearing rooms was the largest single share, 35 %; Oonincx & de Boer, 2012), and 85 % of their land use was for feed grains. The advantage depends on the feed: larvae fed on food-grade grain compete with people; larvae fed on legal by-products do not.

History

The FAO report Edible insects: future prospects for food and feed security (van Huis et al., 2013) put insect farming on the global agenda. It compiled the figure that crickets need about 2.1 kg of feed per kg of edible weight, compared with 4.5 kg for chicken, 9.1 kg for pork and 25 kg for beef.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Current research combines climate engineering (using larval heat, heat recovery), automation of feeding and sieving, genetics and breeding of insect strains, and the search for legal, low-value side streams — at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences in Uppsala, for example, Lalander et al. (2019) showed that the volatile-solids and protein content of urban waste fractions largely decide how well BSF larvae convert them.

The equations behind the simulation

For each batch the simulator integrates, day by day (Euler steps of 0.1 d for mealworms and 0.02 d for BSF), the mean fresh mass \(m\) of a larva and the number of survivors \(N\) in one 60 × 40 cm crate, together with the temperature of the larval mass. Feed, frass, CO₂, heat and evaporation are accumulated along the way.

Larval growth: logistic (BSF) and Gompertz (mealworm)(I1)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = r(T_{\text{l}})\,m\left(1-\frac{m}{m_{\max}}\right)\ \ \text{(BSF)},\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = r(T_{\text{l}})\,m\,\ln\frac{m_{\max}}{m}\ \ \text{(mealworm)} \]

a larva grows at a rate proportional to its own mass times a braking term: in the logistic model the brake is the fraction of the maximum mass still to go; in the Gompertz model it is the logarithm of the ratio between maximum and current mass, which slows growth earlier and more gradually.

Why these two curves, and how were they calibrated?
  1. BSF larvae grow almost exponentially through the first instars and then level off as they approach the prepupal stage — well described by the logistic (Verhulst) model, which Eriksen (2022) used as the kinetic backbone of a dynamic energy-budget model for BSF larvae.
  2. With \(r = 0.60\) d⁻¹ at 28 °C a neonate reaches 90 % of its maximum in about 16 days, matching the 250 degree-days above 12.3 °C that Chia et al. (2018) measured for the larval stage (\(250/15.7 = 15.9\) days). Nielsen et al. (2025) measured maximal specific growth rates of 0.50–0.77 d⁻¹.
  3. Mealworm growth decelerates over a long larval life. The Gompertz form (in the parameterisation of Zwietering et al., 1990) starts with a high specific rate — 19 % per day for 1 mg larvae here, close to the 16.6 % d⁻¹ maximum of Bjørge et al. (2018) — which falls to about 4–5 % d⁻¹ at 50 mg, as in Eberle et al. (2022).
  4. Harvest is triggered at 85 % (mealworm) or 90 % (BSF, prepupae) of \(m_{\max}\); at 27 °C this takes ≈ 90 days for mealworms, within the 76–183 days reported by Oonincx et al. (2015).

Assumptions and validity

One crate, one-dimensional, uniform larvae; larvae in reality move towards comfortable zones and churn the substrate, and microbial heat is ignored. The wetness values are assumptions chosen to reproduce the observation that dense BSF substrates run about 10 °C above the air (Li et al., 2023) and that the water evaporated per kg of BSF larvae (≈ 2.7 kg) matches the drying of a wet substrate into frass.

Put the number in perspective

A soybean field yielding 3 t ha⁻¹ with 36 % protein produces about 0.1 kg protein per m² per year. A mealworm room can produce tens of times more per m² of floor — but the feed must come from somewhere: in the life-cycle assessment of Oonincx & de Boer (2012), 85 % of the land use of mealworm protein was for feed grains.

Model assumptions

  • One representative crate (60 × 40 cm) per species, with uniform larvae; the room holds many such crates at staggered stages, so room loads use batch averages. Feed is offered ad libitum; the model computes what is eaten.
  • Growth follows Gompertz (mealworm) or logistic (BSF) kinetics with a Brière-1 temperature response evaluated at the temperature of the larval mass; diets change the rate, the maximum mass, digestibility and background mortality (calibrated factors).
  • Carbon balance with a constant cost of growth (Y = 0.44) and Q10 = 2 maintenance; constant body composition; feed carbon fraction 0.45 and larval carbon fraction 0.50 are assumptions.
  • Heat from respiration via the oxycaloric equivalent; microbial heat and CO₂ of the substrate are not included; the crate heat balance is one-dimensional, with an assumed surface wetness and an empirical convection coefficient; larvae do not move to cooler zones.
  • Climate: supply air conditioned to 60 % RH at a fixed temperature below the room; outdoor CO₂ 420 ppm; no infiltration, no lighting or equipment loads; floor area per stack 0.48 m² including aisles.
  • Life cycle: mealworm stage durations interpolated from one laboratory study; BSF from a linear degree-day model (valid roughly 15–35 °C).
  • Numbers in the comparison chart are literature values with their own system boundaries; ranges for chicken, pork and beef in the GHG, land and energy views were derived from the ratios reported by Oonincx & de Boer (2012).
  • The 3-D facility, the larvae and their movement are visualisations; sizes, numbers visible, colours and substrate appearance follow the model.
