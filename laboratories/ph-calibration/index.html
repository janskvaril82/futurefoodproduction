pH electrode calibration

A hydroponic nutrient solution is kept between pH 5.5 and 6.5 — a window only ±0.5 units wide — so the pH meter must be right to within a few hundredths. Here you calibrate a virtual glass electrode exactly as a technician does: rinse, dip it into temperature-corrected buffers, wait for the reading to stabilise, record, and let the meter compute slope and offset. Then judge the electrode against the manufacturer's criteria, measure five real-world samples with and without temperature compensation, and discover what an ageing electrode does to your data.

A calibration, step by step

The bench carries a bench-top pH/mV meter with automatic temperature compensation, a combination glass electrode on a stand, a beaker of storage solution (3 mol L⁻¹ KCl), a beaker of deionised rinse water, the three technical buffers pH 4.01, 7.00 and 10.01, and six samples in the front row. Click any beaker to move the electrode there; press the meter's CAL key to record a buffer and READ to record a reading.

  1. Rinse, then pH 7.00. Click the rinse beaker (the reading wanders: deionised water has almost no ions to carry the current), then the green pH 7.00 buffer. Watch the response chart: the potential approaches its new value exponentially. When ✓ stable appears on the display, press CAL.
  2. Rinse, then pH 4.01, CAL. After the second buffer the meter computes the calibration. For the new electrode the slope is about 99 % and the offset a few millivolts: "Electrode in good condition". The meter used the buffer values for the actual temperature — at 20 °C the pH 7.00 buffer is really 7.02.
  3. Measure the samples. Rinse and READ each sample in the front row. Look at the log: the true pH of the cold DWC tank (11 °C) and of the acidified water is recovered only with ATC; without it, the error grows with the distance from pH 7 and from the calibration temperature.
  4. The rainwater sample takes much longer to stabilise and stays noisy: with few ions the liquid junction and the glass membrane respond slowly. This is real, and a reason to measure low-conductivity water with a dedicated electrode.
  5. Age the electrode. Choose Aged and calibrate again with three buffers. The slope drops to about 91 % and the offset rises to about 24 mV: "needs cleaning". Stabilisation now takes minutes. Record one point before the stability mark and see what it does to the residuals.
  6. Drift. After calibrating, press Wait 1 hour twice and READ in the pH 7.00 buffer. The red diamonds in the residual chart show how far the electrode has drifted from its calibration.
  7. The 82 % electrode. Load the preset of that name and calibrate: the meter refuses ("Err 6"). The Investigate tab asks what you should do.
Key idea

A pH meter measures a voltage; calibration with two or more buffers determines the two numbers — slope and offset — that turn it into pH at the electrode's present condition and temperature. The quality of every later reading rests on the buffers' true values at their temperature, on waiting for a stable signal, and on how quickly the electrode drifts away from its calibration.

Why a pH electrode must be calibrated

A combination electrode is two half-cells in one body: a thin pH-sensitive glass membrane with an internal buffer and Ag/AgCl element, and a reference Ag/AgCl element in KCl that contacts the sample through a porous junction (Lesson 9.2). The voltage between them follows the Nernst equation: it changes by \(s = \ln(10)RT/F\) — 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C — and is zero at about pH 7, the isopotential point set by the internal buffer (Buck et al., 2002; Galster, 1991).

A real electrode deviates in two ways that change with age and use. Its slope is a little smaller than Nernstian — the ratio is the slope efficiency η — and its zero point is shifted by an offset (asymmetry, reference and junction potentials). Two unknowns need two equations: two buffers of known pH. A third buffer tests whether the response is really a straight line and averages out random errors.

Buffers are not constant either

A buffer's pH depends on temperature because its acid–base equilibria do. The technical buffers used here are labelled with their value at 25 °C; the manufacturer's table gives, for example, 7.02 at 20 °C and 7.06 at 10 °C for the neutral phosphate buffer, and 10.06 at 20 °C and 10.18 at 10 °C for the carbonate buffer (Mettler-Toledo, 2015). A meter with a temperature probe looks these values up automatically; a meter without one (or with the table switched off) assumes the label value and builds the error into the calibration.

Waiting for a stable reading

After immersion the gel layer of the glass and the liquid junction need time to equilibrate, and the electrode body must reach the solution temperature. The lab treats the potential as a first-order response with time constant τ (a new electrode a few seconds, an aged or fouled one a minute or more) and the temperature sensor with its own lag. The meter declares a reading stable with the manufacturer's criteria: Medium, no change of more than 0.1 mV in 6 s; Strict, 0.03 mV in 8 s or 0.1 mV in 30 s; Fast, 0.6 mV in 4 s (Mettler-Toledo, 2011). A strict criterion gives a more reliable value but waits longer — and a noisy electrode may never pass it.

Two temperature effects that are often confused

The electrode effect: the Nernst slope is proportional to absolute temperature, 55.2 mV pH⁻¹ at 5 °C and 63.1 mV pH⁻¹ at 45 °C. Automatic temperature compensation (ATC) rescales the calibrated slope to the sample temperature. The error it removes is zero at pH 7 and grows with the distance from pH 7 and from the calibration temperature. The solution effect: the true pH of the sample itself changes with temperature. ATC does not — and should not — correct it; report pH together with the measurement temperature.

Good practice checklist

Use fresh buffers and never pour them back · rinse with deionised water and blot, do not rub · bracket your samples (4.01 and 7.00 for nutrient solutions) · wait for the stability mark · calibrate at, or close to, the sample temperature · record slope and offset in your log · re-check a buffer at the end of the session · store the electrode wet in KCl, never in deionised water.

Common misconception

"A calibrated electrode with 91 % slope is as good as a new one — the calibration corrects the slope." Calibration corrects the slope and offset at that moment. An electrode with a low slope usually also responds slowly, drifts faster and is noisier, so its readings are less reliable between calibrations. The slope is a symptom; the cause needs cleaning.

The equations behind the meter

Electrode potential with slope efficiency, offset and drift(PH1)

Follow the derivation
  1. Nernst equation for the H⁺-selective glass membrane: \(E = E^0 + (RT/F)\ln a_{\text{H}^+}\).
  2. With \(\ln a = \ln(10)\log_{10}a\) and \(\text{pH} = -\log_{10}a_{\text{H}^+}\): \(E = E^0 - s(T)\,\text{pH}\).
  3. The internal buffer and reference elements are chosen so that \(E \approx 0\) at pH 7 for every temperature: \(E = -s(T)(\text{pH} - 7)\) — the isopotential point.
  4. A real membrane delivers a fraction η of the theoretical slope, and the zero point is shifted by \(E_0\), which creeps with time: PH1. The course library function electrodeMV(pH, T, slopeEff, offset) implements it without the drift term.
Assumptions and validity

The isopotential point is assumed exactly at pH 7 for all temperatures, the offset does not depend on temperature, and the response is linear from pH 2 to 12 (no acid or alkaline error). Electrode presets are illustrative combinations of η, \(E_0\), τ, drift and noise chosen to span the manufacturer's diagnostic categories; they are not measurements of specific electrodes.

First-order response and the time to a stable reading(PH2)
\[ E(t) = E_{\text{eq}} + (E_{\text{start}} - E_{\text{eq}})\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad t_{\text{stable}} = \tau\ln\!\left[\frac{|\Delta E|\,(1 - e^{-w/\tau})}{\delta}\right] \]

after immersion the potential approaches its equilibrium exponentially; the reading passes a criterion "changes by no more than delta over a window w" once the remaining change in one window has fallen below delta.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\tau\)response time constant of the electrode (× 4 in low-ionic-strength water)s
\(\Delta E\)size of the step, \(E_{\text{start}} - E_{\text{eq}}\)mV
\(w, \delta\)stability window and tolerance (Medium: 6 s, 0.1 mV)s, mV
Follow the derivation
  1. Treat the electrode as a first-order system (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.2): \(\tau\,\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}t = E_{\text{eq}} - E\); for a step the solution is the exponential above.
  2. The change during the window from \(t - w\) to \(t\) is \(|\Delta E|\,e^{-t/\tau}(e^{w/\tau} - 1) = |\Delta E|\,e^{-(t-w)/\tau}(1 - e^{-w/\tau})\).
  3. Setting this (evaluated at the start of the window) equal to δ and solving for the time gives \(t_{\text{stable}}\).
  4. Example: a new electrode (τ = 4 s) moved from pH 7 to pH 4 at 20 °C (|ΔE| ≈ 174 mV) meets the Medium criterion after \(4\ln(174\times0.777/0.1) = 29\) s; an aged one (τ = 28 s, |ΔE| ≈ 161 mV) after about 160 s; a failing one (τ = 75 s) after about 6 min.
Assumptions and validity

Real glass electrodes often show a fast initial response followed by a slow creep, so the stable time is longer than a single exponential predicts. Noise adds to the change within the window; if the noise band exceeds δ the criterion is never met. The meter's own input filter (0.7 s in the lab) is assumed.

\(\tau\ln(\text{mV}\cdot 1/\text{mV})\): s × dimensionless = s ✓
Temperature-corrected buffer value(PH3)
\[ \text{pH}_b(T) = \text{pH}_i + \left(\text{pH}_{i+1} - \text{pH}_i\right)\frac{T - T_i}{T_{i+1} - T_i},\qquad T_i \le T \le T_{i+1} \]

the buffer's pH at the measured temperature is interpolated linearly between the two neighbouring entries of the manufacturer's table.

Follow the derivation

  2. Without temperature measurement the meter must use the 25 °C value. The calibration then contains an error \(\text{pH}_b(T) - \text{pH}_b(25)\) in each buffer: at 10 °C, +0.06 in the pH 7.00 buffer and +0.17 in the pH 10.01 buffer (≈ 10 mV).
  3. An error δ in one buffer of a two-point calibration tilts the line: the slope changes by \(\delta/(\text{pH}_2 - \text{pH}_1)\) relative to one pH unit, and every sample far from that buffer inherits a proportional error.

Values are for fresh, uncontaminated technical buffers of this manufacturer's group (Mettler-Toledo, 2015); other brands' buffers (e.g., 7.01 or 10.00 labels) have their own tables. Alkaline carbonate buffers absorb CO₂ from the air once opened, which lowers their pH — use them fresh.

Two- and three-point calibration by least squares(PH4)
\[ S = \frac{\sum_i(\text{pH}_i - \overline{\text{pH}})(E_i - \bar E)}{\sum_i(\text{pH}_i - \overline{\text{pH}})^2},\qquad a = \bar E - S\,\overline{\text{pH}},\qquad \eta = \frac{-S}{s(\bar T_{\text{cal}})},\qquad E_0 = a + 7S \]

the slope is the covariance of pH and potential divided by the variance of the buffer pH values; the intercept makes the line pass through the mean point; the slope efficiency is the magnitude of the slope divided by the Nernst slope at the mean calibration temperature; the offset is the line's potential at pH 7.

Follow the derivation
  1. Minimise \(\sum_i (E_i - a - S\,\text{pH}_i)^2\): setting the derivatives with respect to \(a\) and \(S\) to zero gives the normal equations and the formulas above (Lesson 9.3, Eq. 9.3.5). The meter uses the same least-squares fit for three points.
  2. With two points the line passes exactly through both, \(S = (E_2 - E_1)/(\text{pH}_2 - \text{pH}_1)\), and the residuals are zero — so two points cannot reveal a bad buffer or an unstable reading. A third point can.
  3. Example (new electrode, 20 °C, ATC on): pH 7.02 → +2.3 mV and pH 4.00 → +176.8 mV give \(S = (176.8 - 2.3)/(4.00 - 7.02) = -57.78\) mV pH⁻¹, \(\eta = 57.78/58.16 = 99.3\,\%\) and \(E_0 = 2.3 + (7 - 7.02)(-57.78) = +3.5\) mV — the preset values are recovered.
Assumptions and validity

The response is assumed linear; residuals of a few tenths of a millivolt come from noise, drift during the calibration, incomplete stabilisation and buffer errors. The criteria for slope and offset are those programmed into the manufacturer's meter (Mettler-Toledo, 2015).

Sample pH with and without temperature compensation(PH5)
\[ \text{pH}_x = 7 - \frac{E_x - E_0}{\eta\,s(T_u)},\qquad T_u = \begin{cases} T_x & \text{(ATC)}\\ \bar T_{\text{cal}} & \text{(no ATC)}\end{cases},\qquad \Delta\text{pH}_{\text{noATC}} = (\text{pH}_x - 7)\left(\frac{T_x}{\bar T_{\text{cal}}} - 1\right) \]

the sample pH is 7 minus the sample potential's distance from the offset divided by the calibrated slope, evaluated at the sample temperature with ATC or at the calibration temperature without; the error of omitting ATC is the distance from pH 7 times the relative difference of the absolute temperatures.

Follow the derivation

  2. With ATC, only \(s\) is re-evaluated at \(T_x\) (η and \(E_0\) are assumed independent of temperature).
  3. Without ATC the meter uses \(s(\bar T_{\text{cal}})\) while the electrode produced \(\eta s(T_x)(\text{pH}_x - 7)\): the ratio of the two slopes, \(T_x/\bar T_{\text{cal}}\), multiplies the distance from pH 7.

ATC corrects the electrode (Nernst) effect only; the solution's own temperature dependence is chemistry and remains in the result. The temperature sensor in the electrode body lags the solution (τ ≈ 12 s in the lab), so wait for both readings to settle.

Drift and the recalibration interval(PH6)
\[ \Delta\text{pH}(t) = -\frac{\dot E_0\,t}{\eta\,s(T)},\qquad t_{\max} = \frac{\Delta\text{pH}_{\max}\,\eta\,s(T)}{|\dot E_0|} \]

an offset that drifts at a constant rate shifts every reading by the accumulated millivolts divided by the calibrated slope; the longest acceptable time between calibrations is the tolerated pH error times the slope divided by the drift rate.

Constant drift rates are an idealisation: real drift is often fastest just after cleaning or refilling and depends on the samples (clogging junctions). Re-check a buffer at the end of every session and let the record set your interval.

Investigation tasks

Work in the simulator and keep the session log; Download log gives you the data for your report, and Save investigation stores settings and results in your notebook.

  1. A complete calibration

    With the new electrode at 20 °C, perform a correct two-point calibration (4.01 + 7.00), rinsing in between and waiting for the stability mark. Record slope and offset and compare them with the preset values. Then READ all six samples with ATC. Which samples have errors above 0.05 pH, and why?

  2. How much does ATC matter?

    Using the log (it shows both variants), plot the error without ATC against \((\text{pH}_x - 7)(T_x/T_{\text{cal}} - 1)\) for the six samples. Do the points fall on the 1 : 1 line predicted by Eq. PH5? For which crop system in your project would you insist on ATC?

  3. The impatient technician

    Choose Aged and a 3-point calibration. Calibrate once properly and once pressing CAL about 10 s after each immersion. Compare slope, offset and residuals, and the error on the NFT sample. Estimate from Eq. PH2 how long each buffer really needed.

  4. A cold calibration without a temperature probe

    Set the buffers to 10 °C, switch ATC off and calibrate with 7.00 + 10.01. Which buffer values did the meter assume, and which are true (Table 1)? Measure the aquaponic sump and explain the error. Repeat with ATC and the buffer table switched on.

  5. Drift and the recalibration interval

    Calibrate the One year electrode, then alternate Wait 1 hour and a READ in the pH 7.00 buffer for five hours. Fit a straight line to the drift (red diamonds) and compare it with the drift slider. With Eq. PH6, how often must a DWC system with this electrode be recalibrated to stay within ±0.05 pH?

  6. Diagnose the 82 % electrode

    Load The 82 % electrode. Try a calibration with the Medium and then the Fast stability criterion. Write a one-paragraph service note: what the meter reports, which physical problems could cause a low slope, a large offset, slow response and noise at the same time, which cleaning steps you would try in which order, and when you would replace the electrode.

Model assumptions

  • Electrode. Linear Nernstian response with slope efficiency η and offset \(E_0\) about an isopotential point at pH 7 (Eq. PH1); no acid or alkaline error, no temperature dependence of η or \(E_0\); constant offset drift. The four electrode presets are illustrative combinations chosen to fall into the four diagnostic categories of Table 2 — they are not data from specific electrodes.
  • Dynamics. First-order potential response with time constant τ, four times slower and four times noisier in deionised water and rainwater (illustrative factors); first-order thermal response of the built-in temperature sensor (τ = 12 s, assumed); in the air the potential wanders (open circuit). The meter smooths its input with a 0.7 s filter (assumed).
  • Noise. White Gaussian noise on the potential; the slider sets the rms of raw readings taken ten times per second (the meter's 0.7 s input filter reduces it about fourfold). Real electrodes also show low-frequency noise and sensitivity to static charge and stirring.
  • Buffers. Values from the manufacturer's table (buffer group B1, 5–40 °C) with linear interpolation; buffers are assumed fresh and uncontaminated, and at the set temperature.
  • Meter. Linear (least-squares) calibration through the chosen buffers; diagnostic limits and the rejection of slopes outside 85–110 % follow Mettler-Toledo (2015); stability criteria follow Mettler-Toledo (2011). Without ATC the meter assumes 25 °C for buffer values and uses the calibration slope unchanged.
  • Samples. The six samples are illustrative, with a "true" pH defined at their own temperature (as a reference laboratory would report it). Rinse carry-over, CO₂ uptake by alkaline buffers, junction potentials in samples of very different ionic strength and the electrode's alkaline error are not modelled.
