Authorisation timeline simulator

A novel food cannot be sold in the European Union until it is on the Union list — and nobody can tell a start-up exactly when that will be. This laboratory turns the procedure of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 into a stochastic model calibrated on the durations of 292 real applications and checked against eighteen real authorisations. Watch a single application move through validity checks, EFSA's risk assessment with its clock stops and the vote of the Member States; then run a thousand of them to see the whole distribution of time-to-market, the chance of success and the levers that shorten the wait most.

What to look at first

  1. Watch one application. The animation follows a single simulated Article 10 application from submission to decision. Green bars are the European Commission, blue bars the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), magenta the Member States in the PAFF Committee. The amber hatched blocks are clock stops: EFSA has asked for more data and waits for the applicant. Notice the dashed "legal deadline" on EFSA's row: it moves to the right with every clock stop, because the nine months of Article 11 are net time.
  2. Press "New application" (the dice) five or six times. The same rules and the same parameters give totals from under two to more than five years. That variability, not the average, is what a company has to plan for.
  3. Read the histogram. It is skewed to the right: the median is shorter than the mean, and the slowest tenth of dossiers takes around five years. The magenta curve tells you the probability of being on the market by a given date — it levels off at the probability of authorisation, because roughly one dossier in four fails (invalid, negative opinion, withdrawn or no qualified majority).
  4. Compare the presets "Excellent dossier" and "Weak dossier". The legal clocks are identical, yet the median moves by more than two years and the probability of success from about 90 % to about 40 %. The quality of the application is the largest variable in the procedure.
  5. Look at the lever chart. Which improvement saves most time — a faster EFSA, a faster Commission, or fewer and quicker answers from the applicant? The answer tells a regulatory-affairs manager where to invest effort before submission.
  6. Switch the route to "Traditional food". Without objections a notification is decided in about a year. Raise the probability of a safety objection and watch a second hump appear in the histogram: the long detour through an Article 16 application.
  7. Compare jurisdictions in the last chart. Be careful: some bars are real cases, some are official targets and some are only the regulator's own maximum review time.
Key idea

An authorisation procedure is a chain of random durations and yes/no decisions. Adding the legal time limits (about 19 months for an Article 10 application, Eq. T7) badly underestimates reality; adding average durations (about 3.3 years) hides the risk. A Monte Carlo model gives what a business plan needs: the distribution of time-to-market and the probability of success.

The procedure and its clocks

Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 offers two routes for a novel food that is not yet on the Union list (see the Novel food navigator for how to decide). The application route (Articles 10–12) is open to every novel food; the notification route (Articles 14–20) only to traditional foods from third countries with at least 25 years of safe use. Figure 1 shows both with their legal clocks.

Three legal details decide how long the procedure really takes:

  • Clock stops. When EFSA asks for additional information, it fixes a period for the answer after consulting the applicant, and unless the Commission objects within eight working days the nine months are "automatically extended by that additional period" (Article 11(4)). Information sent on the applicant's own initiative does not extend the clock (Article 11(6)); if the applicant does not answer in time, EFSA gives its opinion on the data it has (Article 11(5)).
  • Unregulated waiting. The validity check must be done "without delay", and EFSA's suitability check has no deadline at all. In 2018–2024 these two steps together took almost ten months on average (Le Bloch et al., 2025).
  • Risk management. The seven months of Article 12(1) run from the publication of the opinion to the submission of the draft to the committee. The vote, adoption, publication in the Official Journal and entry into force twenty days later come on top.

Two regularities stand out. First, the real Article 10 totals range from about 2.4 to 5.5 years with a median of about 3.4 years — close to, but slightly slower than, the model's median calibrated on the 2018–2024 averages. The early insect dossiers were submitted when the new regulation was only months old. Second, once a traditional-food notification has been forwarded, authorisation followed after 232–324 days in all seven cases: the four-month objection window plus about five months to adopt the implementing act. "Without delay" in Article 15(4) means months in practice.

Why simulate instead of adding averages?

The expected total is the sum of the expected phase durations (Eq. T1) — that part is simple. But a start-up burning cash does not care about the mean. It asks: what is the probability that we are on the market before our money runs out? Three features make that question hard to answer with pencil and paper:

  1. Skewed phases. Every duration is positive and has a long right tail (the validity check ranged from 0 to 1,430 days). Sums of such variables are skewed too, so the median is shorter than the mean and the 90th percentile lies far out.
  2. Random numbers of random durations. The number of clock stops is itself random, and each stop has a random length. The variance of such a compound sum has two parts (Eq. T3).
  3. Failure points. An application can be declared invalid, withdrawn, receive a negative opinion or fail in committee. The probability of success is a product of stage probabilities (Eq. T6), and failures cut off the distribution of times.

Monte Carlo simulation (Metropolis & Ulam, 1949) handles all three by brute force: draw every random quantity from its distribution, follow the rules of the regulation, record the outcome, and repeat a thousand times. Percentiles and probabilities are then simply counted.

Common misconception

"EFSA must decide within nine months, so an assessment cannot take longer." The nine months are net of clock stops, which on average added almost a year (353 days). EFSA's evaluation lasted 629 days on average and up to 1,714 days — and in a quarter of cases EFSA also exceeded its own net nine months.

The traditional-food shortcut

A traditional food from a third country — for example fonio grains from West Africa or haskap berries with a history of safe use in Japan — can be notified instead. Without a reasoned safety objection there is no EFSA opinion and no committee vote, and the seven cases in Table 3 were authorised 8.4 to 19.1 months after notification. The risk lies in the objection window: an objection sends the file into an Article 16 application with EFSA's six-month clock, clock stops and a committee vote, and the whole route can then take longer than a direct Article 10 application. No statistic on the frequency of objections has been published, so the probability is a parameter for you to explore. Remember too that data protection is not available for traditional foods (Article 26(3)).

Other jurisdictions

The last chart places the EU beside the cases analysed in Lesson 14.3. Compare like with like: Singapore's approval of Aleph Farms' cultivated beef took about 46 months from application; Food Standards Australia New Zealand gazetted Vow's cultured quail about 28 months after receiving the application; the UK Food Standards Agency's sandbox aims at about 2.5 years for a routine, non-complex application. The US figures are of a different kind: under the 2016 GRAS rule the FDA answers a GRAS notice within 180 days of filing, extendable by 90 days, and the 2026 proposal would allow up to 405 days — but those clocks exclude the notifier's own preparation, and a self-affirmed GRAS conclusion needs no FDA review at all. In the EU, the first application for a cultivated-meat product (duck cells, July 2024) was still pending in September 2026.

Frontier 2025–2026

Regulators now compete on speed. In the UK sandbox for cell-cultivated products, the average time to the first request for further information fell from 195 days before the sandbox to 90 days, and to 46 days for requests issued in 2026 (FSA, 2026). In the EU the equivalent lever is the applicant's side: EFSA's pre-submission advice, the mandatory notification of studies and complete dossiers written to EFSA's 2024 guidance. Duration of an Article 10 procedure(T1)

Follow the derivation
  1. The phases follow each other without overlap, so the total is their sum.
  2. The expectation of a sum equals the sum of the expectations — no independence assumption is needed for this step.
  3. The clock stops form a random sum. If \(n\) is independent of the stop lengths, Wald's identity gives \(\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n} R_i\right] = \mathbb{E}[n]\,\mathbb{E}[R]\).
  4. With the model values: \(114 + 185 + 276 + 2.7\times130 + 48 + 290 + 21 \approx 1{,}285\) days \(\approx 3.5\) years for the mean of a completed procedure; the median is shorter (≈ 3.3 years) because the distribution is right-skewed.

Clock stops as a compound random sum(T3)
\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^{n} R_i,\quad n \sim \text{NB}\big(\bar n,\ \text{Var}\,n = 1.34\,\bar n\big),\quad R_i \sim \text{LN}(\bar R,\ s_R) \]\[ \mathbb{E}[S] = \bar n\,\bar R, \qquad \text{Var}[S] = \bar n\, s_R^{2} + \text{Var}(n)\,\bar R^{2} \]

the total waiting time is the sum of a random number of random response times; its mean is the mean number of stops times the mean response, and its variance has one part from the variable response times and one from the variable number of stops.

A lognormal duration from a published mean and SD(T4)
\[ X = e^{\mu + \sigma Z},\ Z \sim N(0,1): \qquad \sigma^2 = \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{s^2}{m^2}\right), \qquad \mu = \ln m - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}, \qquad \tilde X = e^{\mu} \]

a positive, right-skewed duration with mean m and standard deviation s is modelled as the exponential of a normal variable; the log-scale parameters follow from the coefficient of variation, and the median is e to the power mu.

Follow the derivation (method of moments)
  1. For a lognormal variable, \(\mathbb{E}[X] = e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\) and \(\text{Var}[X] = (e^{\sigma^2}-1)\,e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}\).
  2. Dividing the variance by the squared mean: \(s^2/m^2 = e^{\sigma^2}-1\), hence \(\sigma^2 = \ln(1 + s^2/m^2)\).
  3. Taking the log of the mean: \(\ln m = \mu + \sigma^2/2\), hence \(\mu = \ln m - \sigma^2/2\).
  4. Example, validity check: \(m = 114,\ s = 181\) d ⇒ \(\sigma = 1.12\), \(\mu = 4.11\), median \(= e^{4.11} = 61\) d. Half of all validity checks take less than two months, but the mean is pulled up by a few very long ones.
Assumptions and validity

The lognormal is a convenient, positive, right-skewed shape; the data do not prove it is the true distribution. Values are truncated at the largest observed duration where relevant.

Monte Carlo estimates and their precision(T8)
\[ \hat P = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}\{\text{run } j \text{ authorised by } t\}, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat P) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat P(1-\hat P)}{N}}, \qquad \text{SE}(\hat t_{0.5}) \approx \frac{1}{2\,f(t_{0.5})\sqrt{N}} \]

a probability is estimated by the fraction of simulated runs in which the event happened; its standard error shrinks with the square root of the number of runs. The median's standard error depends on the density of outcomes at the median.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(N\)number of simulated applications–
\(\hat P\)estimated probability (e.g., on the market within 3 years)–
\(f(t_{0.5})\)probability density of the duration at the mediana⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Each run is a Bernoulli trial with success probability \(P\); the count of successes is binomial with variance \(NP(1-P)\).
  2. Dividing by \(N\): \(\text{Var}(\hat P) = P(1-P)/N\).
  3. Example: \(\hat P = 0.28\) (on the market within 3 years) with \(N = 1000\): \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.28\times0.72/1000} = 0.014\) — about ±3 percentage points at 95 %. Press "Re-run Monte Carlo" to see this sampling noise.
  4. For quantiles, the asymptotic variance is \(p(1-p)/(N f^2)\); for the median \(p = 0.5\), giving the formula above.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator to answer. Record the settings and results with Save investigation, or download the runs as CSV and analyse them in the Statistics workbench.

  1. Mean, median, tail

    With the preset EU average 2018–24, record the mean, median, P10 and P90 of the time to authorisation. Why is the mean larger than the median? Run the Monte Carlo five times and estimate how precise the median is (compare with Eq. T8).

  2. Validate the model

    Table 2 lists eleven real procedures. In which percentile of the simulated distribution does each one fall? If the model were perfect, the percentiles would be spread evenly between 0 and 100. What pattern do you see, and which features of the early insect dossiers (submitted 2018–2019) could explain it?

  3. Where should a start-up invest?

    Use the lever chart. Rank the levers by months saved. Then propose three concrete actions before submission (for example EFSA pre-submission advice, notifying every study in EFSA's database, generating stability and batch data early) and argue which parameter each one changes and by how much.

  4. Cash runway

    A precision-fermentation start-up has cash for 36 months after submission. Read from the magenta curve the probability that it reaches the market in time with an average, an excellent and a weak dossier. How much would the excellent dossier be worth if the company is worth €40 million on the market and nothing if it runs out of money?

  5. Notify or apply?

    A Peruvian fruit with 40 years of documented use could go either route. Find the probability of objection at which the median of the traditional-food route equals the median of an Article 10 application. Which other consideration (hint: Article 26(3)) might still make an Article 10 application attractive?

  6. Which jurisdiction first?

    Using the comparison chart and Lesson 14.3, argue whether a cultivated-meat company should file in Singapore, the UK or the EU first. Distinguish carefully between real cases, official targets and legal review clocks.

Model assumptions

  • Phase durations for the Article 10 route are drawn independently from lognormal distributions with the means and standard deviations of Le Bloch et al. (2025) (Eq. T4); the validity check is truncated at the longest observed value (1,430 d).
  • EFSA's running clock follows Eq. T2; the number of clock stops is negative-binomial with mean 2.7 and variance 1.34 × mean; responses are lognormal (130 ± 103 d). The stops are placed at uniformly random points of the running clock.
  • The risk-management phase (publication of the opinion → adoption of the implementing regulation) is lognormal with median 243 d and log-SD 0.60, fitted to the eleven procedures of Table 2 after subtracting the mean publication lag. It includes the PAFF vote; Article 21 extensions are represented only through this spread.
  • Invalidity under the Transparency Regulation (10.3 %) applies at EFSA's suitability check after 297 ± 121 days. Negative opinions (13.2 %) end the procedure after publication. Withdrawal (2 % per clock stop) and failure in comitology (2 %) are assumptions — no published rates exist.
  • The traditional-food route uses the validity checks and adoption lags of the seven notifications in Table 3. The probability of a safety objection and the time to prepare an Article 16 application are assumptions; after an objection the model uses EFSA's six-month clock with 70 % of the Article 10 clock stops and a Commission phase scaled by 3/7 (the ratio of the legal limits in Articles 18 and 12).
  • Phases are independent, parameters do not change over time, and Article 22 ad hoc extensions, requests to modify an application and parallel health-claim procedures (Article 28) are not modelled. The model describes the procedure; it says nothing about the safety of any food.
  • International comparisons mix documented single cases (Singapore, Australia–New Zealand), an official target (UK) and legal maximum review times (USA). They are not like-for-like statistics.
