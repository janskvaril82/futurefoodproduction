What to look at first

  1. Lower the fixtures to 0.2 m with the PPFD map view. Bright hot-spots appear under each fixture and dark valleys in between: the canopy sees the individual lamps. Now raise them to 0.8 m. The pattern smooths out — each point on the canopy now receives light from several fixtures — but the mean PPFD drops.
  2. Switch the walls from none to reflective film. The mean PPFD at the edges jumps: photons that would have left the growing area are sent back. This is why grow tents are lined with reflective film.
  3. Narrow the beam. Narrow optics concentrate light directly below each fixture. With few fixtures this creates stripes; with many fixtures close together it keeps photons on the bench.
  4. Change the photon efficacy from an older 1.7 µmol J⁻¹ fixture (typical of high-pressure sodium) to a modern 3.0 µmol J⁻¹ LED. The PPFD map does not change if you keep the photon output constant — but the electricity cost almost halves.
  5. Click on the canopy to place the quantum sensor. Compare its reading at the centre and at a corner. Real growers do exactly this with a handheld PAR meter on a grid.
Key idea

Lighting design is a compromise between intensity (enough photons for the crop's DLI), uniformity (every plant gets the same), capture efficiency (photons land on leaves rather than on walls and floors) and cost (every photon was paid for in electricity).

How light from a fixture reaches a leaf

A fixture emits a photosynthetic photon flux (PPF, µmol s⁻¹): the number of photons between 400 and 700 nm leaving the fixture each second. The crop cares about the photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD, µmol m⁻² s⁻¹): how many of those photons arrive on each square metre of leaf.

Three geometric facts turn PPF into PPFD:

  1. Directional emission. An LED board with a diffuser is close to a Lambertian emitter: its intensity is highest straight down and falls off with the cosine of the angle from the vertical. Lenses can concentrate the beam; we model this with a cosine power \(\cos^n\theta\), where larger \(n\) means a narrower beam.
  2. Inverse-square law. The same photons spread over a sphere whose area grows with the square of the distance, so irradiance falls as \(1/d^2\).
  3. Cosine (Lambert) law of incidence. A surface tilted away from the source intercepts fewer photons; for a horizontal canopy the factor is \(\cos\theta\).

Adding the contributions of all fixtures — and of their mirror images in reflective walls — gives the PPFD at every point on the canopy. The simulation evaluates this on a grid of about 2,000 points each time you move a slider.

Why do walls matter so much?

A specular (mirror-like) wall behaves as if a second, slightly dimmer fixture sat behind it — the image source. Its strength is the wall reflectance \(\rho\) times the original. Reflective film (\(\rho\approx 0.9\text{--}0.95\)) returns nearly all photons that would have escaped; matt white paint (\(\rho\approx 0.8\)) reflects diffusely, which the model approximates with the same image method. We include first-order images in all four walls and second-order images in the corners.

From PPFD to DLI and to money

The crop integrates photons over the day: the daily light integral (DLI, mol m⁻² d⁻¹). The electricity meter integrates watts: kilowatt-hours. The ratio of the two — how many kilowatt-hours it costs to deliver one mole of photons to the leaves — is the single most useful number for comparing lighting designs.

The equations behind the simulation

Beam intensity of a cosine-power emitter(L1)
\[ I(\theta) = I_0 \cos^{n}\theta, \qquad I_0 = \frac{(n+1)\,\Phi_p}{2\pi} \]

the photon intensity in a direction \(\theta\) below the fixture equals the on-axis intensity times the cosine of the angle raised to the beam exponent; the on-axis intensity is fixed by requiring that all the emitted photons add up to the fixture's PPF.

\(I(\theta)\)photon intensity (photons per second per steradian) at angle \(\theta\) from the downward axisµmol s⁻¹ sr⁻¹
\(I_0\)on-axis intensityµmol s⁻¹ sr⁻¹
\(n\)beam exponent: 1 = Lambertian (wide), 2 = medium, 5 = narrow lens–
\(\Phi_p\)photosynthetic photon flux of the fixture (PPF)µmol s⁻¹
Follow the derivation of \(I_0\)
  1. The total flux is the intensity integrated over all directions into the lower hemisphere: \(\Phi_p = \int I\,\mathrm{d}\Omega\).
  2. In spherical coordinates \(\mathrm{d}\Omega = \sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi\), with \(\theta\in[0,\pi/2]\) and \(\varphi\in[0,2\pi]\).
  3. \(\Phi_p = I_0\int_0^{2\pi}\!\mathrm{d}\varphi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^n\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta = 2\pi I_0\left[-\frac{\cos^{n+1}\theta}{n+1}\right]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{2\pi I_0}{n+1}.\)
  4. Solving for the on-axis intensity gives \(I_0=(n+1)\Phi_p/(2\pi)\). For a Lambertian emitter (\(n=1\)) this is the familiar \(I_0 = \Phi_p/\pi\).
Assumptions and validity

The fixture is small compared with the distance to the canopy (far-field). Linear bars are therefore split into five point sources along their length. Real fixtures publish measured intensity distributions (IES/LDT files); the cosine-power family is the standard analytical approximation.

\([I_0] = \dfrac{\mu\text{mol s}^{-1}}{\text{sr}}\): the factor \((n+1)/2\pi\) has units of sr⁻¹ because a solid angle was integrated out.
PPFD at a point on the canopy(L2)
\[ E = \sum_{k} \rho_k\,\frac{I_{0,k}\,\cos^{\,n+1}\theta_k}{d_k^{2}}, \qquad \cos\theta_k = \frac{h}{d_k},\quad d_k=\sqrt{h^2+r_k^2} \]

the photon flux density at a point is the sum, over every real and mirrored source \(k\), of its intensity towards that point, times the cosine of incidence, divided by the squared distance.

\(E\)PPFD at the canopy pointµmol m⁻² s⁻¹
\(\rho_k\)1 for a real source, \(\rho\) for a first-order image, \(\rho^2\) for a corner image–
\(h\)mounting height above the canopym
\(r_k\)horizontal distance between source \(k\) and the pointm
\(d_k\)straight-line distancem
Follow the derivation
  1. Intensity is photons per second per steradian. A small canopy patch \(\mathrm{d}A\) at distance \(d\), tilted by \(\theta\) relative to the ray, subtends a solid angle \(\mathrm{d}\Omega=\mathrm{d}A\cos\theta/d^2\).
  2. The photons per second arriving on the patch are \(I(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = I_0\cos^n\theta\cdot \mathrm{d}A\cos\theta/d^2\).
  3. Dividing by \(\mathrm{d}A\) gives the flux density \(E = I_0\cos^{n+1}\theta/d^2\). For a horizontal canopy the ray angle and the incidence angle are the same \(\theta\).
  4. Because \(\cos\theta = h/d\), directly below a Lambertian fixture (\(\theta=0\)) \(E = I_0/h^2\): doubling the height quarters the PPFD directly underneath.
  5. Contributions from independent sources add linearly, so the total is a sum.
Assumptions and validity

Leaves are treated as a flat horizontal plane (no canopy depth, no leaf angle distribution, no mutual shading). Photons reflected by leaves and by the floor are ignored. Walls reflect specularly. These simplifications typically overestimate edge PPFD with matt walls by 5–15 %.

\(\dfrac{\mu\text{mol s}^{-1}\,\text{sr}^{-1}}{\text{m}^2}\) — steradians are dimensionless, so the result is µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. ✓
Canopy photon capture efficiency(L3)
\[ \eta_{\text{cap}} = \frac{\bar E\,A_{\text{canopy}}}{\sum_j \Phi_{p,j}} \]

the fraction of all emitted photons that land on the growing area equals the mean PPFD times the canopy area divided by the total PPF of all fixtures.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\bar E\)area-averaged PPFD on the canopyµmol m⁻² s⁻¹
\(A_{\text{canopy}}\)canopy (bench) aream²
\(\Phi_{p,j}\)PPF of fixture \(j\)µmol s⁻¹
Why can this exceed 100 %?

It cannot physically. If the numbers suggest otherwise, the reflectance assumptions are too optimistic: with walls of reflectance 0.95 the image model counts almost every escaping photon as returned. Real rooms lose photons to floor absorption, aisles and multiple reflections; well-designed vertical farms reach 70–90 %.

\(\dfrac{\mu\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\cdot \text{m}^2}{\mu\text{mol s}^{-1}} = 1\) — dimensionless. ✓
Electricity per mole of photons delivered(L4)
\[ \frac{\text{kWh}}{\text{mol}} = \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{ph}}\;\eta_{\text{cap}}}\cdot\frac{10^{6}\ \mu\text{mol mol}^{-1}}{3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J kWh}^{-1}} = \frac{0.278}{\eta_{\text{ph}}\,\eta_{\text{cap}}} \]

the kilowatt-hours needed to put one mole of photons on the leaves equals 0.278 divided by the product of the fixture's photon efficacy and the capture efficiency.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\eta_{\text{ph}}\)photon efficacy of the fixture (PPF per electrical watt)µmol J⁻¹
\(\eta_{\text{cap}}\)canopy capture efficiency (Eq. L3)–
Follow the derivation
  1. One joule of electricity produces \(\eta_{\text{ph}}\) µmol of photons; a fraction \(\eta_{\text{cap}}\) reaches the canopy.
  2. To deliver 1 mol = 10⁶ µmol to the canopy you need \(10^6/(\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}})\) joules.
  3. Divide by 3.6 × 10⁶ J kWh⁻¹ to convert to kilowatt-hours: \(0.2778/(\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}})\).
  4. Example: \(\eta_{\text{ph}} = 2.8\) µmol J⁻¹ and \(\eta_{\text{cap}} = 0.8\) give 0.124 kWh mol⁻¹. A lettuce crop at DLI 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ then uses 2.1 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ for light alone.

Model assumptions

  • Each bar fixture is represented by five equal point sources along its length, each with a cosine-power intensity distribution \(I_0\cos^n\theta\).
  • Walls are placed at the edges of the bench and extend high enough to intercept all escaping rays. Reflection is specular, with first-order images in each wall and second-order images in each corner.
  • The canopy is a horizontal plane at the top of the plants. Leaf reflection, transmission, canopy depth and mutual shading are neglected.
  • Photon efficacy and PPF refer to 400–700 nm. Far-red photons (700–750 nm), which also drive photosynthesis when combined with shorter wavelengths, are not counted.
  • All electrical power becomes heat in the room eventually; the cooling energy needed to remove it is not included in the cost shown.
  • The 3D lettuce and fixture models are illustrative; the PPFD map is the physically computed quantity.
