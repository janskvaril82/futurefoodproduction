NFT channel simulator

A greenhouse nutrient-film-technique (NFT) installation in which every drop of solution is accounted for. A pump lifts solution from an aerated reservoir to a manifold; it runs down sloping gullies as a film only millimetres deep, past a row of lettuces whose roots take up oxygen, nitrate, potassium and water; sun and air warm it on the way; and a gutter returns it to the reservoir. Change the slope, flow, gully length, spacing and climate, grow the crop from transplanting to harvest, and watch the plants at the far end of the gully respond to what the plants upstream have left them.

What to look at first

  1. Follow the water. Press Inlet and Outlet under Display. Blue particles show the solution leaving the submersible pump, climbing the riser to the feed manifold, dropping through one small tube (with its own valve) into each gully, running down the gully as a film, spilling into the collector gutter and falling back into the reservoir, where an air stone re-aerates it. The particles in the gully move at the real surface velocity of the film.
  2. Look inside a gully. Press Cut-away. The front gully has its front wall removed: the film on the black floor is coloured by dissolved oxygen, and the root mats of the lettuces lie in it, spreading downstream. Roots turn from cream to brown where the solution is short of oxygen or too warm. Click anywhere on a gully to insert a virtual probe that reads the local oxygen, temperature, nitrate, potassium, film thickness and plant growth factor.
  3. Grow the crop. Press ▶ Grow the crop. For the first two weeks the plants are small, use little oxygen and the film is almost uniformly green. In the last ten days the heads grow by 10–13 g a day, their roots respire hard, and oxygen falls along the gully — the colour gradient appears.
  4. Choose the Low flow preset. The film becomes thinner and slower, the same oxygen demand is shared by less water, and the far half of the gully drops below 2 mg L⁻¹. The plants there stay small and pale and the mass chart shows a steep fall from inlet to outlet — while nitrate and potassium hardly change.
  5. Choose Hot summer. Now the limiting factor is temperature: the solution enters at 26 °C and warms further along the gully, and the whole crop is smaller. Switch the film colouring to Temperature and to Growth factor f.
  6. Switch to Film map. The lids and plants disappear and all gullies show their film colour from above — try the Unbalanced manifold preset to see why growers measure the flow of every gully with a jug and a stopwatch.
Key idea

An NFT gully is a plug-flow reactor. Solution enters with a certain oxygen, nutrient and heat content; each plant it passes changes that content a little; the last plant receives what is left. Whether the last plant is as good as the first depends on a race between supply (flow × concentration, plus oxygen dissolving through the film surface) and demand (plants per metre × uptake per plant). Oxygen and temperature, not nutrients, usually lose that race first.

A film a millimetre deep

The nutrient film technique, developed by Allen Cooper at the Glasshouse Crops Research Institute in the late 1960s and 1970s (Cooper, 1979; Graves, 1983), grows plants with their roots lying in a shallow, sloping gully through which a thin film of solution flows continuously. Because the film is thin, the upper part of the root mat is in humid air and oxygen has only a short distance to diffuse. The film thickness is set by a balance of two forces: gravity pulls the solution down the slope, and viscous friction against the gully floor holds it back. For a smooth laminar film this balance gives Nusselt's classic result (Eq. N1; Nusselt, 1916; Bird et al., 2002), derived step by step in Lesson 5.4.

Why the film is so thin — and why that matters

Eq. N1 says that the thickness grows only with the cube root of the flow: doubling the flow deepens the film by 26 % and speeds it up by 59 %. For the default design (1.5 L min⁻¹ in a 10 cm gully at 1:40, 22 °C) the film is 1.43 mm deep and flows at a mean 0.17 m s⁻¹, so a parcel of solution crosses a 10 m gully in about a minute. The film Reynolds number is about 1000: laminar, but rippled. Commercial guidance of about 1 L min⁻¹ per gully (no more than about 2) and slopes of 1:30 to 1:100 (Graves, 1983) keeps the film in the 1–3 mm range where roots are both wet and aerated. The film chart shows the trade-off: a steeper slope gives a thinner, faster film.

Oxygen: the substance that runs out first

A lettuce close to harvest adds about 0.6 g of dry matter a day; its roots respire about 7 mg O₂ h⁻¹ at 20 °C, and 62 % more at 27 °C (Lesson 5.3). At 20 cm spacing a gully carries five such plants per metre, i.e. about 40 mg O₂ h⁻¹ per metre. One litre per minute of solution at 8 mg L⁻¹ brings in only 480 mg O₂ h⁻¹ — enough for 12 m of gully if nothing else happened. What else happens is re-aeration: as the concentration falls below saturation, oxygen dissolves back through the film surface at a rate \(K_LW(C^*-C)\) per metre (Eq. N4). The two effects balance at an equilibrium concentration \(C_{eq}=C^*-r'/(K_LW)\) that the solution approaches exponentially over a relaxation length \(\lambda=Q/(K_LW)\) of several metres. Long gullies therefore do not run out of oxygen completely, but their far end settles at a concentration set only by demand and re-aeration — about 3–4 mg L⁻¹ for a mature lettuce crop at 1 L min⁻¹ in this model, below the 4 mg L⁻¹ that the Cornell lettuce handbook gives as the level above which lettuce grows satisfactorily (Brechner & Both, 2013).

Measurements confirm that such gradients are real. In NFT channels with cucumbers, Gislerød and Kempton (1983) found dissolved oxygen falling from 6.2 mg L⁻¹ near the inlet to 2.9 mg L⁻¹ at the last plant, and with tomatoes from 8.3 to 7.5 mg L⁻¹; in a tomato NFT system studied by Suhl et al. (2019), oxygen fell every day as radiation and solution temperature rose, and consumption during the day was up to 2.8 times that at night. These observations explain the practical rule that growth is depressed in gullies longer than about 12 m (Jones, 2014) and the classic remedies: more flow, shorter gullies, a second feed point part-way along, cooler solution and a well-aerated reservoir.

Common misconception

"The far plants are small because the nutrients are used up." Run the Low flow preset and read the outlet nitrate: at 0.25 L min⁻¹ the sixty plants of a 12 m gully take up about 60 mg of nitrogen an hour from 15 L of solution — a change of a few mg L⁻¹ out of 125. Roots absorb these ions with high-affinity transporters whose half-saturation concentration is about 18 µmol L⁻¹ for K⁺ (Epstein et al., 1963) and that saturate below 1 mmol L⁻¹ for NO₃⁻ (Siddiqi et al., 1990) — far below the 8.9 mmol N L⁻¹ and 5.5 mmol K L⁻¹ of a lettuce solution — so the uptake rate does not fall until the solution is almost empty. Oxygen, which dissolves only to about 9 mg L⁻¹, is exhausted long before.

Heat: a thin film has no thermal inertia

A millimetre-thick film holds almost no heat, so its temperature follows its surroundings: it exchanges heat with the air through the gully walls and absorbs sunlight falling on the gully (Eq. N8). Once the heads cover the gully they shade it, which is why young crops in bright greenhouses are the most exposed. Warm solution is doubly harmful: it holds less oxygen (8.7 mg L⁻¹ at 22 °C but 7.8 at 28 °C) while the roots respire faster (Q₁₀ ≈ 2), and above about 25 °C lettuce growth itself declines — Thompson et al. (1998) obtained the largest lettuce dry mass at a pond temperature of 24 °C and much less at 31 °C, and the Cornell system cools its solution above 26 °C (Brechner & Both, 2013).

How the simulator runs the crop

The solution crosses a gully in seconds to minutes, whereas the plants change over days. The simulator therefore solves the steady along-gully balances once per simulated day with that day's plant sizes, lets every plant grow for one day at a rate reduced by the conditions at its own position, and repeats for 24 days after transplanting. The displayed profiles are daytime conditions held constant, so a preset such as Hot summer represents a sustained heat wave rather than a single hot afternoon.

The equations behind the simulation

The model has nine equations. N1–N3 describe the film, N4–N8 are steady-state balances along the gully (distance \(x\) from the inlet) for oxygen, nutrients, water and heat, and N9 is the growth of each plant. The balances are integrated numerically (second-order Runge–Kutta, four steps per plant spacing); the boxed analytical solutions are exact when the coefficients are constant and are useful for checking.

Laminar falling film on a slope (Nusselt)(N1)
\[ \begin{gathered} h = \left(\frac{3\,\mu\,q}{\rho\,g\,\sin\theta}\right)^{1/3},\qquad q=\frac{Q}{W} \\[6pt] \bar u=\frac{q}{h},\qquad u_s=\tfrac32\,\bar u,\qquad Re_f=\frac{4q}{\nu} \end{gathered} \]

the film thickness is the cube root of three times the viscosity times the flow per unit width, divided by density times gravity times the sine of the slope angle; the mean velocity is the flow per width divided by the thickness, the surface velocity is one and a half times the mean, and the film Reynolds number is four times the flow per width divided by the kinematic viscosity.

Follow the derivation
  1. Take \(x\) down the slope and \(y\) perpendicular to the floor. For steady, fully developed laminar flow, the weight component of the fluid between \(y\) and the free surface, \(\rho g\sin\theta\,(h-y)\), is balanced by the shear stress below it: \(\tau(y)=\rho g\sin\theta\,(h-y)\).
  2. With Newton's law of viscosity, \(\tau=\mu\,\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}y\), and no slip at the floor, integration gives the half-parabola \(u(y)=(\rho g\sin\theta/\mu)(hy-y^2/2)\).
  3. The flow per unit width is \(q=\int_0^h u\,\mathrm{d}y=\rho g\sin\theta\,h^3/(3\mu)\); inverting gives \(h\). The surface value of the parabola is \(1.5\,\bar u\).
  4. The full derivation, with the flow regimes of films, is in Lesson 5.4, Eq. 5.4.2. The simulator evaluates \(\mu(T)\) with the same Vogel-type fit as the lesson.
Assumptions and validity

A flat, clean floor much wider than the film. A mature root mat acts as a porous dam: solution then flows through and under it, deeper and slower than Eq. N1 predicts. At very small flows surface tension breaks the film into rivulets that leave parts of the floor dry. The regime is smooth laminar for \(Re_f\) below about 20 and laminar with ripples up to about 1500 (Bird et al., 2002).

\[ t_{\text{res}}=\frac{L}{\bar u}=\frac{L\,W\,h}{Q} \]

the time a parcel of solution spends in the gully is the gully length divided by the mean film velocity — equivalently, the volume of the film divided by the flow.

Oxygen transfer through the film surface (penetration theory)(N3)
\[ K_L = 2\sqrt{\frac{D_{O_2}\,u_s}{\pi\,\ell}},\qquad k_La=\frac{K_L\,W\,\mathrm{d}x}{W\,h\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{K_L}{h} \]

the liquid-side transfer coefficient equals twice the square root of the oxygen diffusivity times the surface velocity divided by pi times the distance over which the surface is renewed; per unit volume of film, the volumetric coefficient is that coefficient divided by the film thickness.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(K_L\)liquid-film mass-transfer coefficientm s⁻¹≈ 2.7 × 10⁻⁵
\(D_{O_2}\)diffusivity of O₂ in water, \(2.0\times10^{-9}\) at 20 °C, scaled with \(T/\mu\)m² s⁻¹2.1 × 10⁻⁹
\(\ell\)surface-renewal length (distance between ripples, plants, inlets that mix the surface)m1.0
\(k_La\)volumetric transfer coefficient of the films⁻¹≈ 0.019
Follow the derivation
  1. Higbie (1935) pictured a liquid surface element that is exposed to the gas for a time \(t_e\) and then replaced. During the exposure O₂ diffuses into it as into a semi-infinite liquid, and the mean flux over the exposure is \(2\sqrt{D/(\pi t_e)}\,(C^*-C)\).
  2. A surface element moving at \(u_s\) is renewed after a distance \(\ell\), so \(t_e=\ell/u_s\) and \(K_L=2\sqrt{Du_s/(\pi\ell)}\).
  3. With \(u_s\) = 0.18 m s⁻¹ and \(\ell\) = 1 m, \(K_L\approx2\times10^{-5}\) m s⁻¹; ripples every 5 cm raise it to about \(10^{-4}\) m s⁻¹ — the order-of-magnitude range given in Lesson 5.4.
  4. The interfacial area per unit film volume is \(W\,\mathrm{d}x/(Wh\,\mathrm{d}x)=1/h\), hence \(k_La=K_L/h\): a thinner film is re-aerated faster per litre.
Assumptions and validity

\(\ell\) is the least certain parameter of the model: ripples, falling water at the inlet and gaps between plants shorten it; a root mat that covers the film surface effectively lengthens it. Oxygen reaching the upper root mat directly from the humid air — the main route in mature NFT — is not modelled, so absolute oxygen levels are conservative. Use the slider to test the sensitivity.

\(\sqrt{\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}\cdot\text{m s}^{-1}/\text{m}}=\sqrt{\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2}}=\text{m s}^{-1}\) ✓
Dissolved-oxygen balance along the gully(N4)
\[ \begin{gathered} Q\,\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}x}=K_L\,W\,\big(C^{*}(T)-C\big)-\frac{R_{O_2}}{s} \\[6pt] C(x)=C_{eq}+\big(C_{in}-C_{eq}\big)\,e^{-x/\lambda},\qquad \lambda=\frac{Q}{K_LW},\qquad C_{eq}=C^*-\frac{r'}{K_LW} \end{gathered} \]

the change of the oxygen carried by the flow per metre of gully equals oxygen dissolving through the film surface minus oxygen taken up by the roots per metre (uptake per plant divided by the plant spacing). With constant coefficients the concentration relaxes exponentially from the inlet value towards an equilibrium below saturation.

Follow the derivation
  1. Control volume: the film between \(x\) and \(x+\mathrm{d}x\). In steady state, O₂ in with the flow + O₂ dissolving through the surface = O₂ out with the flow + O₂ consumed: \(QC(x)+K_LW\,\mathrm{d}x\,(C^*-C)=QC(x+\mathrm{d}x)+r'\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  2. Divide by \(\mathrm{d}x\): \(Q\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}x=K_LW(C^*-C)-r'\).
  3. Set \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}x=0\) to find the equilibrium \(C_{eq}\); with \(y=C-C_{eq}\) the equation becomes \(\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}x=-y/\lambda\), an exponential.
  4. Without re-aeration (\(K_L\to0\)) it reduces to the linear whole-gully balance \(C(L)=C_{in}-r'L/Q\) of Lesson 5.4, Eq. 5.4.1.
  5. In the simulator \(T\), \(C^*\), \(K_L\), \(Q\) and \(R_{O_2}\) all change along the gully, so the equation is integrated numerically.
Assumptions and validity

Plug flow (no mixing along the gully), uptake spread uniformly over each plant's spacing, and oxygen consumption by microorganisms in the solution and biofilms neglected — Suhl et al. (2019) show that it can matter in solutions made with fish-farm water.

Worked example 1Design check of the textbook gully

A 10 m gully with a 10 cm floor at a slope of 1:40 carries 1.5 L min⁻¹ of solution at 22 °C (\(\mu\) = 0.955 × 10⁻³ Pa s, \(\rho\) = 998 kg m⁻³, \(C^*\) = 8.74 mg L⁻¹, \(D_{O_2}\) = 2.11 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹) that enters at 8.0 mg O₂ L⁻¹. Near harvest, heads of 150 g at 20 cm spacing each respire 8.2 mg O₂ h⁻¹ (Eq. N5 at 22 °C). Assume the film surface is renewed every metre. Estimate the film thickness, the residence time, \(K_L\), the relaxation length, the equilibrium DO and the DO reaching the last plant.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Flow per width and slope: \(q = 1.5/60\,000/0.10 = 2.50\times10^{-4}\) m² s⁻¹; \(\sin\theta = 0.025/\sqrt{1+0.025^2} = 0.0250\).
  2. Film (N1): \(h = \big(3\times0.955\times10^{-3}\times2.50\times10^{-4}/(998\times9.81\times0.0250)\big)^{1/3} = (2.93\times10^{-9})^{1/3} = 1.43\times10^{-3}\) m; \(\bar u = q/h = 0.175\) m s⁻¹; \(u_s = 1.5\,\bar u = 0.262\) m s⁻¹.
  3. Residence time (N2): \(t_{\text{res}} = 10/0.175 = 57\) s.
  4. Surface transfer (N3): \(K_L = 2\sqrt{2.11\times10^{-9}\times0.262/(\pi\times1)} = 2.66\times10^{-5}\) m s⁻¹, so \(K_LW = 2.66\times10^{-6}\) m² s⁻¹.
  5. Uptake per metre: five plants per metre × 8.22 mg h⁻¹ = 41.1 mg h⁻¹ m⁻¹ = \(1.14\times10^{-5}\) g s⁻¹ m⁻¹.
  6. Oxygen profile (N4): \(\lambda = Q/(K_LW) = 2.50\times10^{-5}/2.66\times10^{-6} = 9.4\) m; \(C_{eq} = 8.74 - 1.14\times10^{-5}/2.66\times10^{-6} = 8.74 - 4.30 = 4.44\) mg L⁻¹; \(C(10) = 4.44 + (8.0-4.44)\,e^{-10/9.4} = 4.44 + 3.56\times0.345 = 5.67\) mg L⁻¹.
  7. Check with the simulator. Apply Textbook design at day 24: the outlet reads 5.7 mg L⁻¹. The small difference comes from the 0.6 °C warming along the gully, which the simulator includes.

Worked example 2Why the low-flow gully fails

The same gully is lengthened to 12 m and the flow is cut to 0.25 L min⁻¹ (the Low flow preset). Repeat the estimate of Worked example 1. Why does the simulator show 149 g heads at the inlet but only 93 g at the outlet?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Film: the flow is 6 times smaller, so \(h = 1.43\times6^{-1/3} = 0.79\) mm and \(\bar u = 0.175\times6^{-2/3} = 0.053\) m s⁻¹; \(t_{\text{res}} = 12/0.053 = 227\) s ≈ 3.8 min.
  2. Surface transfer: \(u_s = 0.079\) m s⁻¹, so \(K_L = 2.66\times10^{-5}\sqrt{0.079/0.262} = 1.46\times10^{-5}\) m s⁻¹. Note that \(k_La = K_L/h\) = 0.019 s⁻¹ is exactly the same as at 1.5 L min⁻¹: at a fixed slope both \(K_L\) and \(h\) scale with \(Q^{1/3}\), so each litre of film is re-aerated just as fast.
  3. Oxygen profile: \(\lambda = 4.17\times10^{-6}/1.46\times10^{-6} = 2.9\) m and \(C_{eq} = 8.74 - 1.14\times10^{-5}/1.46\times10^{-6} = 0.9\) mg L⁻¹. After 12 m (≈ 4λ) the solution has reached \(C_{eq}\): the far half of the gully is almost anoxic.
  4. What the simulator adds: respiration slows at such low DO (factor \(C/(K_O+C)\) in N5), so the outlet settles at 1.4 mg L⁻¹ rather than 0.9. The plants beyond ≈ 4 m spend the last week with \(f_{O_2}\) falling to between 0.7 and 0.36, and reach only 130 g down to 93 g (N9).
  5. Interpretation. The failure is caused by the flow: six times less water carries six times less oxygen past the same demand, while re-aeration per litre is unchanged. Nitrate rises slightly (to 128.5 mg L⁻¹), because the plants remove water faster than nitrogen.

  2. Expanding the product, \(Q\,\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}x+N\,\mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}x=-U_N/s\), which gives the boxed form. If the plants take up water in a larger proportion than nitrogen, the second term makes the solution slightly more concentrated downstream.
  3. Uptake follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics (Lesson 2.3): the maximum \(U_{N,\max}\) is the demand of a plant growing at the rate allowed by temperature; the transporter saturates when \(N\gg K_m\).
  4. At 125 mg N L⁻¹, \(N/(K_m+N)=0.997\): uptake is independent of concentration unless the solution is almost exhausted — the reason nutrients rarely limit gully length.
Assumptions and validity

Whole-plant kinetics with the constants of the high-affinity systems of barley roots; the low-affinity systems that operate at millimolar concentrations would only add capacity. Uptake of ions other than nitrate and potassium, pH changes and ion interactions are not modelled (see Lesson 5.2).

Heat balance of the flowing film(N8)
\[ \begin{gathered} \rho\,c_p\,Q\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}x}=U\,P\,(T_{air}-T)+\alpha_s\,G_{sun}\,W\,(1-0.8\,c) \\[6pt] T(x)=T_{eq}+(T_{in}-T_{eq})\,e^{-x/\lambda_T},\qquad \lambda_T=\frac{\rho\,c_p\,Q}{U\,P} \end{gathered} \]

Worked example 3A hot afternoon in the gully

In the Hot summer preset, solution enters at 26 °C, the air is 33 °C and 700 W m⁻² falls on the gullies; the flow is 1.5 L min⁻¹ in 10 m gullies. With \(U\) = 8 W m⁻² K⁻¹, \(P\) = 0.30 m, \(\alpha_s\) = 0.3 and a 0.108 m wide gully top, estimate the outlet temperature (a) just after transplanting, when the heads cover hardly any of the gully (\(c\) ≈ 0), and (b) near harvest (\(c\) = 1). What does it mean for the last plant?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Thermal length: \(\rho c_pQ = 998\times4186\times2.5\times10^{-5} = 104\) W K⁻¹; \(UP = 8\times0.30 = 2.4\) W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; \(\lambda_T = 104/2.4 = 43.5\) m — much longer than the gully, so the solution only starts to approach its equilibrium.
  2. (a) Bare gully: absorbed sunlight \(0.3\times700\times0.108 = 22.7\) W m⁻¹; \(T_{eq} = 33 + 22.7/2.4 = 42.5\) °C; \(T(10) = 42.5 + (26-42.5)\,e^{-10/43.5} = 42.5 - 16.5\times0.795 = 29.4\) °C. (The simulator gives 28.9 °C on day 0 because even the small seedlings shade part of the gully.)
  3. (b) Covered gully: only 20 % of the gain remains, 4.5 W m⁻¹; \(T_{eq} = 33 + 1.9 = 34.9\) °C; \(T(10) = 34.9 - 8.9\times0.795 = 27.8\) °C — exactly the simulator's outlet at day 24.
  4. Consequences: at 27.8 °C the temperature factor is \(f_T\) = 0.83, the saturation concentration falls to 7.8 mg L⁻¹ and respiration rises by \(2^{0.78}\) = 1.7 relative to 20 °C. The simulator's heads reach 126 g at the inlet and 82 g at the outlet.
  5. Design lesson. A thin film has almost no thermal inertia: more flow only lengthens \(\lambda_T\) in proportion to \(Q\). Cooling the reservoir, white or insulated gullies (smaller \(\alpha_s\) and \(U\)) and shading young crops attack the cause.

Growth of each plant from the conditions at its position(N9)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}=r\,f\,m\left(1-\frac{m}{K}\right) \\[6pt] f=f_T(T)\cdot\min\!\Big(f_{O_2}(C),\ \frac{N}{K_{m,N}+N},\ \frac{K}{K_{m,K}+K}\Big) \end{gathered} \]

each head grows logistically at a relative rate reduced by a stress factor: the product of a temperature response and the most limiting of the oxygen, nitrate and potassium responses at the plant's own position.

Follow the derivation
  1. Logistic growth: the relative growth rate \(\frac{1}{m}\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}\) starts at \(r\) and declines as the head approaches its asymptote.
  2. For constant \(f\) over a day the exact solution is \(m(t+1)=K/\big[1+(K/m(t)-1)\,e^{-rf}\big]\), which the simulator uses day by day.
  3. Because growth compounds, a modest reduction of \(f\) has a large effect on the final mass: with \(f\) = 0.9 for 24 days the head reaches 118 g instead of 150 g; with \(f\) = 0.8, 87 g.
  4. The stresses are combined with Liebig's law of the minimum for the root-zone supplies and multiplicatively for temperature, a common structure in crop models.
Assumptions and validity

The response functions are illustrative shapes anchored on published thresholds, not fitted to a data set; the relative ranking of scenarios is more reliable than the exact masses. Light, CO₂, air humidity and tipburn are assumed identical for all plants.

Model assumptions

  • Hydraulics. Laminar Nusselt film on a flat, clean floor of the stated width; the thickening and slowing of the flow by a mature root mat, rivulet formation at very low flows and local ponding in sagging gullies are not modelled. Flow per gully is set directly (the pump and manifold are not solved); the imbalance control distributes a given mean flow linearly between gullies.
  • Plug flow and quasi-steady state. No mixing along the gully; the balances are solved at steady state once per simulated day with that day's plant sizes, and the daytime climate is held constant over the crop cycle.
  • Oxygen. Transfer only through the film surface by penetration theory with a single surface-renewal length \(\ell\); direct uptake of oxygen from the humid air by the upper root mat and consumption by microorganisms are neglected. Saturation follows Benson & Krause (1984) for fresh water at sea-level pressure (the small salting-out effect of a nutrient solution is ignored). The inlet DO is a control: re-aeration in the reservoir is not modelled.
  • Plants. Every head starts at 2 g and follows a logistic curve calibrated to the Cornell schedule (150 g at 24 days after transplanting); root respiration is linked to growth (Lesson 5.3) with Q₁₀ = 2. The temperature and oxygen response curves are illustrative functions anchored on published thresholds, not fitted models. Light, CO₂ and air humidity are uniform, and tipburn and disease are ignored — even though warm, oxygen-poor solution favours Pythium in reality.
  • Nutrients. Only nitrate-N and potassium are tracked, with whole-plant Michaelis–Menten kinetics using high-affinity constants measured in barley; the reservoir concentrations are assumed to be kept constant by the grower.
  • Heat. One overall coefficient (U = 8 W m⁻² K⁻¹) couples the solution to the air, 30 % of the sunlight falling on the (white) gully heats the solution, and the canopy shades up to 80 % of it. Evaporation from the film and pump heat are neglected.
  • 3D scene. Plant sizes, root colour, film thickness, flow speeds and the film colour map are driven by the model; the greenhouse, stands, pump and plumbing are illustrative hardware at realistic scale.
