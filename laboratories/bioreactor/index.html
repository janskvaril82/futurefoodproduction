Stirred-tank bioreactor

Precision fermentation turns sugar into a food protein inside a steel tank full of yeast. Run a Komagataella phaffii (Pichia) culture that secretes a recombinant protein — in batch, fed-batch or continuous mode — and keep it alive the way a process engineer does: supply enough oxygen, remove enough heat and feed at the right rate. Then scale the same recipe from a 2 L glass fermenter to a 100 m³ production vessel and discover which physical limit bites first.

A guided tour

  1. Press ▶ Run with the default 2 L fed-batch. For the first 20 h the culture grows exponentially on the 20 g L⁻¹ of glucose in the medium. Watch the broth turn from clear straw-yellow to an opaque, creamy suspension: at 30 g L⁻¹ of yeast you can no longer see the impellers.
  2. Watch the stirrer. The dissolved-oxygen (DO) controller speeds up the motor as the cells breathe harder, keeping DO at its 30 % set-point. The bubbles become finer and more numerous, and the magenta OUR curve climbs towards the dashed transfer ceiling.
  3. The feed starts as soon as the batch sugar is gone: the feed pump turns, the feed bottle empties and the level in the vessel rises. When the stirrer reaches top speed, the cascade switches to its second stage — O₂-enriched air. Near the end the feed protection throttles the pump because even enriched air is not enough.
  4. Open the Probes and Impellers views (buttons in the control panel). Click any component — probe, sparger, jacket, off-gas analyser — to read what it measures, live.
  5. Now choose 100 m³ (or the preset 100 m³ fed-batch) and run the same recipe. The vessel is 4 m wide and 10 m tall; a person on the platform gives the scale. This time the magenta total heat load curve hits the dashed blue cooling capacity long before oxygen runs short: the feed is throttled and the run takes far longer.
  6. Try the continuous mode at D = 0.10 h⁻¹ and then at 0.22 h⁻¹. At the higher dilution rate the cells cannot divide as fast as they are washed out, and the biomass curve slides towards zero.
Key idea

A fermenter is a mass-transfer and heat-transfer machine that happens to contain living cells. The cells would happily grow at their maximum rate; what limits a real process is how fast the equipment can deliver oxygen, remove heat and mix — and all three get harder as the vessel gets bigger.

What happens inside a fermenter

An aerobic fermentation is a controlled combustion. The yeast oxidises glucose with oxygen to gain energy, uses part of the carbon to build new cells and — because it carries a gene for our product — secretes a protein. Every mole of oxygen consumed releases about 460 kJ of heat, and every watt of stirrer power also ends up as heat in the broth. The bioreactor has to supply glucose and oxygen, remove carbon dioxide and heat, and mix everything so that each cell sees the same conditions.

Growth, maintenance and the product

Cells take up glucose at a specific rate \(q_S\) that saturates at high sugar concentration (the Monod form). Most of that sugar is turned into new cells with the true yield \(Y_{X/S}\), but a small, constant slice — the maintenance \(m_S\) — is burned just to keep existing cells alive (Pirt, 1965). The consequence matters in fed-batch and continuous culture: the slower the cells grow, the larger the fraction of sugar lost to maintenance. The secreted product follows the Luedeking–Piret law: part is made in proportion to growth (\(\alpha\mu\)) and part at a constant background rate (\(\beta\)). For the Fab antibody fragment studied by Maurer et al. (2006), production is strongly growth-associated — which is why the protein titre rises only while the cells grow.

Oxygen: the first bottleneck

Oxygen dissolves poorly in water: air-saturated broth at 25 °C holds only about 8 mg L⁻¹, i.e. 0.26 mmol L⁻¹. A dense culture can consume that in a few seconds, so oxygen must be transferred continuously from bubbles into the liquid. The rate is \(\text{OTR} = k_La\,(C^{*} - C)\): a mass-transfer coefficient times a driving force. The engineer can raise \(k_La\) with more stirrer power and more gas (Eq. B10), or raise the driving force with a higher head pressure or O₂-enriched air (Eq. B8). The DO controller does this automatically in a cascade: stirrer speed first, O₂ enrichment second (Figure 2).

Common misconception

"If DO is at the set-point, oxygen is not limiting." The DO reading only tells you that the controller is winning for now. The real margin is the gap between OUR and the transfer ceiling (dashed line in the oxygen chart). A process running at 95 % of its ceiling will crash the moment the feed rises a little.

Heat: the second bottleneck

The metabolic heat is proportional to the oxygen uptake: \(\dot Q = 460\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\times\text{OUR}\times V\) (Doran, 2013; Cooney et al., 1969). An OUR of 100 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ releases 12.8 kW in every cubic metre — the output of a large domestic boiler per m³ of broth. The jacket can remove \(U A (T - T_{cw})\), and here geometry is merciless: for geometrically similar vessels with \(H = 2T\) the wall area per volume is \(A/V = 4/T\). A 2 L fermenter has 37 m² of wall per m³ of broth; a 100 m³ vessel has 1 m² m⁻³. Heat removal per volume therefore falls about 37-fold on scale-up, while the oxygen transfer per volume can be kept roughly constant by keeping the power per volume constant.

Scaling up at constant power per volume

The most common scale-up rule for microbial fermentations is to keep the power input per volume \(P/V\) constant, because \(k_La\) depends mainly on \(P/V\) and the gas velocity (Eq. B10). Why does the O₂ ceiling rise with scale?

At constant P/V, a tall vessel helps oxygen transfer in two ways: the hydrostatic pressure at mid-depth raises the solubility \(C^{*}\) (Henry's law), and the same vvm pushes gas faster through the liquid (higher \(v_s\)). Large vessels also run with head-space overpressure. What they cannot escape is the shrinking wall area per volume — and the long mixing times, which create sugar and oxygen gradients that this well-mixed model cannot show.

Worked example 1Oxygen and heat at 100 g L⁻¹

A K. phaffii fed-batch (\(Y_{X/S} = 0.559\) g g⁻¹, \(m_S = 0.0161\) g g⁻¹ h⁻¹) grows at \(\mu = 0.10\) h⁻¹ at \(X = 100\) g L⁻¹ and 25 °C. (a) What is the oxygen uptake rate? (b) What \(k_La\) is needed with air and with 50 % O₂, if DO is held at 30 % of air saturation (\(C^{*}_{\text{air}} = 0.258\) mmol L⁻¹)? (c) What is the heat load per m³, and can a jacket remove it in a 2 L and in a 100 m³ vessel with \(H = 2T\), \(U = 500\) W m⁻² K⁻¹ and ΔT = 10 K?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Sugar uptake (Eq. B1). \(q_S = \mu/Y_{X/S} + m_S = 0.10/0.559 + 0.0161 = 0.195\) g g⁻¹ h⁻¹.
  2. Oxygen by electron balance (Eq. B6). \(q_{O_2} = \tfrac14\left(4\times0.195/30.03 - 4.2\times0.10/24.6\right) = \tfrac14(0.02597 - 0.01707) = 2.23\times10^{-3}\) mol g⁻¹ h⁻¹. OUR \(= q_{O_2}X = 0.223\) mol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ = 223 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹.
  3. Required \(k_La\) (Eq. B7). With air: \(k_La = 223/(0.258 - 0.3\times0.258) = 223/0.181 = 1230\) h⁻¹ — far above what stirred tanks reach. With 50 % O₂: \(C^{*} = 0.258\times0.50/0.2095 = 0.616\) mmol L⁻¹, driving force \(0.616 - 0.077 = 0.539\), so \(k_La = 414\) h⁻¹ — achievable.
  4. Heat (Eq. B11). \(223\ \text{mol m}^{-3}\text{h}^{-1}\times460\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}/3600 = 28.5\) kW m⁻³ (plus stirrer power).
  5. Jacket capacity per volume (Eq. B12). \(A/V = 4/T\). At 2 L (\(T = 0.108\) m): \(37\ \text{m}^2\text{m}^{-3}\times500\times10 = 185\) kW m⁻³ — plenty. At 100 m³ (\(T = 3.99\) m): \(1.0\times500\times10 = 5.0\) kW m⁻³ — less than a fifth of the load.
OUR ≈ 223 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹; k_La ≈ 1230 h⁻¹ (air) or 414 h⁻¹ (50 % O₂); heat ≈ 28.5 kW m⁻³ — easy at 2 L, impossible with a jacket alone at 100 m³.

Sanity check: the lesson's rule of thumb, 12.8 kW m⁻³ per 100 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹, gives 28.5 kW m⁻³ for 223 ✓. Try the preset 100 m³ with coils + chilled water to see how much extra surface is needed.

Worked example 2Maintenance lowers the yield of a chemostat

The same yeast (\(\mu_{\max} = 0.20\) h⁻¹, \(K_S = 0.05\) g L⁻¹) grows in a chemostat on \(S_R = 20\) g L⁻¹ glucose at \(D = 0.10\) h⁻¹. Find the steady-state sugar, biomass, productivity and the observed yield. Then find the washout dilution rate.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. At steady state \(\mu = D\), so \(q_S = D/Y_{X/S} + m_S = 0.10/0.559 + 0.0161 = 0.195\) g g⁻¹ h⁻¹, and \(q_{S,\max} = 0.20/0.559 + 0.0161 = 0.374\) g g⁻¹ h⁻¹.
  2. Sugar (Eq. B5): \(\bar S = K_S q_S/(q_{S,\max} - q_S) = 0.05\times0.195/0.179 = 0.054\) g L⁻¹.
  3. Biomass: \(\bar X = D(S_R - \bar S)/q_S = 0.10\times19.95/0.195 = 10.2\) g L⁻¹; productivity \(D\bar X = 1.02\) g L⁻¹ h⁻¹.
  4. Observed yield: \(\bar X/(S_R - \bar S) = 10.2/19.95 = 0.513\) g g⁻¹ — 8 % below the true yield, because 8 % of the sugar is burned for maintenance.
  5. Washout: \(D_{\text{crit}} = Y_{X/S}\left(q_{S,\max}S_R/(K_S + S_R) - m_S\right) = 0.559\times(0.373 - 0.0161) = 0.200\) h⁻¹.
S̄ = 0.054 g L⁻¹, X̄ = 10.2 g L⁻¹, D·X̄ = 1.02 g L⁻¹ h⁻¹, Y_obs = 0.51 g g⁻¹, D_crit = 0.200 h⁻¹.

The simulator gives X̄ ≈ 10.3 g L⁻¹ and S̄ ≈ 0.07 g L⁻¹ — slightly different because the cells also see a small oxygen limitation at DO = 30 % (the \(C/(K_O + C)\) term in Eq. B1).

The equations behind the simulation

The simulator integrates six state variables — biomass \(X\), glucose \(S\), product \(P\), dissolved oxygen \(C\), volume \(V\) and temperature \(T\) — with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method and a 5 s time step, while the controllers act between steps. Units: concentrations in g L⁻¹ (= kg m⁻³), dissolved O₂ in mmol L⁻¹ (= mol m⁻³), time in hours.

Monod uptake with maintenance (Herbert–Pirt)(B1)
\[ \begin{aligned} q_S &= q_{S,\max}\,\frac{S}{K_S+S}\,\frac{C}{K_O+C}, \qquad q_{S,\max} = \frac{\mu_{\max}}{Y_{X/S}} + m_S \\ \mu &= Y_{X/S}\left(q_S - m_S\,\frac{C}{K_O+C}\right) \end{aligned} \]

the cells take up sugar at a rate that saturates with sugar and with dissolved oxygen; what is left after paying the maintenance "tax" is turned into new cells with the true yield.

\(q_S\)specific glucose uptake rateg g⁻¹ h⁻¹—
\(\mu\)specific growth rate (negative = endogenous decay)h⁻¹—
\(\mu_{\max}\)maximum specific growth rateh⁻¹0.20 (Maurer et al., 2006)
\(Y_{X/S}\)true biomass yield on glucoseg g⁻¹0.559 (Maurer et al., 2006)
\(m_S\)maintenance coefficientg g⁻¹ h⁻¹0.0161 (Maurer et al., 2006)
\(K_S\)half-saturation constant for glucoseg L⁻¹0.05 (assumed; sugars are typically mg L⁻¹ to 0.1 g L⁻¹)
\(K_O\)half-saturation constant for dissolved O₂mmol L⁻¹0.01 (≈ 4 % of air saturation, assumed)
Follow the derivation
  1. Monod (1949) observed that growth saturates with the limiting substrate: \(\mu = \mu_{\max}S/(K_S+S)\). Writing the same law for the uptake rate is more mechanistic: transporters saturate, \(q_S = q_{S,\max}S/(K_S+S)\).
  2. Pirt (1965) split the uptake into a part for growth and a part for maintenance: \(q_S = \mu/Y_{X/S} + m_S\). Solving for \(\mu\) gives \(\mu = Y_{X/S}(q_S - m_S)\).
  3. For \(\mu\) to reach \(\mu_{\max}\) when sugar is in excess, \(q_{S,\max} = \mu_{\max}/Y_{X/S} + m_S\).
  4. Without oxygen the (strictly aerobic) cells can neither take up sugar nor respire for maintenance, so both terms are multiplied by \(C/(K_O + C)\).
  5. When the sugar runs out (\(q_S \to 0\)) the formula gives \(\mu = -Y_{X/S}m_S\): the cells burn their own mass (endogenous metabolism) — visible as a slow decline of \(X\) after a batch ends.
Assumptions and validity

Unstructured, unsegregated model: all cells are identical and the population is described by its total mass. Kinetic constants of Maurer et al. were measured for a GAP-promoter K. phaffii strain on glucose at 25 °C and pH 5; other strains, substrates (glycerol, methanol) and temperatures have different values. No overflow metabolism (K. phaffii is Crabtree-negative) and no substrate inhibition.

\([q_S] = \text{g g}^{-1}\text{h}^{-1}\); \(Y_{X/S}\,q_S\): (g g⁻¹)(g g⁻¹ h⁻¹) = h⁻¹ = \([\mu]\) ✓
Mass balances for batch, fed-batch and continuous culture(B2)
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \mu X - \frac{F_{\text{in}}}{V}X, &\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -q_S X + \frac{F_{\text{in}}}{V}\left(S_F - S\right), \\ \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} &= q_P X - \frac{F_{\text{in}}}{V}P, &\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} &= F_{\text{in}} - F_{\text{out}} \end{aligned} \]

biomass grows at μX and is diluted by incoming feed; sugar is consumed by the cells and replenished by the feed; product is made at q_P·X and diluted; the volume changes by feed in minus harvest out.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(F_{\text{in}}, F_{\text{out}}\)feed and harvest flow rates (batch: both 0; fed-batch: \(F_{\text{out}} = 0\); chemostat: \(F_{\text{out}} = F_{\text{in}} = DV\))m³ h⁻¹
\(S_F\)glucose in the feed: 550 g L⁻¹ in fed-batch (Maurer et al., 2006); the medium \(S_R = S_0\) in continuous cultureg L⁻¹

Follow the derivation
  1. Balance the total amount of biomass \(XV\): accumulation = production − outflow, \(\mathrm{d}(XV)/\mathrm{d}t = \mu XV - F_{\text{out}}X\) (the feed contains no cells).
  2. Product rule: \(\mathrm{d}(XV)/\mathrm{d}t = V\,\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t + X\,\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t = V\,\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t + X(F_{\text{in}} - F_{\text{out}})\).
  3. Equate and divide by \(V\): \(\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = \mu X - (F_{\text{in}}/V)X\). The harvest drops out: only incoming liquid dilutes the concentration — a neat result that holds in every mode.
  4. The same steps for \(SV\), with an inflow term \(F_{\text{in}}S_F\) and consumption \(q_SXV\), give the sugar balance; for \(PV\) the product balance.
Assumptions and validity

Ideally mixed liquid (no gradients). Sampling, base and antifoam additions and evaporation are neglected in the volume balance. Protein degradation by proteases is ignored.

\([\mu X] = \text{h}^{-1}\,\text{g L}^{-1}\); \([(F/V)X] = (\text{m}^3\text{h}^{-1}/\text{m}^3)\,\text{g L}^{-1}\) ✓
Product formation (Luedeking–Piret)(B3)
\[ q_P = \alpha\,\mu + \beta \]

the specific rate of product formation has a growth-associated part, proportional to the growth rate, and a non-growth-associated part that continues while the cells are active.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue used
\(q_P\)specific product formation rateg g⁻¹ h⁻¹—

Follow the derivation
  1. Luedeking and Piret (1959) found for lactic acid that \(\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}t = \alpha\,\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t + \beta X\). Dividing by \(X\) gives the specific form above.
  2. Maurer et al. (2006) measured \(q_P\) of a secreted Fab fragment in chemostats at several growth rates. At low growth rates (\(\mu \le 0.05\) h⁻¹) their data follow \(q_P = 0.2051\mu + 0.002\) (mg g⁻¹ h⁻¹, μ in h⁻¹); at higher μ the increase saturates (their Monod-type fit, \(q_{P,\max} = 0.0735\) mg g⁻¹ h⁻¹). The simulator uses the linear form throughout, so it slightly over-predicts \(q_P\) at μ above ≈ 0.1 h⁻¹.
  3. In the model \(\beta\) is multiplied by the sugar and oxygen saturation terms — starving cells make no product — and \(\alpha\mu\) counts only positive growth.
Assumptions and validity

The "food protein" strain is a thought experiment (all rates of the Fab strain multiplied by 100), not a published strain: it shows what specific productivity a commercial food-ingredient process must reach. Substrate and oxygen used to make the product are lumped into \(Y_{X/S}\) and \(m_S\).

\([\alpha\mu] = \text{g g}^{-1}\cdot\text{h}^{-1}\) = \([\beta]\) = g g⁻¹ h⁻¹ ✓
Exponential feed for a set growth rate(B4)
\[ F(t) = \frac{\left(\mu_{\text{set}}/Y_{X/S} + m_S\right)X_fV_f}{S_F}\;e^{\mu_{\text{set}}\,(t - t_f)} \]

to keep the cells growing at μ_set, feed exactly the sugar they need for growth plus maintenance; since the biomass grows exponentially, so must the feed.

\(\mu_{\text{set}}\)chosen growth rate (must be below \(\mu_{\max}\))h⁻¹

Follow the derivation
  1. Quasi-steady state for sugar: the residual \(S\) stays small and constant, so feed = uptake: \(F S_F = q_S XV\).
  2. For growth at \(\mu_{\text{set}}\): \(q_S = \mu_{\text{set}}/Y_{X/S} + m_S\) (Eq. B1), and the total biomass grows as \(XV = X_fV_f e^{\mu_{\text{set}}(t - t_f)}\).

Assumptions and validity

Open-loop (feed-forward) control: if the real yield differs from the assumed one, sugar accumulates or the culture starves. Industry therefore often combines exponential feeding with feedback on DO (DO-stat) or off-gas signals.

Chemostat steady state and washout (with maintenance)(B5)
\[ \begin{aligned} \mu &= D, \qquad q_S = \frac{D}{Y_{X/S}} + m_S, \qquad \bar S = \frac{K_S\,q_S}{q_{S,\max} - q_S}, \\ \bar X &= \frac{D\,(S_R - \bar S)}{q_S}, \qquad D_{\text{crit}} = Y_{X/S}\left(\frac{q_{S,\max}S_R}{K_S + S_R} - m_S\right) \end{aligned} \]

in a steady chemostat the cells grow exactly as fast as they are washed out; the residual sugar is whatever gives that growth rate, and all the sugar consumed ends up in the cells or is burned for maintenance.

Ideal mixing, sterile feed, no wall growth, no mutation. The simulated chemostat starts as a batch and switches to continuous flow when the sugar is used up, so the approach to steady state is visible.

\[ \begin{aligned} q_{O_2} &= \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\gamma_S\,q_S}{M_S} - \frac{\gamma_X\,\mu}{M_X}\right), \qquad \text{OUR} = q_{O_2}X, \\ \text{CER} &= \left(\frac{q_S}{M_S} - \frac{\mu}{M_X}\right)X, \qquad \text{RQ} = \frac{\text{CER}}{\text{OUR}} \end{aligned} \]

  2. New biomass keeps \(\gamma_X\mu/M_X\) mol of electrons (the product is small and lumped with biomass).

Oxygen solubility, pressure and gas-phase depletion(B8)
\[ \begin{aligned} C^{*} &= C^{*}_{\text{sat}}\!\left(T,\bar p\right)\frac{\bar y_{O_2}}{0.2095}, \qquad \bar p = p_{\text{top}} + \frac{\rho g H}{2}, \\ y_{\text{out}} &= y_{\text{in}} - \frac{\text{OTR}\;V}{\dot n_G}, \qquad \bar y_{O_2} = \frac{y_{\text{in}} + y_{\text{out}}}{2} \end{aligned} \]

the saturation concentration grows with the oxygen partial pressure: with the O₂ fraction in the gas and with the absolute pressure, which in a tall vessel includes the weight of the liquid above the bubbles.

\(C^{*}_{\text{sat}}(T,\bar p)\)O₂ solubility for air (Benson & Krause, 1984; doSaturation in physics.js)mmol L⁻¹
\(p_{\text{top}}\)head-space pressure (1 atm + overpressure)kPa

Follow the derivation
  1. Henry's law: \(C^{*} = p_{O_2}/H_{O_2}\), with \(p_{O_2} = y_{O_2}\,p\). The table value for air at 1 atm is scaled by the ratio of partial pressures.
  2. A bubble at depth \(z\) is at pressure \(p_{\text{top}} + \rho g z\); the mid-depth value is a simple average over the rise path. In a 100 m³ vessel (\(H\) = 8 m) it is about 0.4 bar higher than at the top.
  3. An O₂ balance over the gas phase: what enters minus what leaves equals what was transferred, \(\dot n_G(y_{\text{in}} - y_{\text{out}}) = \text{OTR}\,V\) (assuming the total molar flow is constant, which is accurate for RQ ≈ 1).
  4. The same relation, read backwards, is how plants measure OUR: gas flow × (O₂ in − O₂ out) from the off-gas analyser.
Assumptions and validity

Solubility in pure water (salts lower it by a few per cent); arithmetic mean of inlet and outlet gas composition and of top and bottom pressure (a log-mean is more exact for tall vessels).

\(\text{OTR}\,V/\dot n_G\): (mol m⁻³ h⁻¹)(m³)/(mol h⁻¹) = dimensionless ✓
Stirrer power, tip speed and Reynolds number(B9)
\[ \begin{aligned} P_0 &= n_i\,N_P\,\rho\,N^3D^5, \qquad P_g = 0.5\,P_0, \\ v_{\text{tip}} &= \pi N D, \qquad Re = \frac{\rho N D^2}{\eta} \end{aligned} \]

the power drawn by a turbine grows with the cube of its speed and the fifth power of its diameter; gassing roughly halves it.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(n_i\)number of submerged impellers (upper one only when covered by liquid)–1–2
\(N_P\)power number of a Rushton turbine in a baffled tank, turbulent flow–≈ 5 (Doran, 2013)
\(N\)stirrer speeds⁻¹—
\(D\)impeller diameter = \(T/3\)m—
\(P_g/P_0\)gassed-to-ungassed power ratio–0.5 (assumed; 30–60 % reductions are typical)
Follow the derivation
  1. Dimensional analysis: \(P\) depends on \(\rho, \eta, N, D\) and geometry → \(N_P = P/(\rho N^3D^5) = f(Re)\).
  2. For \(Re > 10^4\) (all scales here) \(N_P\) is constant, so \(P = N_P\rho N^3D^5\).
  3. Gas collects in cavities behind the blades and lowers the power the impeller can transmit; the model uses a fixed ratio \(P_g/P_0 = 0.5\).
Assumptions and validity

Water-like (Newtonian, low-viscosity) broth; impellers far enough apart (spacing \(T\)) for their powers to add. Filamentous or very dense broths are viscous and need different correlations.

\((\text{kg m}^{-3})(\text{s}^{-3})(\text{m}^5) = \text{kg m}^2\text{s}^{-3} = \text{W}\) ✓
kLa correlations for stirred vessels (van 't Riet)(B10)
\[ \begin{aligned} k_La &= 0.002\left(\frac{P_g}{V}\right)^{0.7}v_s^{0.2} \quad \text{(non-coalescing)} \\ k_La &= 0.026\left(\frac{P_g}{V}\right)^{0.4}v_s^{0.5} \quad \text{(coalescing)}, \qquad v_s = \frac{Q_G}{\pi T^2/4} \end{aligned} \]

the transfer coefficient rises with the gassed power per volume and with the superficial gas velocity; in salty broths, where bubbles do not merge, power helps even more.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(k_La\)volumetric mass-transfer coefficients⁻¹ (× 3600 → h⁻¹)
\(P_g/V\)gassed power per volumeW m⁻³
\(v_s\)superficial gas velocity at operating pressure and temperaturem s⁻¹

Follow the derivation
  1. These are empirical power laws fitted to many measurements (van 't Riet, 1979); the constants carry hidden units, so use exactly the units above.
  2. More power breaks bubbles into smaller ones (larger \(a\)); more gas adds more bubbles. In coalescing liquids (pure water) small bubbles quickly merge again, so extra power pays off less (exponent 0.4 instead of 0.7).
  3. The actual gas flow at the sparger follows from vvm by the ideal-gas law: \(Q_G = Q_N\,(T/273.15\,\text{K})(101.3\,\text{kPa}/\bar p)\).
Assumptions and validity

Van 't Riet's data covered vessels from a few litres to a few cubic metres and 500–10 000 W m⁻³, with an accuracy of roughly ±20–40 %. Applying the correlation to 100 m³ is an extrapolation, as is common in first design estimates; measure \(k_La\) in the real vessel (Garcia-Ochoa & Gomez, 2009).

\((\text{W m}^{-3})^{0.7}(\text{m s}^{-1})^{0.2}\) × constant (s⁻¹ in these units) — empirical, not dimensionally homogeneous.
Heat balance and cooling capacity(B11)
\[ \begin{aligned} \dot Q_{\text{met}} &= \Delta h_{O_2}\,\text{OUR}\,V, \qquad \dot Q_{\text{cool,max}} = U A\left(T - T_{cw}\right), \qquad A = \pi T H + a_c V \\ \rho c_p V\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \dot Q_{\text{met}} + P_g - \dot Q_{\text{cool}} \end{aligned} \]

the culture releases 460 kJ for every mole of oxygen it consumes; together with the stirrer power this heat must leave through the jacket and coils, or the broth warms up.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(\Delta h_{O_2}\)heat released per mol O₂ consumedkJ mol⁻¹460 (Doran, 2013; Cooney et al., 1969)
\(U\)overall heat-transfer coefficientW m⁻² K⁻¹250 (glass, 2 L); 500 (steel), assumed
\(A\)wetted jacket area plus extra coil area \(a_cV\)m²—
\(T_{cw}\)cooling-water temperature°C15 (default)
\(\dot Q_{\text{cool}}\)heat removed; the controller sets it to hold \(T\), up to \(\dot Q_{\text{cool,max}}\)W—
Follow the derivation
  1. Aerobic metabolism transfers electrons from substrate to O₂. About 115 kJ is released per mole of electrons reaching oxygen, so 4 × 115 ≈ 460 kJ per mol O₂, largely independent of the substrate — confirmed calorimetrically by Cooney et al. (1969) (Doran, 2013).
  2. All shaft power is dissipated by viscous friction and ends up as heat, so \(P_g\) is added.
  3. Energy balance on the broth: \(\rho c_pV\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t\) = heat in − heat out. The controller removes exactly the load (plus a correction to return to set-point) as long as \(\dot Q_{\text{cool,max}}\) allows.
  4. Rule of thumb: OUR in mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹ × 0.128 = heat in kW m⁻³.
Assumptions and validity

Coolant temperature taken as its inlet value (high flow); evaporation, sensible heat of feed and gas, and heat losses to the room are neglected. The kinetic model does not include the effect of an overheated broth on the cells: the red readouts tell you when a real culture would be in trouble.

\((\text{J mol}^{-1})(\text{mol m}^{-3}\text{s}^{-1})(\text{m}^3) = \text{W}\) ✓ · \((\text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1})(\text{m}^2)(\text{K}) = \text{W}\) ✓
Scale-up at constant power per volume(B12)
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{P}{V}\propto N^3D^2 = \text{const} \ &\Rightarrow\ N\propto D^{-2/3}, \quad v_{\text{tip}}\propto D^{1/3}, \quad t_m\propto\frac{1}{N}\propto D^{2/3} \\ \frac{A}{V} = \frac{4}{T}, \qquad &\text{OUR}_{\text{heat}} = \frac{U\,\frac{A}{V}\,(T - T_{cw}) - \frac{P_g}{V}}{\Delta h_{O_2}} \end{aligned} \]

keeping the power per volume constant slows the stirrer as the vessel grows, speeds up the blade tips and lengthens the mixing time; the cooling surface per volume shrinks in inverse proportion to the tank diameter, and with it the oxygen uptake the vessel can afford.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(t_m\)mixing time (\(Nt_m\) = constant for geometrically similar tanks, turbulent flow)s
\(A/V\)jacket area per volume for \(H = 2T\)m² m⁻³
\(\text{OUR}_{\text{heat}}\)largest oxygen uptake whose heat can be removed at the set-pointmol m⁻³ s⁻¹ (× 3600 → mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹)
Follow the derivation
  1. From Eq. B9, \(P/V \propto N^3D^5/D^3 = N^3D^2\) for geometrically similar vessels (\(V \propto D^3\)). Constant \(P/V\) → \(N \propto D^{-2/3}\).
  2. Tip speed \(\pi ND \propto D^{1/3}\): a 37-fold larger diameter (2 L → 100 m³) gives a 3.3-fold higher tip speed at the same P/V.
  3. Wall area \(\pi TH = 2\pi T^2\), volume \((\pi/4)T^2\cdot2T = (\pi/2)T^3\) → \(A/V = 4/T\).
  4. Setting \(\dot Q_{\text{met}} + P_g = UA(T - T_{cw})\) and solving for OUR gives the heat ceiling.
Assumptions and validity

Strict geometric similarity (real production vessels are often taller, H/T = 2.5–3, with three or four impellers, and use internal coils or external heat exchangers). The mixing-time rule ignores the effect of aspect ratio and multiple impellers.

\((\text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1})(\text{m}^2\text{m}^{-3})(\text{K})/(\text{J mol}^{-1}) = \text{mol m}^{-3}\text{s}^{-1}\) ✓
Dissolved-oxygen cascade controller(B13)
\[ \begin{aligned} e &= \frac{\text{DO}_{\text{sp}} - \text{DO}}{100}, \qquad u = K_p\,e + K_i\!\int e\,\mathrm{d}t, \\ N &= N_{\min} + (N_{\max} - N_{\min})\min(u,1), \qquad y_{\text{in}} = 0.2095 + 0.29\,\mathrm{clip}(u-1,\,0,\,1) \end{aligned} \]

a proportional-integral controller turns the DO error into one output u; the first half of its range sets the stirrer speed, the second half adds oxygen to the inlet gas.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(K_p, K_i\)proportional gain; integral gain–; h⁻¹1.2; 40
\(N_{\max}\)speed giving the chosen maximum ungassed P/V with both impellers covereds⁻¹—
\(N_{\min}\)minimum speed (keeps the broth mixed and the gas dispersed)s⁻¹\(0.25N_{\max}\)
Follow the derivation
  1. Proportional action reacts to the current error; integral action removes the steady offset by accumulating past error.
  2. The integral is clamped between 0 and the top of the range (anti-windup), so the controller does not "wind up" while an actuator is saturated.
  3. Split-range (cascade) operation lets one controller drive several actuators in a chosen order: cheap agitation first, expensive pure oxygen last.
Assumptions and validity

Ideal, noise-free DO measurement without probe lag (real membrane probes respond in 10–60 s). The gains were tuned by trial in this simulator; see the module on control for systematic tuning.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator to answer. Record settings and results with Save investigation, or download the full time series as CSV for your own plots.

  1. Read the growth rate from the data

    Run the Lab batch (2 L) preset. Download the CSV and plot ln X against time. Fit a straight line to the exponential phase: is the slope equal to μmax = 0.20 h⁻¹? Explain any difference (hint: look at the oxygen term in Eq. B1 and at the early DO). Why does X slowly decline after the sugar is gone, and why does the RQ change at that moment?

  2. Find the oxygen-transfer limit

    Switch to Manual speed and run batches at 30, 40, 60 and 80 % of maximum speed. For each, record the minimum DO and the maximum OUR. Plot the maximum OUR against Pg/V and compare with the transfer ceiling predicted by Eqs. B7 and B10. At what speed does the culture just avoid oxygen limitation (DO > 10 %)?

  3. Design a fed-batch

    At 2 L, find the set growth rate μset that gives the highest product space–time yield within the run, (a) with and (b) without O₂ enrichment. Tabulate μset, final X, final titre and run time. Why is the fastest feed not the best? What happens to the glucose readout if you switch the feed protection off at μset = 0.18 h⁻¹?

  4. Map the chemostat

    In continuous mode, measure the steady-state X̄, S̄ and D·X̄ at D = 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.18 and 0.20 h⁻¹ (wait at least five residence times, 5/D). Plot them against D together with Eq. B5 (mini-calculator). Where is the productivity maximum, how close is it to washout, and why is the observed yield lower at D = 0.02 h⁻¹?

  5. Scale it up

    Run the same fed-batch recipe at 2 L, 1 m³ and 100 m³ (constant P/V = 3 kW m⁻³). For each scale record the run time, final X, titre, space–time yield, peak OUR and which limit was active (oxygen or heat). Then, at 100 m³, find the combination of extra cooling surface and cooling-water temperature that restores the 2 L productivity. Estimate the coil area in m² for the whole vessel.

  6. Is it a food process?

    Switch to the hypothetical food protein strain and run the best 100 m³ process you found. From the final titre and run time (add 12 h for cleaning, sterilisation and filling), estimate how many 100 m³ fermenters a factory would need to produce 10 000 t of protein per year, assuming 90 % recovery in downstream processing. Discuss what would have to change — strain, process or equipment — to halve that number.

Model assumptions

  • Organism and product. Komagataella phaffii (Pichia pastoris) X-33 with the constitutive GAP promoter, growing on glucose at 25 °C and pH 5 and secreting a Fab antibody fragment (Maurer et al., 2006). Its kinetic constants stand in for a precision-fermentation host; the "food protein" strain is a hypothetical ×100 version.
  • Kinetics. Unstructured Herbert–Pirt model (Eq. B1) with dual (sugar, oxygen) saturation; endogenous decay when sugar is absent; no overflow metabolism, inhibition, death or morphology changes; kinetics independent of temperature (temperature excursions are flagged, not simulated).
  • Mixing. Ideal mixing of the liquid at every scale. Real large vessels have mixing times of tens of seconds to minutes and gradients of sugar, oxygen and CO₂ that affect the cells — the main reason why processes often perform worse after scale-up.
  • Oxygen transfer. van 't Riet (1979) correlations with gassed power \(P_g = 0.5P_0\), Rushton power number 5, two impellers (the upper one only when submerged); solubility from Benson & Krause (1984) for pure water, corrected for pressure and gas composition; mean of inlet/outlet gas and top/bottom pressure; O₂ enrichment up to 50 %.
  • Heat. 460 kJ per mol O₂ plus all shaft power; heat-transfer coefficient 250 W m⁻² K⁻¹ (glass jacket) or 500 W m⁻² K⁻¹ (steel), assumed typical values; coolant at its inlet temperature; no evaporation or heat losses.
  • Geometry. Geometrically similar vessels with H = 2T, two Rushton turbines D = T/3 at 0.5T and 1.5T, four baffles T/10, ring sparger under the lower impeller. Fed-batch starts at 60 % of the working volume.
  • Visual elements. Bubble size and number, foam height and impeller rotation (capped at about 2 rev s⁻¹ with a motion-blur disc above that) are illustrative. The foam index grows with gas velocity and protein content; the antifoam pump resets it. Bubbles at 1 m³ and 100 m³ are drawn much larger than real bubbles so that they remain visible.
