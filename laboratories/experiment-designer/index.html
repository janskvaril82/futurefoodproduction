Growth-room experiment designer

Plan a real experiment before you grow a single plant. Put trays of lettuce for your treatments on the shelves of a 3D growth room, decide how many replicate trays and plants you need and how to arrange them — in convenient groups, completely at random, in blocks, or with one tray per treatment. The room hides what every real growth room hides: less light towards the ends and the bottom of the racks, warmer air near the ceiling and a cold draught from the door. Grow and harvest once to get the ANOVA your design calls for, then repeat the experiment a thousand times to find out which designs tell the truth.

A guided tour in six experiments

  1. Read the plan. The laboratory opens with a randomised complete block design: three LED spectra, four replicate trays per spectrum and six lettuce plants per tray. Each shelf of rack A is one block and holds one tray of every spectrum in a random order. The coloured tray labels (T1·3 = treatment 1, tray 3) and the LED module above each tray show which spectrum it receives; the white dividers keep the spectra apart. The Layout plan below the analysis panel is the drawing you would pin to the door.
  2. Grow & harvest once. The seedlings grow to their simulated harvest weight and the analysis appears: an ANOVA on the twelve tray means with shelf as block, the treatment means and a report sentence. Look at the box The truth: the best spectrum really is 15 % (about 26 g) better than the control, yet your estimate misses it by a random error of typically ±13 g — and with four trays per treatment the effect is usually not significant.
  3. Reveal the room. Switch Reveal to Growth. Shelves near the floor, the ends of every shelf and the positions next to the door are poorer; the top shelves are best. Try PPFD and Air temp., switch on the air currents and click a tray to read its hidden light, temperature and growth potential. Differences of 20–30 % between positions are larger than the treatment effect you are trying to detect.
  4. Convenient but confounded. This preset loads the trays treatment by treatment, from the top of rack A down. The control gets the best shelves, the best spectrum the worst. Harvest a few times: the estimated effect hovers around zero although the truth is +26 g — the treatment effect is cancelled by the position effect. Now set the replicates to 6: the bias changes sign and the same design “proves” a huge effect. A systematic layout is not wrong by a known amount that you could correct afterwards — it is wrong by an unknown amount in an unknown direction.
  5. The tempting shortcut. One tray per treatment with twelve plants each, and no true effect at all (Δ = 0). Harvest several times: small p-values keep appearing, because the plant-level ANOVA mistakes tray and position differences for treatment effects. Press Run 1000 times: about seven experiments in ten are “significant”. Twelve plants in one tray are twelve sub-samples of one replicate, not twelve replicates.
  6. Judge the designs. Go back to Textbook RCBD and press Run 1000 times. The ridges show that the completely randomised and the blocked designs are unbiased, the blocked one more precise; the red bars show which designs keep their promise of 5 % false positives; the power curves show how many replicate trays you really need — far more than most student projects use.
Key idea

A controlled-environment room is not a uniform environment. Replicate the thing you applied the treatment to (the tray, not the plant), randomise which position each replicate gets so that unknown gradients cannot masquerade as treatment effects, and block on the gradients you know about so that they stop inflating your error. Statistics after the experiment cannot rescue a design that broke these rules.

Why a growth room is not uniform

Growth rooms and climate chambers are built to hold light, temperature and humidity constant, yet uniformity trials — growing one crop with no treatments at all and mapping how it grows — have repeatedly found position effects that are as large as typical treatment effects. In the North Carolina State University phytotron, Lee and Rawlings (1982) found consistent differences in plant growth between positions within chambers and between chambers, and Potvin and Tardif (1988) concluded that such variability must be handled by the experimental design. The reasons are physical, and this laboratory models the three most common ones.

  • Light. Near the open ends of a shelf, fewer LED modules contribute photons and some light escapes; photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) falls towards the ends (see the Grow-room lighting designer). Drivers and LEDs also age at different rates, and the top shelf receives some light from the ceiling luminaires. Lettuce fresh weight increases almost in proportion to the daily light integral in the range used indoors (Kelly et al., 2020), so a 10 % light deficit costs roughly 8 % in yield.
  • Temperature. Warm air is less dense and rises. Heat from the lamps accumulates towards the ceiling, so the air around the top shelf can be 2 °C or more warmer than near the floor. Lettuce growth increases with air temperature up to the low or mid 20s °C (Thompson et al., 1998).
  • Air movement. Every time the door opens — or if it does not close properly — cold, dense air flows in along the floor and chills the nearest, lowest positions; draughts also raise transpiration and can damage leaves.

None of this is visible when you set up an experiment. That is the whole point of this laboratory: the gradients are hidden until you choose to reveal them, just as they would be in your own growth room unless you had mapped it with a quantum sensor, a thermometer and a uniformity trial.

Treatment, experimental unit, observational unit

Getting the experimental unit right determines everything else: the number of replicates, the error term and the degrees of freedom of the test. If the treatment is applied to a whole shelf (one lamp setting per shelf), the shelf is the unit, and two shelves per treatment means two replicates — however many plants stand on them.

Four layouts

Randomisation turns bias into noise

If the trays of one treatment happen to stand on better positions, the treatment looks better than it is. Nobody can list every gradient in a room, so the only general protection is to let chance decide the positions: then no treatment is favoured on average, the position effects become part of the random error, and the ANOVA's error term measures them honestly. This is Fisher's (1935) central argument for randomisation. A particular randomisation can still be unlucky — the truth box shows its chance imbalance — but over many experiments the estimate is unbiased, and the confidence interval accounts for the extra noise.

Common misconception

“I arranged the treatments neatly so that every treatment has the same conditions.” Neat layouts are almost always systematic: treatment one at the door, treatment two in the middle, treatment three at the back. Any gradient along that direction — light, temperature, draught, the order in which you water or harvest — is then confounded with the treatment, and no statistical analysis can separate the two afterwards (Casler, 2015).

Blocking removes the gradients you know about

Randomisation protects against bias but not against noise: the position differences still inflate the error. If you know a direction in which conditions change — here, shelf height — you can form blocks of units that are alike (one shelf), put every treatment once in every block and randomise within blocks. The analysis then removes the block differences from the error (Eq. X7). Blocking pays when blocks really differ; in a perfectly uniform room it costs error degrees of freedom and slightly reduces power — try the Perfectly uniform room preset with both designs. Blocking by shelf does not remove the draught or the shelf-end effect, which act within every shelf; designs that block in two directions (Latin squares, row–column designs) or model the spatial trend can remove those as well (Mead et al., 2012; Brien et al., 2013).

Pseudo-replication

Hurlbert (1984) coined the term pseudo-replication for “the use of inferential statistics to test for treatment effects with data from experiments where either treatments are not replicated (though samples may be) or replicates are not statistically independent”. With one tray per treatment, the difference between two trays contains the treatment effect plus the difference between two trays plus the difference between two positions. The plant-level ANOVA compares it with the scatter of plants within a tray, which contains none of the tray or position differences; the test statistic is inflated and small p-values appear by the dozen (Eq. X9). More plants per tray make it worse, not better. The same error is common in laboratory science (Lazic, 2010) and in student projects: repeated measurements of the same plant, several leaves of one plant, several plants in one pot or reservoir.

A test that cannot tell you it is wrong

A pseudo-replicated analysis produces a perfectly normal-looking ANOVA table. Nothing in the output warns you; only the design does. That is why reviewers — and your teacher — ask first: what was the experimental unit, and how many were there?

More trays or more plants?

Every tray mean contains three kinds of variation: position, tray and plants (Eq. X5). Measuring more plants per tray only shrinks the last term; the first two are divided only by the number of trays. In the default room, 4 trays × 12 plants per treatment give an estimate with a standard deviation of about 16 g, while 8 trays × 6 plants — the same 48 plants — give about 11 g, and even 8 trays × 3 plants (24 plants) beat 4 × 12. Spend your resources on experimental units.

Frontier

Large plant-phenotyping facilities move plants on conveyor belts past imaging stations and use row–column designs with spatial statistical models to separate treatment effects from greenhouse gradients (Brien et al., 2013). The model behind the simulator

Notation: treatment \(i = 1,\dots,t\) (treatment 1 is the control), tray \(j\) (the experimental unit), plant \(k = 1,\dots,n\) in the tray, \(r\) replicate trays per treatment. The hidden fields (Eqs. X2–X4) are deterministic functions of position; everything else is random.

Why multiply by g but add u?
  1. Light and temperature change the growth rate, so they scale the whole plant: a treatment that adds 15 % adds 15 % of whatever the position allows. A multiplicative model captures this; it also means that the treatment effect in grams is slightly larger on good positions (a treatment × position interaction on the gram scale, none on the log scale).
  2. Tray effects — a slower pump, a reservoir that warmed up, a batch of weaker seedlings — shift all plants in the tray by a similar amount, so they are modelled as an additive random effect shared by the plants.
  3. Plant-to-plant variation (σ ≈ 14 % of the mean) reflects genetic and micro-site differences between seedlings.
Assumptions and validity

Normal, independent tray and plant effects; no competition between neighbouring trays; no missing plants. Fresh weights are truncated at 5 g, which practically never happens at the default settings.

Physics behind the two terms
  1. Stratification. Air heated by lamps and drivers expands and becomes buoyant; without strong mixing it collects under the ceiling, giving a stable vertical gradient of a few kelvin per room height.
  2. Draught. Cold air from a colder corridor is denser; it enters low through an open or leaking door and spreads along the floor as a gravity current, mixing with room air as it goes. An exponential decay with a length scale of about 0.9 m is a simple description of that mixing.
Assumptions and validity

Steady state; humidity, air speed and leaf temperature are not modelled separately. In a real room the draught comes and goes with door openings, which makes its effect harder to see and easier to confound.

How large are the differences?

At G = 1 the best position (top shelf of rack B, middle of the shelf) has g = 1.15, the worst (bottom shelf of rack A, next to the door) g = 0.80: a 44 % range, of which more than half comes from light and the rest roughly equally from the warmer air higher up and from the door draught (its cooling and air-movement stress). How precise is a treatment mean?(X5)
\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar y_{j\cdot}) = \sigma^2_{\text{pos}} + \sigma^2_{\text{tray}} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{plant}}}{n},\qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar y_{i}-\bar y_{1}) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{r}\left(\sigma^2_{\text{pos}} + \sigma^2_{\text{tray}} + \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{plant}}}{n}\right)} \]

the variance of one tray mean is the variance of position effects plus the variance of tray effects plus the plant variance divided by the number of plants in the tray; the standard error of the difference between two treatment means divides that variance by the number of replicate trays.

Follow the derivation
  1. A tray mean is \(\bar y_{j\cdot} = B\tau g_j + u_j + \bar e_{j\cdot}\). With randomised positions \(B\tau g_j\) is a random draw with variance \(\sigma^2_{\text{pos}}\) (for the control, τ = 1).
  2. The three terms are independent, so their variances add; averaging n independent plant errors divides their variance by n.
  3. A treatment mean averages r independent tray means (variance ÷ r), and the difference of two independent means doubles it.
  4. Default room, CRD: \(\sigma_{\text{pos}} = 17.0\), \(\sigma_{\text{tray}} = 10\), \(\sigma_{\text{plant}} = 25\), n = 6, r = 4 → SE = \(\sqrt{0.5\,(289+100+104)} = 15.7\) g; the Monte Carlo gives 16.3 g. RCBD: within-shelf \(\sigma_{\text{pos}} = 11.2\) → SE = 12.8 g (Monte Carlo 13.1).
  5. As n → ∞ the SE only falls to \(\sqrt{0.5\,(289+100)} = 13.9\) g: plants per tray cannot replace trays.

One-way ANOVA on tray means (CRD)(X6)
\[ F = \frac{r\sum_{i}(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2/(t-1)}{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}(\bar y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot})^2/\big(t(r-1)\big)} \;\sim\; F_{t-1,\;t(r-1)}\ \text{ if } H_0 \text{ is true} \]

the variance between treatment means, scaled to one tray, is divided by the variance between trays of the same treatment; if all treatments are equal both estimate the variance of a tray mean and their ratio follows the F distribution with t − 1 and t(r − 1) degrees of freedom.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\bar y_{ij}\)mean of the n plants in tray j of treatment ig

Why tray means, and not plants?
  1. Under H₀ the expected numerator is \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar y_{j\cdot})\) from Eq. X5 — tray, position and plant variation together.
  2. The denominator compares trays that received the same treatment, so it contains exactly the same three components: the ratio is fair.
  3. For balanced data this is identical to the F-test of a nested ANOVA with plants within trays within treatments. Using the plants as the error term instead leaves out the tray and position components (Eq. X9).
Assumptions and validity

Randomised positions, independent trays, approximately normal tray means with equal variances. Randomisation itself justifies the test approximately even when the position effects are not normal (Fisher, 1935).

Why more replicates do not help

Replication shrinks random error, not bias. The systematic design's false-positive rate can even rise with r, because a fixed bias becomes ever more “significant” as the standard error falls.

How pseudo-replication inflates the false-positive rate(X9)
\[ \lambda = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{between}} + \sigma^2_{\text{plant}}/n}{\sigma^2_{\text{plant}}/n} = 1+\frac{n\,\sigma^2_{\text{between}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{plant}}},\qquad \sigma^2_{\text{between}} = \sigma^2_{\text{pos}}+\sigma^2_{\text{tray}},\qquad \alpha_{\text{actual}} = P\!\left(F_{t-1,\;t(n-1)} > \frac{F_{1-\alpha;\,t-1,\,t(n-1)}}{\lambda}\right) \]

with one tray per treatment, the real variance of a treatment mean is λ times larger than the plant-level analysis assumes; the naive F statistic is therefore inflated by the factor λ and exceeds its critical value far more often than α — and λ grows with the number of plants per tray.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator. Tick each task when done; Save investigation copies the settings and the last result into your notebook.

  1. Map the room before you plant

    Reveal PPFD, air temperature and growth potential in turn and click trays in different positions. Which shelf and which end are best and worst? Estimate the size (in %) of the vertical and the horizontal gradients. Which of them does blocking by shelf remove, and which does it leave in the error?

  2. A bias you cannot see

    Load Convenient but confounded. Harvest five times and write down the estimated effect each time; compare with the truth box. Then set r = 6 and r = 8 and repeat. Explain, with Eq. X8, why the bias changes size and sign, and why more replicates cannot remove it.

  3. How often does pseudo-replication lie?

    Load The tempting shortcut (Δ = 0). Run the Monte Carlo for 4, 6, 8 and 12 plants per tray and plot the false-positive rate against n. Compare with Eq. X9. Then set σtray = 0 and the gradient strength to 0: why is the plant-level test now valid — and why can you never know this in a real growth room?

  4. When does blocking pay?

    With Δ = 15 %, t = 3 and r = 4, compare the power of the CRD and the RCBD at gradient strengths 0, 1 and 2 (Monte Carlo). Record the relative efficiency from single harvests. When does blocking help, when does it cost, and why?

  5. Trays or plants?

    You can afford 48 plants per treatment. Compare 4 trays × 12 plants with 8 trays × 6 plants in the completely randomised design: record the SD of the estimates (ridge chart) and the power. Explain the result with Eq. X5, and calculate the SE for 16 trays × 3 plants.

  6. Design your project experiment

    Describe your own treatments, experimental unit and space. Choose t, r, n and a design that reaches at least 80 % power for a realistic effect, or explain what effect size you can realistically detect. Draw your layout plan with a real randomisation (for example from a random-number table or a spreadsheet) and justify your blocks.

Model assumptions

  • The room (6.4 × 5.2 m, two racks with four shelves and four tray positions each) has smooth, deterministic gradients of light, temperature and draught (Eqs. X2–X4). They are constant during the crop cycle and the same in every simulated repetition; only randomisations, trays and plants are new each time. Real rooms also fluctuate from day to day and with lamp ageing and door use.
  • Fresh weight responds multiplicatively to position (light0.8, +3 % per °C, −6 % in the core of the draught) and to the treatment; there is no treatment × position interaction beyond this multiplicative scaling. The parameter values are plausible for lettuce but chosen for teaching, not fitted to a particular room.
  • Spectral treatments are perfectly separated by light-tight compartments (the white partitions are drawn semi-transparent so that the trays stay visible); EC treatments have independent reservoirs. Neighbouring trays do not shade or compete with each other, and plants within a tray do not compete either.
  • Tray effects and plant deviations are independent and normally distributed; no plants are lost; fresh weights are truncated at 5 g.
  • All designs use the same area: the first r shelves (rack A from the top down, then rack B) and the first t positions from the door. The systematic design fills it treatment by treatment; the pseudo-replicated design places one tray per treatment at random positions within it.
  • Analyses (from the course library stats.js): one-way ANOVA on tray means (systematic, CRD), randomised-block ANOVA on tray means with shelf as block (RCBD), and the plant-level one-way ANOVA commonly — and wrongly — applied to pseudo-replicated data. The contrast best − control has a Welch confidence interval (one-way designs) or an interval from the block-design error mean square (RCBD). The block ANOVA was checked against Montgomery's (2017) textbook example and an independent least-squares fit.
  • Monte Carlo: 1000 experiments per design with the chosen effect and 1000 with Δ = 0; 300 per point for the replication sweep. With 1000 experiments a true rate of 5 % is estimated with a standard error of 0.7 percentage points.
  • The 3D plants are drawn with a size proportional to (fresh weight)0.55; the LED colours, heat maps and air-flow particles illustrate the model.
