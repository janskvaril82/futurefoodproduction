Neophobia scales

Before your panel tastes cricket bread or vertical-farm lettuce, it is worth knowing who is sitting in the booths. The Food Neophobia Scale measures the reluctance to eat new foods; the Food Technology Neophobia Scale measures distrust of foods made with new technologies. Take both questionnaires, see exactly how the reverse-worded items are scored, and place your score among published populations from Sweden, Finland, Norway and beyond. Then become the survey designer: simulate panels of different sizes to see how reliable the scales are, what careless answers and a forgotten reverse-coding do to Cronbach's α, and how strongly neophobia can predict who will actually taste the novel food.

What to look at first

  1. Answer the FNS. Tick one circle per statement. With Show how each answer is scored on, the right-hand column shows the value that enters your score: the five items marked R are worded in the neophilic direction (“I will eat almost anything”), so agreement means less neophobia and the answer is reversed (8 − x). The footer builds your total from the two sums.
  2. Place yourself. Choose a reference population. The same score can be average in a Finnish sample and clearly above average in a Swedish one — the percentile readout changes with the reference, not with you.
  3. Switch to the FTNS. Thirteen statements about food technologies; four of them (10–13) are reversed. Many people who love exotic food are sceptical of “lab food”, and the two scores correlate only moderately.
  4. Run a survey. The Simulate a survey panel generates answers from a model population. With 60 respondents α is about 0.8, and the Feldt interval tells you how uncertain that estimate is. Try the preset Small class (20): the interval becomes wide.
  5. Make the classic mistake. Apply Forgot reverse coding. α collapses or even becomes negative, and the item analysis shows the reversed items correlating negatively with the rest — the tell-tale signature of this error.
  6. Predict tasting. The last chart shows how the share of people willing to taste the novel food falls with the scale score. Change ρ and the reliability of the scale (item loading) and watch the observed correlation shrink below the true one.
Key idea

A questionnaire score is a measurement with error. Reverse-scored items protect against people who agree with everything, Cronbach's α tells you how consistently the items measure one trait, and the reliability limits how strongly the score can ever predict behaviour such as tasting a novel food.

Measuring a trait with a questionnaire

Food neophobia is the reluctance to eat, or the avoidance of, novel foods. Pliner and Hobden (1992) turned it into a ten-item questionnaire, the Food Neophobia Scale, and showed that the scores had satisfactory test–retest reliability and internal consistency and predicted behaviour in laboratory food-selection tests. Five items are worded in the neophobic direction and five in the neophilic direction; each is answered on a seven-point agreement scale, the neophilic items are reverse-scored, and the total runs from 10 (very neophilic) to 70 (very neophobic) (Eq. N1; see Lesson 13.1).

Many future foods are familiar products made in unfamiliar ways. The Food Technology Neophobia Scale of Cox and Evans (2008) was built from 81 candidate statements, reduced to 13 items in samples of Australian consumers. Nine items express scepticism (for example “It can be risky to switch to new food technologies too quickly”), four are positive and reverse-scored; the total runs from 13 to 91 (Eq. N2). Its items group into four factors — new food technologies are unnecessary, perception of risks, healthy choices, and information/media. In a retest study the FTNS was stable (intra-class correlation 0.86) and correlated between −0.39 and −0.58 with willingness to try foods produced by novel technologies (Evans et al., 2010). A systematic review of consumer studies using the FTNS is given by Wendt and Weinrich (2023).

Population differences — and the Nordic picture

Mean FNS scores differ between countries and samples. As compiled by Szakály et al. (2021): Swedish mothers and fathers in families with children scored 25.0 and 27.0 (Koivisto & Sjödén, 1996); Finnish adults 33.9 in a representative sample (Tuorila et al., 2001) and 38.0 in another adult sample; Canadian students in the original study 34.5 (Pliner & Hobden, 1992); US and Lebanese students 29.8 and 36.4; Hungarian adults 39.8. A cross-national confirmatory factor analysis of US, Swedish and Finnish samples found the Swedish respondents generally more willing to try novel foods (Ritchey et al., 2003). For the FTNS, Norwegian adults averaged 4.18 per item (≈ 54 of 91), lower than Romanian and Turkish respondents in the same survey (Almli et al., 2019). Compare only like with like: samples differ in age, education and urbanisation, all of which relate to neophobia (Tuorila et al., 2001).

Reliability: do the items agree?

If all items measure the same trait, people who score high on one item tend to score high on the others. Cronbach's (1951) α summarises this internal consistency (Eq. N4): it compares the sum of the item variances with the variance of the total score. With 10 items and a typical inter-item correlation of about 0.35, α is about 0.84. Values of 0.7–0.9 are usually considered good; above 0.95 the items may be redundant (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). The corrected item–total correlations and “α if item deleted” identify items that do not fit. A negative α almost always means that reverse-worded items were not recoded. α is itself an estimate: Feldt's (1965) interval (Eq. N5) shows that a class of 20 students cannot pin it down to better than about ±0.13 (for α ≈ 0.8 and 10 items).

From score to behaviour

Neophobia predicts whether people try a new food, but not perfectly. In the simulator each respondent has a latent trait θ (the “true” neophobia) and a latent willingness W that correlates with it (ρ). A person tastes the novel food when W exceeds a threshold that sets the overall share of tasters (Eq. N8). The questionnaire measures θ with error, so the observed correlation between score and willingness is weaker than ρ — by the factor √α (Spearman, 1904; Eq. N7). The effect sizes found in real studies are of this order: FTNS–willingness correlations of −0.39 to −0.58 (Evans et al., 2010); in a Belgian study, neophobia was the strongest predictor of readiness to adopt insects as a meat substitute (Verbeke, 2015). For your own study, measure neophobia before tasting and use it to describe the panel and as a covariate in the hedonic analyser.

\[ \text{FNS} = \sum_{i\in N} x_i + \sum_{i\in R}(8 - x_i) = S_N + 40 - S_R, \qquad N = \{2,3,5,7,8\},\ R = \{1,4,6,9,10\} \]

the FNS total is the sum of the five neophobic answers plus, for each of the five neophilic answers, eight minus the answer — equivalently the neophobic sum plus forty minus the neophilic sum.

Cronbach's α, item–total correlation and α if item deleted(N4)
\[ \alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} s_j^2}{s_X^2}\right), \qquad r_{j,\text{rest}} = \operatorname{corr}\bigl(x_j,\ X - x_j\bigr), \qquad \alpha_{(-j)} = \alpha \text{ of the other } k-1 \text{ items} \]

alpha compares the sum of the item variances with the variance of the total score, scaled by k over k minus one; the corrected item–total correlation correlates each item with the sum of all other items; alpha-if-deleted recomputes alpha without that item.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(k\)number of items (10 or 13)–
\(s_j^2, s_X^2\)variance of item \(j\) and of the total score \(X\)points²
\(r_{j,\text{rest}}\)corrected item–total correlation–
Follow the derivation
  1. \(s_X^2 = \sum_j s_j^2 + \sum_{j\ne l} s_{jl}\): the total variance is the item variances plus all covariances.
  2. If the items measure one trait, the covariances are positive and large relative to the item variances, so \(\sum s_j^2/s_X^2\) is small and α approaches 1.
  3. The factor \(k/(k-1)\) makes α equal to the reliability for parallel items (Cronbach, 1951; Eq. 13.2.7).
  4. An unreversed item correlates negatively with the others: the covariances turn negative, \(s_X^2\) shrinks below \(\sum s_j^2\), and α becomes small or negative.

Confidence interval for α (Feldt)(N5)

the ratio of one minus the true reliability to one minus the sample alpha follows an F distribution with n − 1 and (n − 1)(k − 1) degrees of freedom; inverting it gives a 95 % confidence interval for alpha.

Simulated answers: an ordinal one-factor model(N6)
\[ z_{ij} = \lambda_j\,\theta_i + \sqrt{1-\lambda_j^2}\;\varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad x^{\text{scored}}_{ij} = \operatorname{clip}_{[1,7]}\operatorname{round}\bigl(m + 1.5\,z_{ij} + \delta_j\bigr), \qquad \omega = \frac{(\sum\lambda_j)^2}{(\sum\lambda_j)^2 + \sum(1-\lambda_j^2)} \]

each respondent has a latent neophobia θ; the latent response to an item is its loading times θ plus independent noise; the scored answer is this response placed around the population mean item level, rounded and clipped to the seven categories; neophilic items are then written back in raw form as eight minus the scored answer. Omega is the reliability of the sum of the latent responses.

Careless responding

A share of respondents ignore the content: “random” ticks any category; “straight-lining” gives the same raw answer to every item. Straight-lining is particularly damaging to scales with reversed items, because the same raw answer becomes opposite scored values.

\(z\) in SD units, multiplied by 1.5 points per SD ✓
Attenuation of a correlation by measurement error(N7)
\[ r_{XY} = \rho_{\theta W}\sqrt{\alpha_X\,\alpha_Y} \]

the observed correlation between two measured scores equals the correlation between the underlying true quantities times the square root of the product of their reliabilities.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\rho_{\theta W}\)correlation of the true trait with true willingness–
\(\alpha_X, \alpha_Y\)reliabilities of the neophobia score and of the willingness measure–
Follow the derivation
  1. Classical test theory: \(X = T_X + E_X\) with uncorrelated error, reliability \(\alpha_X = \sigma_{T}^2/\sigma_X^2\).
  2. \(\operatorname{cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{cov}(T_X, T_Y)\) because the errors are uncorrelated with everything.
  3. \(r_{XY} = \operatorname{cov}(T_X,T_Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y) = \rho_{T_XT_Y}\,\sigma_{T_X}\sigma_{T_Y}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y) = \rho\sqrt{\alpha_X\alpha_Y}\) (Spearman, 1904).
  4. Example: ρ = −0.5, α = 0.64 → \(r = -0.40\). A panel measured with an unreliable scale underestimates how strongly neophobia matters.
correlations and reliabilities are dimensionless ✓
Probability of tasting given a neophobia score(N8)
\[ W = \rho\,\theta + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\eta, \quad \text{taste} \iff W > c = \Phi^{-1}(1-p_0), \qquad P(\text{taste}\mid X) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\rho\sqrt{\alpha}\,z_X - c}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2\alpha}}\right) \]

willingness is partly the neophobia trait and partly independent influences; a person tastes when willingness exceeds a threshold that gives the overall share of tasters. Given only the observed score, the best guess of the trait is the square root of alpha times the standardised score, and the probability of tasting follows from the normal distribution.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(p_0\)overall share of people willing to taste–
\(z_X\)standardised observed score \((X - \bar X)/s_X\)–
\(\rho, \alpha\)trait–willingness correlation (negative); reliability of the score–
Follow the derivation
  1. In classical test theory with standardised variables, \(\operatorname{corr}(X, \theta) = \sqrt{\alpha}\), so \(E[\theta\mid X] = \sqrt{\alpha}\,z_X\) and \(\operatorname{var}(\theta\mid X) = 1-\alpha\).
  2. Then \(W\mid X\) is normal with mean \(\rho\sqrt\alpha\,z_X\) and variance \(\rho^2(1-\alpha) + 1-\rho^2 = 1-\rho^2\alpha\).
  3. \(P(W > c\mid X) = \Phi\bigl((\rho\sqrt\alpha z_X - c)/\sqrt{1-\rho^2\alpha}\bigr)\) — a probit model in the observed score.
Assumptions and validity

A deliberately simple latent-variable model for teaching; real willingness also depends on the product, its presentation, social norms and disgust (Lesson 13.1). The default ρ = −0.5 lies within the correlations reported by Evans et al. (2010).

Investigation tasks

  1. Score yourself

    Complete both scales. Report your FNS and FTNS with their percentiles against a Swedish and a Finnish reference (FNS) and the Norwegian reference (FTNS). Explain why the percentiles differ although your answers did not change.

  2. The reverse-coding trap

    Switch on Forget to reverse-code. What happens to α, to the item–total correlations of the R items and to the willingness chart? Write two sentences you could put in a methods section to show that reverse items were handled correctly.

  3. How many respondents?

    With the default loading, record α and its Feldt interval for n = 20, 60, 200 and 1000, and run “1000 replicate surveys” for n = 20. How many respondents are needed to know α within ±0.05? Compare with the mini-calculator of Eq. N5.

  4. Careless answers

    Add 15 % careless respondents, first as random ticks, then as straight-lining. Which lowers α more for the FNS, and why do straight-liners hurt scales with reversed items in particular?

  5. Attenuation

    Set ρ = −0.5. Compare the observed correlation between score and willingness for item loadings of 0.35 and 0.8. Use Eq. N7 to explain the difference, and discuss what it means for the neophobia–liking correlation in your own (small) panel.

  6. Your panel

    Include the FNS (and, for technology-based products, the FTNS) in your project questionnaire, to be answered before tasting. Paste the raw answers into the box below the readouts and choose My panel (pasted). Report the mean, SD and α (with its Feldt interval) of your panel, compare the mean with a published reference (the readout gives a one-sample t-test), and use the scores as a covariate in the hedonic analyser.

Model assumptions

  • Items and scoring follow Pliner and Hobden (1992) for the FNS and Cox and Evans (2008) for the FTNS (English wording; translations must be validated separately). Your answers are stored only in this browser (key ffp-neophobia-v1).
  • Reference populations are summarised by normal curves from published means and SDs: FNS values as compiled by Szakály et al. (2021) from the studies named in the menu (Koivisto & Sjödén, 1996; Tuorila et al., 2001; Pliner & Hobden, 1992; Olabi et al., 2009; Fernández-Ruiz et al., 2013; Choe & Cho, 2011; Previato & Behrens, 2015; Zhao et al., 2020); FTNS values converted from item means (× 13) reported by Almli et al. (2019). Samples differ in age, education and recruitment, so the comparison is indicative only.
  • Survey simulation: ordinal one-factor model (Eq. N6) with item loadings within ±20 % of the slider value, item shifts of up to ±0.5 points, answers rounded to 1–7; careless respondents answer at random or straight-line (half of them choosing the midpoint). The simulated scores are illustrations, not norms.
  • Willingness model (Eq. N8): bivariate normal latent trait and willingness with correlation ρ, a threshold for tasting, and a 7-point willingness rating for the observed correlation. The default ρ = −0.5 is chosen within the range reported by Evans et al. (2010).
  • Cronbach's α is computed with cronbachAlpha from the course statistics library; the Feldt interval uses the F quantiles of the same library. Pasted panel data are analysed in the browser only; the comparison of a panel mean with a reference uses a one-sample t-test and assumes that the reference mean is known exactly.
