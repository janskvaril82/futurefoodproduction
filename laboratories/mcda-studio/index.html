Multi-criteria decision studio

Beef or mycoprotein, peas or insects, a heated greenhouse or a vertical farm? No single number decides. Build the decision the way an analyst does: choose the criteria, normalise them, elicit weights by points, by swings or by pairwise comparisons with a consistency check, aggregate with the weighted sum, TOPSIS or the weighted product — and then find out how much of the ranking survives when the weights, the method or the data change.

    A guided tour of the decision board

    The stage is a decision board. On the left, each vertical axis is a criterion; its thickness and the coloured bar at the top show its weight. Each coloured line is an alternative, plotted at its normalised score on every criterion — 1 at the top means best in the set, 0 at the bottom worst. On the right, the alternatives are ranked by their aggregated score; the ranking re-sorts itself smoothly whenever you change anything. Hover over a line or a bar to read the raw values.

    1. Start with the protein sources and equal weights. Beef's line runs along the bottom on the environmental axes but high on acceptance, nutrition and readiness; pea protein and mycoprotein are high almost everywhere. Read the ranking and the readout “Lead over runner-up”.
    2. Be a stakeholder. Press Environmentalist, Economist, Consumer and Farmer under Stakeholder weights. Which alternatives stay near the top whatever the weights? Which move? This is the core message of MCDA: value judgements are visible, and some conclusions do not depend on them.
    3. Change the normalisation. Switch between min–max, target and vector. Min–max stretches every criterion to the full 0–1 range, so a criterion on which all options are nearly equal still separates them; target normalisation asks “is it good enough?”; vector normalisation keeps proportions. The ranking can change even though no value did.
    4. Reproduce Lesson 15.4. Load the method preset Lesson 15.4 example: the lettuce systems with the swing weights of Worked example 2. The weighted sum gives C 0.92 > D 0.60 ≈ A 0.59 > B 0.41. Switch to TOPSIS (the TOPSIS check preset): A and D swap. Then untick option B in the matrix — the dominated gas greenhouse — and watch the order of A and D reverse again (rank reversal).
    5. Try the AHP. Choose AHP matrix as weighting method and change one judgement in the matrix card so that it contradicts the others: the consistency ratio rises above 0.10, and the studio names the most inconsistent judgement.
    6. Stress-test. Look at the rank-acceptability table: under uniform weights (SMAA), how often is the leader first? Switch Monte Carlo weights between Uniform and Around mine, and add data uncertainty. A recommendation is only as strong as its acceptability.
    Key idea

    MCDA does not remove value judgements — it makes them explicit and testable. A good analysis reports three things: the ranking under stated weights, the weights at which it changes (sensitivity), and how often each alternative wins across plausible weights (acceptability).

    From performance matrix to recommendation

    Every MCDA starts with a performance matrix: alternatives in rows, criteria in columns, each cell a value in the criterion's own unit (Lesson 15.4). The studio then follows the standard steps — normalise, weight, aggregate, test robustness — and lets you change each choice independently.

    The two data sets

    Protein sources compares six ways to deliver 100 g of protein. The three life-cycle criteria come from peer-reviewed or industry LCAs: beef, chicken and peas from the global meta-analysis of Poore and Nemecek (2018); mycoprotein from a Carbon Trust study commissioned by its producer (2023); mealworms from Oonincx and de Boer (2012); cultivated meat from the ex-ante LCA of Sinke et al. (2023) for 2030 with renewable energy at the facility. The other six criteria have no natural unit; they are scored on constructed scales (1–5, or TRL 1–9) whose anchors are given in the matrix notes. Those scores are the course team's illustrative judgements for 2026 — replace them with your own evidence, or with scores from a stakeholder workshop. The badges in the matrix show the quality of every value; a matrix that mixes a peer-reviewed global mean, an industry report and a judgement must say so.

    Lettuce systems is Table 1 of Lesson 15.4 — four ways to supply a Stockholm retailer in January — so that you can reproduce every worked example of the lesson in the studio.

    Comparability of LCA data

    Values from different studies differ in functional unit, system boundary, year and impact method: Poore and Nemecek report global means from farm to retail; the Carbon Trust study stops at the factory gate; Sinke et al. model a future (2030) system. Before trusting a small difference, check that the studies are comparable — and use the data-uncertainty option of the Monte Carlo analysis to see whether the ranking survives.

    Normalisation changes the question

    Min–max normalisation measures each option against the best and worst in the set; it is the natural partner of swing weights and the weighted sum. Target normalisation measures against an external target — a planetary-boundary share, a policy goal — and stops rewarding once the target is met. Vector normalisation divides by the length of the criterion column, keeps ratios between options and is the classical choice for TOPSIS (Hwang & Yoon, 1981). Because min–max depends on the extremes of the set, adding or removing an option can reverse the ranking of two others (Lesson 15.4, Worked example 3).

    Weights are trade-offs across ranges

    Direct points answer “how important is this criterion?”. Swing weighting asks the question the weighted sum actually needs: how valuable is moving each criterion from its worst to its best value in this set (von Winterfeldt & Edwards, 1986)? The studio shows each swing next to its slider. The analytic hierarchy process derives weights from pairwise comparisons as the principal eigenvector of the comparison matrix and checks the judgements with Saaty's consistency ratio; CR ≤ 0.10 is the usual acceptance threshold (Saaty, 1980).

    Compensation and the choice of aggregation

    The weighted sum is fully compensatory: a very poor score can be bought back by good scores elsewhere. TOPSIS ranks by relative closeness to an ideal and distance from an anti-ideal point; its Euclidean distance makes large deviations count more. The weighted product multiplies ratio-normalised scores, so a near-zero score on one criterion drags the whole product down — a strong-sustainability reading in which critical natural capital cannot be substituted.

    Common misconception

    “The MCDA proved that alternative X is best.” An MCDA shows which alternative is best under stated weights, data and method. If the leader's first-rank acceptability is 55 % under plausible weights, the honest conclusion is that the choice depends on values that stakeholders must negotiate — not that the analysis failed.

    Frontier 2024–2026

    Assessments of emerging food technologies increasingly combine prospective LCA, techno-economic analysis and social indicators in one MCDA, and use stochastic methods (SMAA) to report acceptabilities instead of single rankings. Reviews of vertical farming call for exactly this integration of environmental, economic and social criteria (Dziumla et al., 2025).

    The equations behind the studio

    Alternatives are indexed \(i = 1\dots m\), criteria \(j = 1\dots n\); \(x_{ij}\) is the performance of alternative \(i\) on criterion \(j\), and \(v_{ij}\) its normalised, benefit-oriented score (higher is better).

    Min–max normalisation(M1)
    \[ v_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}-\min_i x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij}-\min_i x_{ij}}\ \ \text{(benefit)}, \qquad v_{ij} = \frac{\max_i x_{ij}-x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij}-\min_i x_{ij}}\ \ \text{(cost)} \]

    the score is the distance from the worst value in the set divided by the range of the set; the best option gets 1 and the worst 0.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(x_{ij}\)performance of alternative \(i\) on criterion \(j\)criterion unit
    \(v_{ij}\)normalised score, 0 (worst in set) to 1 (best)–
    Follow the derivation
    1. Look for a linear map \(v = \alpha x + \beta\) with \(v = 1\) at the best and \(v = 0\) at the worst value; solving the two conditions gives the expressions above (Lesson 15.4, Eq. 15.4.1).
    2. Protein example (GHG, cost criterion): range 0.44–49.89 kg CO₂e per 100 g protein; chicken \((49.89-5.70)/(49.89-0.44) = 0.894\).
    3. If all values are equal the criterion cannot discriminate; the studio then gives every option 1.
    Assumptions and validity

    Interval scale defined by the option set: adding or removing an option that sets an extreme changes every score in the column and can reverse rankings.

    unit / unit = dimensionless ✓
    Target normalisation(M2)
    \[ v_{ij} = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{t_j}{x_{ij}}\right)\ \ \text{(cost)}, \qquad v_{ij} = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{x_{ij}}{t_j}\right)\ \ \text{(benefit)} \]

    the score is the target divided by the value for a cost criterion, or the value divided by the target for a benefit criterion, capped at one when the target is met.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(t_j\)external target for criterion \(j\) (editable in the target row of the matrix)criterion unit
    Follow the derivation
    1. Divide by an external reference so that the scale does not depend on the option set (Lesson 15.4, Eq. 15.4.2).
    2. The ratio rewards improvement in proportion (halving emissions doubles the score) until the target is reached: “good enough” is a deliberate non-linearity.
    3. Example: a climate target of 0.5 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ gives the vertical farm (0.84) \(0.5/0.84 = 0.60\).
    Assumptions and validity

    The default targets in the studio are illustrative. Science-based targets — a per-capita share of a planetary boundary or a national climate budget — are more defensible than round numbers.

    unit / unit = dimensionless ✓
    Vector normalisation(M3)
    \[ r_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\sqrt{\sum_{k} x_{kj}^{2}}}, \qquad v_{ij} = r_{ij}\ \text{(benefit)}, \qquad v_{ij} = 1 - r_{ij}\ \text{(cost, weighted sum only)} \]

    each value is divided by the Euclidean length of its criterion column; for the weighted sum, cost criteria are turned into benefits by subtracting from one, whereas TOPSIS keeps the raw direction and picks the minimum as ideal.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(r_{ij}\)vector-normalised value: each column becomes a unit vector–
    Follow the derivation
    1. Treat the column \((x_{1j},\dots,x_{mj})\) as a vector and divide by its length; ratios between options are preserved.
    2. Unlike min–max, no option is forced to 0 or 1, so small differences stay small — which is why vector and min–max normalisation can rank the same data differently.
    unit / unit = dimensionless ✓
    Direct and swing weights(M4)
    \[ w_j = \frac{s_j}{\sum_{k} s_k}, \qquad \sum_j w_j = 1 \]

    each weight is the points given to its criterion divided by the total points of all included criteria.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(s_j\)points for criterion \(j\): importance (direct) or value of its worst-to-best swing (swing)points
    \(w_j\)normalised weight–
    Follow the derivation
    1. Swing elicitation: imagine an option that is worst on every criterion; choose the criterion whose improvement from worst to best you value most and give it 100; rate the other swings relative to it.
    2. Normalise to sum 1. Lesson 15.4, Worked example 2: points (100, 30, 40, 80, 50) → weights (0.333, 0.100, 0.133, 0.267, 0.167).
    3. Excluding a criterion re-normalises the others — its points no longer count.
    Assumptions and validity

    Swing weights are only valid for the ranges they were elicited on: if the option set changes the ranges, ask again (range sensitivity).

    points / points = dimensionless ✓
    AHP weights: the principal eigenvector(M5)
    \[ A\,\mathbf w = \lambda_{\max}\mathbf w, \qquad \mathbf w^{(k+1)} = \frac{A\,\mathbf w^{(k)}}{\sum_i\big(A\,\mathbf w^{(k)}\big)_i} \]

    the weight vector is the principal eigenvector of the pairwise matrix; the studio finds it by power iteration — multiplying a starting vector by the matrix and rescaling it to sum one until it stops changing.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(A = [a_{ij}]\)pairwise matrix, \(a_{ij}\) = importance of \(i\) over \(j\) on the 1–9 scale, \(a_{ji} = 1/a_{ij}\)–
    \(\lambda_{\max}\)principal eigenvalue, \(\ge n\)–
    Follow the derivation
    1. If judgements are perfectly consistent, \(a_{ij} = w_i/w_j\) and \(A\mathbf w = n\mathbf w\): the weights are an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(n\) (Saaty, 1977).
    2. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem a positive matrix has a unique positive principal eigenvector; repeated multiplication amplifies its component fastest, so power iteration converges to it.
    3. Lesson 15.4, Figure 3 defaults (climate ≻ land 3, ≻ water 3, = cost, ≻ security 2, …) give about (0.32, 0.09, 0.14, 0.29, 0.15).
    Assumptions and validity

    Pairwise questions without reference to ranges share the weakness of direct rating; adding an alternative can reverse AHP rankings when AHP is also used to score alternatives (Belton & Gear, 1983). The studio uses AHP for criterion weights only.

    ratios of importance: dimensionless ✓
    Consistency index and ratio(M6)
    \[ \lambda_{\max} = \frac1n\sum_i\frac{(A\mathbf w)_i}{w_i}, \qquad \text{CI} = \frac{\lambda_{\max}-n}{n-1}, \qquad \text{CR} = \frac{\text{CI}}{\text{RI}_n} \]

    the largest eigenvalue is estimated as the average ratio of A w to w; the consistency index is its excess over n divided by n − 1; the consistency ratio divides by the average index of random matrices of the same size.

    \(n\)345678910
    \(\text{RI}_n\) (Saaty, 1980)0.580.901.121.241.321.411.451.49
    Follow the derivation
    1. The trace of \(A\) equals \(n\) and the sum of its eigenvalues; inconsistency pushes \(\lambda_{\max}\) above \(n\), so \(\text{CI}\) is zero only for perfect consistency.
    2. Dividing by the random index compares your matrix with randomly filled ones; CR ≤ 0.10 is accepted.
    3. The studio also finds the judgement that deviates most from the ratio implied by the weights, \(\max|\ln(a_{ij}w_j/w_i)|\), and proposes the Saaty value closest to \(w_i/w_j\).
    dimensionless ✓
    Weighted sum(M7)
    \[ V_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\,v_{ij} \]

    the overall value of an alternative is the weighted sum of its normalised scores.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(V_i\)overall value, 0–1–
    Follow the derivation
    1. Multi-attribute value theory: with mutually preferentially independent criteria, preferences can be represented by an additive value function (Keeney & Raiffa, 1993).
    2. Lesson 15.4, Worked example 3: option C = \(0.333(1)+0.100(0.927)+0.133(0.927)+0.267(0.763)+0.167(1) = 0.920\).
    Assumptions and validity

    Full compensation: a poor score is offset by good scores elsewhere. Use swing weights with min–max scores.

    TOPSIS closeness coefficient(M8)
    \[ u_{ij} = w_j r_{ij}, \quad D_i^{\pm} = \sqrt{\sum_j\big(u_{ij}-u_j^{\pm}\big)^2}, \quad C_i = \frac{D_i^{-}}{D_i^{+}+D_i^{-}} \]

    weighted normalised values place every alternative in criterion space; its closeness is its distance from the anti-ideal point divided by the sum of its distances from the ideal and the anti-ideal.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(u_j^{+}, u_j^{-}\)ideal and anti-ideal: best and worst weighted value of criterion \(j\) (min for cost criteria with vector normalisation)–
    \(C_i\)closeness: 1 at the ideal, 0 at the anti-ideal–
    Follow the derivation
    1. Hwang and Yoon (1981): the best alternative should be near the ideal and far from the anti-ideal; the ratio balances both.
    2. With vector normalisation the studio uses the classical form (Lesson 15.4, Eq. 15.4.6); with min–max or target scores, which are already benefit-oriented, the ideal is the maximum of every column.
    3. Worked example 3 of Lesson 15.4 (swing weights, vector): C 0.878 > A 0.626 > D 0.586 > B 0.445.
    dimensionless ✓
    Rank acceptability and weight perturbation(M9)
    \[ b_i^{r} = \frac1N\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\!\left[\text{rank}_i(\mathbf w^{(k)}) = r\right], \qquad w_j^{(k)} = \frac{E_j}{\sum_l E_l},\ \ E_j = \begin{cases} -\ln U_j & \text{uniform (SMAA)}\\ w_j\,e^{\sigma Z_j} & \text{around mine}\end{cases} \]

    the acceptability of rank r for alternative i is the share of N weight vectors under which it attains that rank; uniform weight vectors come from normalised exponential random numbers, perturbed ones from your weights times lognormal factors.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(U_j, Z_j\)uniform (0, 1) and standard normal random numbers–
    \(\sigma\)spread of ln(weight) around your weights–
    \(b_i^{r}\)rank acceptability index (Lahdelma et al., 1998)–
    Follow the derivation
    1. Normalised independent exponential numbers are Dirichlet(1,…,1): uniform over all weight vectors (Lesson 15.4, Eq. 15.4.9).
    2. Around your weights, \(\sigma = 0.4\) means that a weight typically varies by a factor \(e^{0.4}\approx1.5\) before renormalisation.
    3. Each \(b_i^r\) is a binomial proportion with standard error \(\sqrt{b(1-b)/N}\).
    4. One-at-a-time sensitivity (the sensitivity chart) varies one weight from 0 to 1 and rescales the others in proportion; lines cross at the break-even weights of Lesson 15.4, Eq. 15.4.8.
    dimensionless proportions ✓

    \[ P_i = \prod_{j} v_{ij}^{\,w_j}, \qquad v_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij}}\ \text{(benefit)}, \quad v_{ij} = \frac{\min_i x_{ij}}{x_{ij}}\ \text{(cost)} \]

    the score is the product of ratio-normalised scores, each raised to its weight — a weighted geometric mean.

    SymbolMeaningUnit
    \(P_i\)weighted-product score–
    Follow the derivation
    1. \(\ln P_i = \sum_j w_j \ln v_{ij}\): because \(\ln v\to-\infty\) as \(v\to0\), a near-zero score cannot be compensated (Lesson 15.4, Eq. 15.4.7).
    2. Ratio normalisation keeps orders of magnitude: beef's land score is \(1.52/163.6 = 0.009\) with mycoprotein's land use as the best in the set.

    Investigation tasks

    Use the studio; save each analysis with Save investigation. The page link keeps the method, your weights and any edits to the matrix.

    1. Reproduce the lesson

      With the method preset Lesson 15.4 example, check the weighted-sum scores of Worked example 3 and the TOPSIS scores. Untick option B and explain, with Eq. M1, why the order of A and D reverses although neither changed.

    2. Whose protein?

      For the protein sources, record the top three under each stakeholder profile and under equal weights. Which alternative is in the top three for every stakeholder? Which conclusion would you present to a municipal procurement officer, and which uncertainty would you flag?

    3. How robust is the winner?

      With your preferred weights, compare the first-rank acceptability of the leader under Around mine (σ = 0.2, 0.4, 0.8) and under uniform weights. Then add data uncertainty. At what point would you stop calling the leader “the best option”?

    4. Consistent judgements

      Choose AHP matrix and fill the matrix for the protein criteria from your own judgement, criterion pair by criterion pair. Is your CR below 0.10? Use Repair the most inconsistent judgement once and compare the weights with your direct points.

    5. Compensation

      Compare the weighted sum and the weighted product for the protein sources with equal weights. Which alternative gains most under the product, and which criterion causes that? Relate the result to strong and weak sustainability (Lesson 15.4).

    6. Your own decision

      Paste the performance matrix of your course project (or of a decision in your municipality) into Your own decision matrix. Document the source and quality of each value, choose and justify a normalisation and a weighting method, and report the ranking with its acceptability.

    Model assumptions

    • Protein data set (per 100 g protein): GHG, land and freshwater of beef (beef herd), chicken and peas are the global means of Poore & Nemecek (2018) via Our World in Data, as used in Lessons 1.3 and 12.1. Mycoprotein: 0.53 kg CO₂e, 0.000167 ha and 31 L blue water per kg (Carbon Trust, 2023; cradle to processing gate; commissioned by the producer), converted with 11 g protein per 100 g (44 % protein in dry matter, Lesson 12.3, and about 25 % dry matter). Mealworm: 14 kg CO₂e and 18 m² per kg edible protein (Oonincx & de Boer, 2012). Cultivated meat: 4.0 kg CO₂e per kg with renewable energy at the facility (Sinke et al., 2023), 21.5 % protein (their assumed 18–25 %); its land use is derived from Sinke et al.'s finding that it converts crops into meat almost three times more efficiently than chicken. Freshwater of mealworm (190 L) and cultivated meat (450 L) are estimates without direct data.
    • Constructed scales (cost, nutrition, acceptance, animal welfare, resilience; TRL) are illustrative judgements for 2026 with the anchors given in the matrix notes; they are the place where your own evidence or stakeholder scores belong.
    • Lettuce data set: Table 1 of Lesson 15.4 (teaching values from Lessons 15.1–15.3).
    • Normalisation: min–max (M1), target with editable illustrative targets (M2), vector (M3; for the weighted sum, cost criteria are converted with 1 − r). The weighted product always uses ratio normalisation (M10). TOPSIS uses the classical vector form when vector normalisation is chosen, otherwise the benefit-oriented scores of M1 or M2.
    • AHP: principal eigenvector by power iteration (200 iterations or convergence to 10⁻¹²); random indices of Saaty (1980); “repair” replaces the most inconsistent judgement by the Saaty value closest to \(w_i/w_j\).
    • Monte Carlo: uniform weights (Dirichlet(1,…,1)) or your weights × lognormal factors with spread σ; optional data uncertainty multiplies every value by a lognormal factor with geometric standard deviation 1.2 (as in Lesson 15.4, Figure 5). Ties are broken by the order of the alternatives.
    • Stakeholder weight profiles are illustrative stereotypes for discussion, not survey results.
