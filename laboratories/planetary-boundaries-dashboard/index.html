Planetary boundaries dashboard

It is 2050 and between 8.5 and 10 billion people need to eat. Will the food system still fit inside the safe operating space of the planet? This dashboard runs the published scenario grid of Springmann and colleagues (2018) — the analysis behind the EAT–Lancet Commission — and lets you pull the three big levers yourself: what we eat, how we produce it and how much we waste. Two optional levers, alternative proteins and vertical farming, show what new technologies might add — and what they cost in electricity. Every petal of the flower is one environmental pressure; the dashed green circle is its planetary boundary.

A guided tour of the flower

  1. Start with business as usual. Press the BAU 2050 preset. Five petals grow far beyond the dashed boundary circle: greenhouse-gas emissions double compared with 2010, cropland grows by two thirds and nitrogen application by half. The dotted white arcs show 2010 — even then, cropland, nitrogen and phosphorus were already at or beyond their boundaries.
  2. Pull one lever at a time. Try Diet only, Tech only and Waste only. Notice that each lever is good at different things: diets cut greenhouse gases, technology cuts cropland, water and fertiliser, and less waste helps a little everywhere. No single lever brings all five petals inside the circle.
  3. Combine them. All combined (medium) still leaves greenhouse gases, nitrogen and phosphorus in the red. All combined (high) — flexitarian diets, high-ambition technology and waste reduced to a quarter — brings every petal inside the safe operating space. Look at the waterfall chart to see how much each lever contributes.
  4. Change the world. Switch the socio-economic pathway between SSP1, SSP2 and SSP3 and move the population slider. More people with the same per-capita habits means proportionally larger petals; richer pathways eat more animal products.
  5. Try the techno-fix. The Techno-fix preset replaces 30 % of animal products with alternative proteins and grows 20 % of vegetables in vertical farms, without changing diets. Watch the yellow “CEA electricity” band appear on the greenhouse-gas petal and the electricity readout climb into thousands of terawatt-hours.
  6. Zoom out to the whole Earth. Set the flower to Earth system 2023. This is the planetary-boundary assessment of Richardson et al. (2023): six of nine boundaries transgressed. The yellow dots mark the processes that agriculture is a major driver of.
Key idea

The food system can only be brought back inside the planetary boundaries by a combination of dietary change, better technology and management, and less food loss and waste. Each lever acts on different pressures, so they are complements rather than alternatives.

Planetary boundaries and the food system

The planetary boundaries framework (Rockström et al., 2009; Steffen et al., 2015; Richardson et al., 2023) identifies nine Earth-system processes that together keep the planet in the relatively stable state of the Holocene — the 12 000 years in which agriculture and civilisation developed. For each process a control variable is chosen (for example the atmospheric CO₂ concentration or the amount of reactive nitrogen fixed by industry) and a boundary is placed at the safe end of the zone of uncertainty about where dangerous change begins. Beyond the boundary lies a zone of increasing risk, and beyond that a high-risk zone. The 2023 assessment found six of the nine boundaries transgressed.

Food production is the single largest human driver of several of them (Campbell et al., 2017). Agriculture occupies about 44 % of the world’s habitable land (FAO land-cover data), and the food supply chain causes about 26 % of anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions, while roughly two-thirds of freshwater withdrawals are for irrigation (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). Springmann et al. (2018) therefore translated five of the global boundaries into food-system boundaries: a budget for greenhouse-gas emissions, a maximum area of cropland, a maximum amount of irrigation water, and maximum applications of nitrogen and phosphorus fertiliser.

The scenario grid behind the dashboard

Springmann and colleagues coupled the IMPACT global agricultural-market model with commodity- and country-specific environmental footprints to project the five pressures to 2050 for three socio-economic pathways (SSP1–3). They then combined three variants of each lever — 3 diets × 3 technology levels × 3 loss-and-waste levels — giving 27 scenarios per pathway, and published the results for every country. The dashboard uses the world totals of all 81 published combinations and interpolates between them when you choose intermediate slider positions.

Why is the GHG boundary only about methane and nitrous oxide?

Following IPCC accounting, the food-system budget of 4.7 Gt CO₂e yr⁻¹ covers the non-CO₂ gases that agriculture emits biologically (enteric and rice methane, manure and fertiliser nitrous oxide). CO₂ from land-use change must fall to zero under the land boundary, and CO₂ from energy belongs to the energy sector’s budget. That is why electricity for vertical farms is shown as a separate, switchable band: it is real warming, but under the original framework it would be charged to the power sector.

The optional what-if levers

Alternative proteins. A share of all animal-product consumption is replaced by alternatives (plant-based analogues, mycoprotein, precision-fermentation or cultivated products). The pressures of the replaced animal products disappear, and a residual fraction reappears as the alternatives’ own footprint. A diet-optimisation study for Europe found that replacing animal-source foods with novel or plant-based foods reduced global warming potential, water use and land use by more than 80 % (Mazac et al., 2022), which motivates the default residual of 20 %. For nitrogen and phosphorus the same fraction is assumed — a simplification you should question.

Controlled-environment agriculture (CEA). A share of vegetables is moved from fields into plant factories. Their share of the fruit-and-vegetable cropland, water and fertiliser is cut by an adjustable saving, but every kilogram now needs electricity for lighting and climate control. The resulting CO₂ is electricity use × grid carbon intensity. The defaults (10 kWh kg⁻¹, 80 % saving) are illustrative round numbers, not literature values — explore how sensitive the result is (see Graamans et al., 2018, for a careful comparison of plant factories and greenhouses).

Worked exampleIs doubling emissions really “BAU”?

In 2010 food-system GHG emissions were 5.22 Gt CO₂e. Under SSP2 business as usual they reach 9.78 Gt CO₂e in 2050 while the population grows from 6.88 to 9.19 billion. How much of the increase is explained by population alone?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Population effect. With 2010 per-capita emissions, 2050 emissions would be \(5.22 \times 9.19/6.88 = 6.97\) Gt CO₂e.
  2. Total increase. \(9.78 - 5.22 = 4.56\) Gt CO₂e, of which \(6.97 - 5.22 = 1.75\) Gt (38 %) is due to more people.
  3. The rest (2.81 Gt, 62 %) comes from rising per-capita consumption of animal products as incomes grow — the “nutrition transition”.
Population explains ≈ 38 % of the BAU increase; changing diets explain ≈ 62 %.
Frontier 2025–2026

The second EAT–Lancet Commission (Rockström et al., 2025) updated the food-system boundaries, including tighter nitrogen and phosphorus limits — now defined as a nitrogen surplus of 57 Tg N yr⁻¹ and a phosphorus loss to surface water of 4.6 Tg P yr⁻¹ rather than as fertiliser application — and a freshwater limit of 2000 km³ yr⁻¹, as tabulated by van Dooren et al. (2026). The dashboard keeps the 2018/2019 values because the scenario grid was built for them — try mentally moving the circles inward.

The equations behind the dashboard

Normalised pressure and boundary status(PB1)
\[ x_k = \frac{P_k}{B_k}, \qquad \text{status}_k = \begin{cases} 1 & P_k < B_k^{\text{low}} \\ 2 & B_k^{\text{low}} \le P_k \le B_k \\ 3 & B_k < P_k \le B_k^{\text{high}} \\ 4 & P_k > B_k^{\text{high}} \end{cases} \]

the length of each petal is the pressure divided by its boundary, so the boundary always sits at 1; the colour tells whether the pressure is below the uncertainty range, below the central value, inside the range, or beyond it.

Assumptions and validity

The categories reproduce those of Springmann et al. (2018, Fig. 3). The EAT–Lancet set has no ranges in the dashboard, so only “within” (≤ 1) and “beyond” (> 1) are distinguished. A single global number hides regional overshoot: nitrogen can be far too high in China and far too low in sub-Saharan Africa at the same time.

\([x_k] = \dfrac{\text{Gt CO}_2\text{e yr}^{-1}}{\text{Gt CO}_2\text{e yr}^{-1}} = 1\) — dimensionless, so all five pressures can share one flower. ✓
Multilinear interpolation in the scenario grid(PB2)
\[ P_k(s,t,w) = \sum_{d\in\{0,1\}} \sum_{i\in\{i_0,i_0+1\}} \sum_{j\in\{j_0,j_0+1\}} \omega_d(s)\,\omega_i(t)\,\omega_j(w)\; P_k^{\text{grid}}(d,i,j) \] \[ \omega_1(s) = s,\quad \omega_0(s)=1-s,\qquad \omega_{i_0+1}(t) = \frac{t-t_{i_0}}{t_{i_0+1}-t_{i_0}},\quad \omega_{i_0}(t) = 1-\omega_{i_0+1}(t) \]

a pressure at intermediate lever settings is a weighted average of the eight published grid scenarios that surround the chosen point; each weight is the product of how far along each lever axis the point lies.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(s\)share of the population adopting the target diet (0–1)–
\(t\)technology ambition; grid nodes \(t\in\{0, 0.5, 1\}\) = BAU, TECH, TECH+–
\(w\)loss-and-waste reduction; nodes \(w\in\{0, 0.5, 0.75\}\)–
\(P_k^{\text{grid}}(d,i,j)\)published world total for diet \(d\), technology node \(i\), waste node \(j\)varies
Follow the derivation
  1. Along the diet axis the interpolation is exact if the world is a mixture of two groups: a share \(s\) eating the target diet and \(1-s\) eating BAU. Pressures are sums over consumers, so \(P = sP_{\text{target}} + (1-s)P_{\text{BAU}}\).
  2. Along the technology and waste axes the published scenarios are discrete packages. Linear interpolation assumes that implementing a fraction of a package delivers the same fraction of its effect — a first-order approximation.
  3. Applying 1-D linear interpolation along each of the three axes in turn gives the trilinear formula with the product weights \(\omega_d\omega_i\omega_j\), which sum to 1.
  4. At grid nodes the weights collapse to a single scenario, so the dashboard reproduces the published values exactly (e.g. BAU 2050, SSP2: 9.78 Gt CO₂e).
Assumptions and validity

Interactions between levers (for example, higher yields matter less when less food is needed) are captured at the grid nodes but only linearly between them. Outside the grid (e.g. waste reduction beyond 75 %) the model does not extrapolate.

Weights are dimensionless and sum to one, so \(P_k\) keeps the unit of the grid values. ✓

Replacing animal products by alternatives(PB4)
\[ P_k' = P_k - a\,(1-\rho)\,P_k^{\text{anim}} \]

replacing a share of animal products removes that share of their pressure, but the alternatives bring back a residual fraction of it, so the net saving is the share times one minus the residual.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a\)share of animal-product consumption replaced–

Assumptions and validity

The substitution is assumed nutritionally equivalent and to scale linearly. \(\rho=0.2\) reflects the > 80 % reductions reported for GWP, land and water by Mazac et al. (2022); applying it to nitrogen and phosphorus, and ignoring the energy used by bioreactors, are simplifications.

\(a\) and \(\rho\) are dimensionless, so \(P_k'\) has the unit of \(P_k\). ✓
Vertical farming: resources saved, electricity added(PB5)
\[ \Delta P_k = -\,c\,\phi_{\text{veg}}\,\sigma\,P_k^{\text{fv}}, \qquad E = c\,m_{\text{veg}}\,N\cdot 365\;e, \qquad \text{CO}_2 = E\,I_{\text{grid}} \]

moving a share of vegetables into plant factories saves that share of the vegetables’ part of the fruit-and-vegetable pressures, scaled by how much the factory saves per kilogram; the electricity needed is the vegetable mass grown indoors times the electricity per kilogram, and its CO₂ is that electricity times the grid’s carbon intensity.

\(c\)share of vegetables grown in CEA–0
\(\phi_{\text{veg}}\)vegetables’ share of fruit + vegetable mass in the diet–0.64 (BAU)
\(\sigma\)saving of cropland, water, N, P and field emissions per kg–0.8

Follow the derivation
  1. Springmann et al. report one “fruits & vegetables” group. Assuming pressures scale with mass, vegetables cause the share \(\phi_{\text{veg}} = m_{\text{veg}}/(m_{\text{veg}}+m_{\text{fruit}})\); for BAU 2050, \(327.8/(327.8+180.4) = 0.645\).
  2. World vegetable consumption is \(m_{\text{veg}} N \cdot 365\): \(0.328\times 9.19\times10^9\times 365 = 1.10\times10^{12}\) kg yr⁻¹.
  3. Growing 20 % of it indoors at 10 kWh kg⁻¹ needs \(2.2\times10^{12}\) kWh = 2200 TWh yr⁻¹, about 7 % of world electricity generation in 2024 (≈ 30 800 TWh).
  4. At 0.47 kg CO₂ kWh⁻¹ this emits \(1.0\times10^{12}\) kg = 1.0 Gt CO₂ yr⁻¹ — more than a fifth of the whole food-system GHG budget.
Assumptions and validity

Consumption rather than production mass is used (losses would add more); fruit and staples are assumed unsuitable for plant factories; embodied emissions of buildings are ignored. The defaults are illustrative, not measured values.

\(\text{kg d}^{-1}\cdot\text{d yr}^{-1}\cdot\text{kWh kg}^{-1}\cdot\text{kg CO}_2\,\text{kWh}^{-1} = \text{kg CO}_2\,\text{yr}^{-1}\) per person; × N gives the world total. ✓
Fair attribution of the reduction to each lever(PB6)
\[ \varphi_i = \sum_{S\subseteq L\setminus\{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(n-|S|-1)!}{n!}\,\Big[V(S\cup\{i\}) - V(S)\Big] \]

the contribution of lever i is its extra effect when it is added to a group of other levers, averaged over every possible order in which the levers could be switched on.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(L\)set of \(n = 5\) levers (waste, technology, diet, alternative proteins, CEA)–
\(V(S)\)pressure when only the levers in \(S\) are active at their chosen settingsvaries
\(\varphi_i\)Shapley contribution of lever \(i\) (negative = reduction)varies
Follow the derivation
  1. If levers acted independently, each lever’s effect would simply be \(V(\{i\}) - V(\varnothing)\). But they interact: less waste means less land to save through higher yields.
  2. The order-dependent effect of adding lever \(i\) after the levers in \(S\) is \(V(S\cup\{i\}) - V(S)\). There are \(n!\) orders; the fraction in which exactly the set \(S\) precedes \(i\) is \(|S|!(n-|S|-1)!/n!\).
  3. Averaging over orders gives \(\varphi_i\). The contributions add up exactly: \(\sum_i \varphi_i = V(L) - V(\varnothing)\) — the waterfall chart closes.
Weights are dimensionless and sum to one over \(S\); \(\varphi_i\) has the unit of the pressure. ✓
Radial scale of the Earth-system flower(PB7)
\[ q = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x-H}{PB-H} & \text{(safe zone, } q\le 1) \\[2mm] 1+\dfrac{x-PB}{U-PB} & \text{(increasing risk, } 1<q\le 2) \\[2mm] 2+\log_{10}\dfrac{x-PB}{U-PB} & \text{(high risk)} \end{cases} \]

each control variable is measured as the distance travelled from its Holocene value towards the boundary; between the boundary and the high-risk limit the scale restarts, and beyond the high-risk limit it becomes logarithmic so that nitrogen and extinction rates still fit on the page.

Assumptions and validity

The piecewise scale is a visualisation choice made for this dashboard, inspired by — but not identical to — the published figure. It works for variables that increase (CO₂) and decrease (forest cover, ozone) because the signs cancel in the ratios. Novel entities have no single control variable and are drawn hatched.

Model assumptions

  • Data. All 2010 and 2050 pressures are the world totals published by Springmann et al. (2018) in their Oxford University Research Archive data set (81 combinations of pathway, diet, technology and waste, with a split by food group). GHG covers CH₄ and N₂O from food production in CO₂-equivalents; cropland, bluewater, nitrogen and phosphorus refer to agricultural use and fertiliser application.
  • Interpolation. Intermediate lever settings are interpolated multilinearly between the published scenarios (Eq. PB2). The diet lever is a population share; technology and waste levers are treated as partial implementation of the published packages.
  • Population. Pressures scale linearly with population at constant per-capita pressures of the chosen pathway (Eq. PB3).
  • Alternative proteins (illustrative). A share of animal-product pressures is replaced with a residual fraction ρ for all five pressures (default 0.2, motivated by Mazac et al., 2022); bioreactor energy and nutritional equivalence are not modelled.
  • CEA vegetables (illustrative). Savings apply to the vegetable share of the fruit-and-vegetable group, estimated from the scenario diets’ mass shares (Springmann et al., Supplementary Table 2); electricity use (10 kWh kg⁻¹) and savings (80 %) are adjustable round-number assumptions; the world grid intensity of 0.47 kg CO₂ kWh⁻¹ is derived from Ember (2025): 14.6 Gt CO₂ from 30 800 TWh in 2024.
  • Boundaries. Springmann et al. (2018, Extended Data Table 7) with uncertainty ranges; EAT–Lancet (Willett et al., 2019) central values as tabulated by van Dooren et al. (2026). Global boundaries ignore regional distribution, which matters most for nitrogen, phosphorus and water.
  • Earth-system flower. Control-variable values from Richardson et al. (2023, Table 1); the radial scale (Eq. PB7) is our visual normalisation. “Agriculture is a major driver” follows Campbell et al. (2017).
  • The model is static: it compares 2010 with 2050 and does not simulate pathways, prices, rebound effects or feedbacks between pressures (e.g. climate change lowering yields).
