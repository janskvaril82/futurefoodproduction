Triangle test and Thurstonian d′

Three coded cups, two identical, one different: which one is odd? Here a virtual panel tastes wheat bread against bread with cricket flour. Each assessor's perceptions are drawn from a Thurstonian model, you watch the choice being made, and the correct answers pile up against the binomial distribution of pure guessing. Then turn the numbers around: how large is the difference, how many assessors would you need, and can you ever claim that two products are “the same”? What to look at first

  1. Watch one assessor decide. Press New panel with the default moderate difference (d′ = 1.5). In the top-left panel each cup's perception is drawn from its bell curve — two from A, one from B. The assessor cannot see the letters, only the three perceptions: the two closest ones are judged “the same” and the remaining cup is named odd. Often the two A perceptions are not the closest pair, and a real difference still produces a wrong answer.
  2. Watch the evidence accumulate. Every answer lights a seat in the panel. The bottom-left histogram is what the count would look like if everybody guessed: the magenta bars are the critical region. With 24 assessors you need at least 13 correct answers.
  3. Remove the difference. Apply No difference: the two bell curves coincide and about a third of the answers are still correct — by chance. Press Repeat 1000 panels: only a few per cent of the panels are significant, never more than α.
  4. Feel the sawtooth. Back at d′ = 1.5, look at the power chart. A panel of 24 detects this difference less than half of the time; the triangle test needs over 50 assessors for 80 % power, the tetrad about 16–20.
  5. Turn to your data. Choose Analyse my results (or the preset Class: 13 of 24). The readouts give the p-value, the proportion of discriminators and d′ — each with an interval. The psychometric chart shows how the uncertainty in p̂c becomes uncertainty in d′.
  6. Ask the other question. Apply Similar? 21 of 60: the difference test is not significant and the upper confidence limit of pd is below 0.30, so the products can be called similar. Try the same proportion with 24 assessors — similarity can no longer be shown.
Key idea

A triangle test answers “is there a perceptible difference?” with a binomial count. Its size — pd or d′ — needs an interval, and its absence can only be claimed with a similarity test designed for that purpose. A non-significant triangle test with 24 assessors is almost never evidence that the products taste the same.

How a triangle test works

Each assessor receives three coded samples, two of one product and one of the other, in one of the six balanced arrangements, and must name the odd sample — even if it is a guess (ISO 4120:2021). Because the answer is forced, a person who perceives nothing is right with probability \(p_0 = 1/3\). The number of correct answers in a panel of \(n\) independent assessors therefore follows a binomial distribution, and a count that would be improbable under pure guessing is evidence of a perceptible difference (Eq. T1). The count is discrete, so the actual risk of a false positive is at most α and usually below it: for \(n = 24\) the critical number is 13, and \(P(X \ge 13) = 0.028\).

Two ways to describe “how different”

The guessing model imagines a population of discriminators, who always recognise the odd sample, and non-discriminators, who guess: \(p_c = p_d + (1-p_d)/3\) (Eq. T3). It is simple, it is used in ISO 4120's tables, and it is psychologically unrealistic: perception is graded, not a switch. The Thurstonian model assumes that each tasting produces a noisy perception: A samples vary around 0, B samples around \(d'\), with a standard deviation of 1 (Thurstone, 1927). \(d'\) is the distance between the products in units of perceptual noise, and it does not depend on the test method (Ennis, 1993).

The link between the two descriptions is the psychometric function \(p_c = f(d')\), which is different for every method (Table 3 in Lesson 13.4). The triangle test has the flattest one of the common methods: the assessor must compare three noisy perceptions and does not know which attribute to attend to. The same products that give 42 % correct answers in a triangle test give 76 % in a 2-AFC test (“which one is more bitter?”), and the guessing model would translate these into 13 % and 52 % discriminators — the paradox of the discriminatory non-discriminators (Frijters, 1979). The Thurstonian d′ = 1 describes both results with a single number.

Uncertainty: why d′ comes with an interval

With a few dozen assessors, p̂c is uncertain by ±0.1 or more. Near p₀ = 1/3 the triangle curve is almost flat, so a small uncertainty in p̂c becomes a large uncertainty in d′. The usual standard error from the delta method (Eq. T4) assumes a straight curve and gives a symmetric (Wald) interval, which can be seriously wrong for small panels. A more honest interval takes the exact Clopper–Pearson interval of pc and maps its ends through the monotone psychometric function: for 13 correct out of 24 it runs from d′ = 0 to 2.76 — the data are compatible with no difference at all at the lower end, although the test is significant at the one-sided 5 % level. Likelihood-based intervals (Christensen, 2023) behave similarly.

Power and the choice of panel size

Power is the probability that the test detects a difference that really exists (Eq. T5). It depends on the method, the size of the difference, α and n — and only the last is yours to choose once the question is fixed. The chart above shows why triangle tests with 24 assessors so often “find nothing”: at d′ = 1.5 their power is 0.44. The tetrad test (two pairs, “group the four samples into two pairs of identical samples”) has the same p₀ but a steeper psychometric function, which is why Ennis and Jesionka (2011) recommend it when fatigue permits.

Showing similarity

A supplier change, a new process or a hydroponic crop often has to be shown to be “not different”. ISO 4120:2021 provides a similarity test for this purpose: choose the largest proportion of discriminators you could tolerate, pd0, and reject the hypothesis that the true pd is at least that large when the count of correct answers is small enough (Eq. T6). Similarity needs large panels — ISO suggests about twice as many assessors as a difference test — because a small panel cannot exclude moderate differences.

The equations behind the simulator

Exact binomial test and critical number(T1)
\[ P(X \ge x \mid H_0) = \sum_{i=x}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left(\tfrac13\right)^{i}\left(\tfrac23\right)^{n-i}, \qquad x_c = \min\bigl\{x : P(X \ge x \mid H_0) \le \alpha\bigr\} \]

the p-value is the probability of at least the observed number of correct answers if every assessor guessed with a one-in-three chance; the critical number is the smallest count whose tail probability is at most alpha.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(n\)number of independent assessors (one triangle each)–
\(x\)observed number of correct answers–
\(X\)random number of correct answers under \(H_0: p_c = 1/3\)–
\(\alpha\)significance level (one-sided)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Under \(H_0\) each answer is a Bernoulli trial with success probability 1/3, independent of the others (every assessor tastes one triangle).
  2. The number of ways to have exactly \(i\) successes among \(n\) is \(\binom{n}{i}\); each such way has probability \((1/3)^i(2/3)^{n-i}\).
  3. A perceptible difference can only increase \(p_c\), so the test is one-sided: sum the upper tail from the observed \(x\).
  4. Example: \(n = 24\), \(x = 13\): \(P = 0.028\) < 0.05. The critical number for \(n = 24\) is 13, for \(n = 30\) it is 15 (ISO 4120:2021, Table A.1).
Assumptions and validity

Independence: replicated triangles from the same assessor are not independent and must not be pooled as if they came from different people. The exact test is valid for any \(n\); normal approximations are not needed.

Thurstonian decision rule and psychometric function(T2)
\[ a_1, a_2 \sim N(0,1),\;\; b \sim N(d',1): \quad \text{correct} \iff |a_1 - a_2| < \min\bigl(|b-a_1|,\;|b-a_2|\bigr) \] \[ p_c = f_{\text{tri}}(d') = 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl[\Phi\bigl(-\sqrt3\,z + \sqrt{2/3}\,d'\bigr) + \Phi\bigl(-\sqrt3\,z - \sqrt{2/3}\,d'\bigr)\Bigr]\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z \]

the assessor answers correctly when the two perceptions of the identical samples are closer to each other than either is to the perception of the odd sample; averaging this event over all possible perceptions gives the probability of a correct answer as an integral over the standard normal distribution.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a_1, a_2, b\)perceptions of the two identical samples and of the odd sampleperceptual SD
\(d'\)distance between the mean perceptions of B and Aperceptual SD
\(\Phi, \varphi\)standard normal CDF and density–
\(z\)standardised half-difference of the two A perceptions–
Follow the derivation
  1. Write \(a_{1,2} = m \pm \delta\) with \(\delta = (a_1-a_2)/2 \sim N(0, 1/2)\) independent of \(m\); let \(w = b - m \sim N(d', 3/2)\).
  2. The distances are \(|a_1-a_2| = 2|\delta|\) and \(|b - a_{1,2}| = |w \mp \delta|\). Both exceed \(2|\delta|\) exactly when \(|w| > 3|\delta|\).
  3. Put \(\delta = z/\sqrt2\) and \(w = d' + \sqrt{3/2}\,\varepsilon\); then \(P(|w| > 3|z|/\sqrt2 \mid z) = \Phi(-\sqrt3|z| + \sqrt{2/3}\,d') + \Phi(-\sqrt3|z| - \sqrt{2/3}\,d')\).
  4. Average over \(z \sim N(0,1)\), using symmetry: \(E[g(|z|)] = 2\int_0^\infty g(z)\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\).
  5. Check: \(d' = 0\) gives \(4\int_0^\infty \Phi(-\sqrt3 z)\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z = 4/12 = 1/3\) ✓. The laboratory evaluates the integral with Simpson's rule (260 intervals on [0, 8.5]); the simulator draws \(a_1, a_2, b\) directly and applies the rule, so the two must agree in the long run — press Repeat 1000 panels to check.
Assumptions and validity

Equal-variance normal perceptions on one dimension, independent tastings and the “closest pair” decision rule (Ennis, 1993; Brockhoff & Christensen, 2010). Real assessors may use other strategies, and carry-over between the three samples is ignored.

\(d'\) is a ratio of perceptual distances to a perceptual SD; \(\Phi\) and \(\varphi\,\mathrm{d}z\) are dimensionless, so \(p_c\) is a probability ✓
Guessing model and exact interval for pd(T3)
\[ p_c = p_d + \frac{1-p_d}{3} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \hat p_d = \frac{3\hat p_c - 1}{2}, \qquad [\,p_{d,L},\,p_{d,U}\,] = \left[\frac{3p_{c,L}-1}{2},\;\frac{3p_{c,U}-1}{2}\right]_{\ge 0} \]

the probability of a correct answer is the share of discriminators plus one third of the non-discriminators; inverting gives the estimated share of discriminators, and its confidence limits follow by transforming the exact limits of the proportion correct, truncated at zero.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(p_d\)proportion of discriminators in the population–
\(\hat p_c = x/n\)observed proportion correct–
\(p_{c,L}, p_{c,U}\)exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95 % limits of \(p_c\)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Law of total probability: \(P(\text{correct}) = 1\cdot p_d + \tfrac13(1-p_d)\).
  2. Solve: \(p_d = (p_c - \tfrac13)/\tfrac23 = (3p_c - 1)/2\) (Abbott's formula).
  3. The Clopper–Pearson limits solve \(P(X \ge x \mid p_{c,L}) = 0.025\) and \(P(X \le x \mid p_{c,U}) = 0.025\) (Clopper & Pearson, 1934). A monotone transformation of an exact interval is an exact interval for the transformed parameter.
  4. Example: 13 of 24 → \(\hat p_d = 0.31\); \(p_c \in [0.328, 0.744]\) → \(p_d \in [0, 0.62]\).
Proportions — dimensionless ✓
Estimating d′ with a standard error and an exact interval(T4)
\[ \hat d' = f_{\text{tri}}^{-1}(\hat p_c), \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat d') \approx \frac{\sqrt{\hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)/n}}{f'_{\text{tri}}(\hat d')}, \qquad \bigl[d'_L,\,d'_U\bigr] = \bigl[f_{\text{tri}}^{-1}(p_{c,L}),\,f_{\text{tri}}^{-1}(p_{c,U})\bigr] \]

d-prime is the value whose predicted proportion correct equals the observed one; its Wald standard error is the binomial standard error divided by the slope of the psychometric function, and an exact interval maps the Clopper–Pearson limits of the proportion correct through the inverse function.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(f_{\text{tri}}^{-1}\)inverse psychometric function (solved by bisection; 0 if \(\hat p_c \le 1/3\))SD
\(f'_{\text{tri}}\)slope \(\mathrm{d}p_c/\mathrm{d}d'\) (central difference)per SD
Follow the derivation
  1. \(f_{\text{tri}}\) increases monotonically from 1/3 to 1, so it has a unique inverse.
  2. Delta method: a small error \(\epsilon\) in \(\hat p_c\) gives an error \(\epsilon/f'\) in \(\hat d'\); with \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_c) = \sqrt{\hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)/n}\) this gives the formula.
  3. 13 of 24: \(\hat p_c = 0.542\), \(\hat d' = 1.68\), \(f' = 0.197\) per SD, SE = 0.102/0.197 = 0.52, Wald interval 0.67–2.69; exact interval 0–2.76.
  4. The two intervals disagree because the curve is flat near 1/3: the Wald interval pretends that the slope at 1.68 applies everywhere.
Assumptions and validity

Wald intervals are acceptable only when \(\hat p_c\) is well above 1/3 and \(n\) is large. Report the exact (or likelihood) interval and note when its lower end reaches 0 (Christensen, 2023).

Exact power of a triangle test(T5)
\[ 1-\beta = P\bigl(X \ge x_c \mid n,\ p_{c1}\bigr), \qquad p_{c1} = f_{\text{tri}}(d') \ \text{ or } \ p_{c1} = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23\,p_d \]

the power is the probability of reaching the critical number when the true probability of a correct answer corresponds to the difference you want to detect.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x_c\)critical number from Eq. T1–
\(p_{c1}\)probability correct under the alternative–
\(1-\beta\)power–
Follow the derivation
  1. \(x_c\) depends only on \(n\) and α. Under the alternative, \(X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p_{c1})\), so the rejection probability is its upper tail from \(x_c\).
  2. Because \(x_c\) jumps in steps, power is a sawtooth in \(n\). At d′ = 1.5 (\(p_{c1} = 0.506\)) power first exceeds 0.80 at \(n = 53\) and stays above it from \(n = 57\); at d′ = 2 from \(n = 23\).

Similarity test(T6)
\[ H_0: p_d \ge p_{d0} \;\text{ vs }\; H_1: p_d < p_{d0}, \qquad p_{\text{sim}} = P\bigl(X \le x \mid n,\ p_{c0}\bigr), \quad p_{c0} = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23\,p_{d0} \]

to show similarity, reverse the hypotheses: the similarity p-value is the probability of so few or fewer correct answers if the difference were exactly at the tolerable limit; if it is at most beta, the products are declared similar.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(p_{d0}\)largest tolerable proportion of discriminators–
\(\beta\)risk of wrongly declaring similarity–
Follow the derivation
  1. Among all \(p_d \ge p_{d0}\), the case most likely to produce a small count is \(p_d = p_{d0}\); a count that is improbable there is improbable for every larger difference.
  2. Equivalently, similarity holds when the one-sided \((1-\beta)\) upper confidence limit of \(p_d\) is below \(p_{d0}\) (the lower curve of the similarity chart).
  3. 21 of 60 with \(p_{d0} = 0.30\): \(p_{c0} = 0.533\), \(p_{\text{sim}} = 0.003\) — similar. With 24 assessors you would need \(x \le 8\), which even identical products produce only 59 % of the time.

Investigation tasks

  1. How often does chance fool us?

    Set d′ = 0 and n = 24 and press Repeat 1000 panels. What share of panels is significant at α = 0.05? Why is it lower than 5 %? Repeat for n = 30 and explain the difference with the table of critical numbers.

  2. The sawtooth

    For d′ = 1.5, find the smallest panel for which the power of the triangle test is at least 0.80 and stays above it for all larger panels. Compare with the tetrad and the 2-AFC test. What would you recommend for a class of 30 students, and what is the catch with the 2-AFC test?

  3. One difference, two numbers

    Switch the model to pd and set pd = 0.3. Which d′ does this correspond to in a triangle test (HUD)? Now suppose the same products were tested with a 2-AFC test: what proportion of discriminators would the guessing model report? Explain Frijters' paradox in your own words.

  4. How precise is d′?

    Analyse 13 of 24. Report the Wald and the exact interval for d′. Keeping the proportion correct at 0.54, how many assessors are needed for the exact interval to exclude 0.5? Use the table and the readouts.

  5. Similarity needs numbers

    With pd0 = 0.3 and β = 0.05, simulate panels of 24 and 60 assessors with d′ = 0 (Repeat 1000 panels, then read the table: “similar if x ≤ …”). In what share of panels could similarity be concluded, even though the products are identical? What does this mean for a student project?

  6. Your own test

    Load your data from the project (or type them in). Write a results paragraph: n, x, p-value, p̂d and d′ with intervals, and — if relevant — the similarity conclusion with pd0 and β stated.

Model assumptions

  • Each simulated assessor evaluates one triangle; arrangements are balanced in random blocks of six; answers of different assessors are independent.
  • Thurstonian mode: perceptions are independent normal variables with equal variances (A: mean 0, B: mean d′); the assessor applies the “closest pair is identical” rule. Guessing mode: discriminators always answer correctly, non-discriminators choose at random.
  • p-values and critical numbers are exact one-sided binomial calculations with p₀ = 1/3; confidence intervals for pc are exact Clopper–Pearson intervals, transformed monotonically to pd and d′; the Wald standard error of d′ uses the delta method.
  • Psychometric functions of the tetrad, duo–trio, 2-AFC and 3-AFC are the standard Thurstonian models (Ennis, 1993; Ennis et al., 1998; Brockhoff & Christensen, 2010) evaluated by numerical integration; the 2-AFC assumes that the assessors know which attribute differs.
  • Similarity is tested as in ISO 4120:2021 with the tolerable proportion of discriminators pd0 and risk β; replicated evaluations are not supported.
  • Project data are read from the browser (key ffp-sensory-v1): n = number of triangle responses, x = number marked correct.
