Sampling, noise and filtering

Every number on a greenhouse dashboard has travelled a long way: from the moving air, through a sensor that responds slowly, an amplifier that adds noise, a radio that occasionally injects a spike, an analogue-to-digital converter that rounds, and a logger that only looks every so often. This virtual oscilloscope lets you follow that chain. Choose how often to log, watch a ten-minute ventilation cycle turn into a slow wave that never existed, and then fight the noise with four filters — moving average, exponential smoothing, running median and a Kalman filter — scored against the truth that only a simulator can know.

What to look at first

The dark screen is an oscilloscope. The faint white trace is the true air temperature at the sensor position, simulated every 5 s: a day–night swing, a 12-minute cycle as vents or heating switch around their set point, and small turbulent eddies. Yellow dots are what your logger stores. The coloured lines are four filters working on those dots in real time. Time runs from left to right; the S/H marker (sample-and-hold) shows the newest sample.

  1. Log fast enough. With the default Δt = 30 s, count the yellow dots on one climate cycle: twenty-four. The joined dots look like the real signal, only noisier. The readout confirms fN = 60 h⁻¹, far above the 5 h⁻¹ of the cycle.
  2. Spring the aliasing trap. Choose the preset Aliasing trap (Δt = 10 min). The joined samples now draw a smooth wave with a period of one hour — a wave that does not exist. In the spectrum, the true peak at 5 h⁻¹ lies beyond the Nyquist line (3 h⁻¹) and reappears at 1 h⁻¹. Now slide Δt towards 12 min: the fake wave slows down, and at exactly 12 min the oscillation vanishes from the log altogether.
  3. Oversample and average. Switch to Mean of 5-s readings at Δt = 10 min. The fake wave almost disappears: averaging over the logging interval is an anti-aliasing filter. It cannot recover the 12-minute cycle, but it stops it from masquerading as something else.
  4. Break the filters with spikes. Choose Spiky radio interference. Each red ring marks a reading hit by a spike. The moving average and exponential smoothing smear every spike into a bump; the running median ignores isolated spikes completely. Turn on the Kalman filter's 3σ gate and watch it cross out the spikes instead of following them.
  5. Watch the gain settle. In the Kalman chart, change √Q and √R. The gain always starts near 1 (the first measurements are all the filter knows) and converges within a few samples to K∞. In steady state the Kalman filter is an exponential smoother with α = K∞ — compare the dashed Kalman curve with the EMA curve in the frequency-response chart when you set α = K∞.
  6. Find the corner. In the trade-off chart, drag the moving-average window from 1 to 60. The RMSE first falls (noise is averaged away) and then rises again (the filter can no longer follow the climate cycle and the diurnal ramp). The optimum is a compromise that depends on the signal, the noise and Δt.
Key idea

Two decisions are irreversible: how often you sample and what you do before sampling. Aliasing cannot be undone by any filter afterwards, because the samples of a fast cycle and of its slow alias are identical. Everything after the logger — smoothing, spike removal, Kalman filtering — can be redone later from the raw data, which is why the raw log must always be kept.

A greenhouse signal lives on many time scales

Greenhouse air temperature changes on at least four time scales at once. The diurnal cycle (24 h) follows the sun and the heating strategy. The control cycle (minutes) appears because vents, screens and heating valves are switched by controllers with dead bands and delays: the temperature swings around the set point like a thermostat-controlled oven. Turbulence (seconds) moves warm and cool parcels of air past the sensor. And events — a heating boost, a screen opening, a door left open — produce steps. The logger must resolve the time scales that matter for its purpose and suppress the ones that do not.

The measurement chain

Figure 1 shows the path from the air to the dashboard. Each block changes the signal in a characteristic way: the sensor acts as a first-order low-pass filter; the electronics add white noise and occasional spikes; the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) rounds each reading to one of 2b levels; the logger keeps one number every Δt; and a digital filter finally trades noise against lag.

Sampling and aliasing

Sampling replaces a continuous signal by a list of numbers taken every Δt. The sampling theorem (Nyquist, 1928; Shannon, 1949) says that the list determines the signal completely only if the signal contains no frequencies at or above half the sampling frequency, the Nyquist frequency fN = fs/2. A faster component is not lost — it is misrepresented: its samples are exactly those of a slower sinusoid, the alias (Figure 2). You know the effect from films in which the wheels of a moving car seem to turn slowly backwards: the camera samples 24 or 25 frames per second, too slowly for the spokes.

The practical rule is therefore not "sample at 2 fmax" but "remove what you cannot sample, then sample 5–10 times faster than what you want to see". Three anti-aliasing filters are available in a greenhouse logger. The sensor itself is a first-order low-pass filter — a slow, unventilated sensor is a poor thermometer but a decent anti-aliasing filter. An analogue RC filter in front of the ADC does the same electronically. And oversampling with block averaging — read every 5 s, store the mean — attenuates a component of frequency f by |sin(πfΔt)/(πfΔt)|, which is small for all components that would otherwise alias strongly.

Common misconception

"If the logged data look smooth, the sampling must have been fine." The opposite is often true: a smooth, slow wave in logged greenhouse data with a period of about an hour is a classic sign of an aliased control cycle. Check the Nyquist condition against every mechanism that can oscillate — vents, heating valves, fogging, CO₂ dosing, lamp cycling, compressors.

Rounding to 2b levels: quantisation

An ADC with b bits spread over a full-scale range divides it into 2b steps of size q. Every reading is rounded to the nearest step, an error that is spread evenly between −q/2 and +q/2 and has a standard deviation of q/√12. A 12-bit converter over −10 … 50 °C has q = 0.015 °C, negligible next to typical sensor noise; an 8-bit converter has q = 0.23 °C, and its output looks like a staircase. When the analogue noise is at least comparable to q, averaging many readings gives values between the steps: noise acts as "dither", and oversampling improves the effective resolution. Try the preset Cheap 8-bit ADC, then add 0.1 °C of noise and switch to block averaging.

Four filters, one trade-off

All four filters in this laboratory are causal: they use only the current and earlier samples, as a logger or controller must. Causality is why every filter lags; offline, with the whole record available, a centred window removes the lag (but not the smoothing of real events).

The median's noise reduction for Gaussian noise (≈ 1.25σ/√N for large N, from the asymptotic variance πσ²/(2N) of the sample median) is slightly worse than the moving average's σ/√N. It pays that small price for robustness: its output is always one of the actual readings, and a single wild value cannot move it. That is why data-quality procedures remove spikes with a median or Hampel filter first and smooth afterwards (Tukey, 1977; Pearson, 2002).

The Kalman filter as weighted averaging

The Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) treats filtering as estimation. Before each measurement it predicts: under the random-walk model the best guess is the previous estimate, but its variance has grown by Q, because the true temperature may have moved. It then updates: prediction and measurement are averaged with weights inversely proportional to their variances (Figure 3). The weight of the measurement is the gain K. With a large Q (the truth moves a lot between samples) K approaches 1 and the filter follows the data; with a large R (a noisy sensor) K approaches 0 and the filter trusts its prediction. Unlike the other filters, it also reports how uncertain its estimate is — the green band on the oscilloscope is ±2√P.

Because the gain depends only on Q, R and the initial P — not on the data — the gain sequence can be computed in advance, and it converges to the steady-state value K∞ (Eq. SP7). A steady-state Kalman filter is therefore an exponential smoother whose α was derived from two physically meaningful numbers instead of guessed: R from the sensor specification (white noise plus quantisation), Q from how fast the truth can move between two samples. For the default climate cycle, the fastest true change is Av · 2π/Pv ≈ 0.2 °C min⁻¹, about 0.1 °C per 30-s sample, so √Q of order 0.1–0.2 °C is physically reasonable.

Reading a spectrum

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) decomposes the record into sinusoids at the frequencies j/(NΔt), j = 1 … N/2, and the amplitude spectrum shows how much of each is present. Peaks reveal periodic mechanisms: the diurnal cycle at 1/24 h⁻¹, the control cycle at 60/Pv h⁻¹. Broad, flat regions are noise: white noise is spread evenly up to the Nyquist frequency, turbulence falls off above 1/(2πτu). Before the transform the data are multiplied by a Hann window to stop the abrupt ends of the record from leaking energy into all frequencies (Harris, 1978); try switching the window off to see the leakage. The spectrum is computed with the fast Fourier transform algorithm of Cooley and Tukey (1965).

Frontier — deciding at the edge what to send

Battery-powered wireless nodes pay for every transmitted sample (Lesson 9.4). Modern nodes therefore sample fast, filter and check the data on the microcontroller, and transmit only block means, statistics or significant changes ("send-on-delta"). This is exactly the oversample-and-average strategy of this laboratory, moved into the sensor node: it saves energy and prevents aliasing at the same time. The true greenhouse air temperature(SP1)
\[ \begin{gathered} T(t) = \bar T + A_d\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi\,(t - t_{\text{pk}})}{24\ \text{h}}\right) + A_v\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{P_v}\right) + u(t) + B(t) \\[6pt] u_{i+1} = u_i\,e^{-\Delta/\tau_u} + \sigma_u\sqrt{1 - e^{-2\Delta/\tau_u}}\;\varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \end{gathered} \]

Follow the derivation of the turbulence update
  1. Model the fluctuation as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: \(\mathrm{d}u = -u\,\mathrm{d}t/\tau_u + \sqrt{2\sigma_u^2/\tau_u}\,\mathrm{d}W\). The first term pulls \(u\) back to zero with time constant \(\tau_u\); the second adds white noise.
  2. Over a step \(\Delta\) the deterministic part decays exactly: \(u(t+\Delta) = u(t)e^{-\Delta/\tau_u} + \text{(noise accumulated during the step)}\).
  3. The accumulated noise is Gaussian with variance \(\int_0^{\Delta}(2\sigma_u^2/\tau_u)e^{-2s/\tau_u}\,\mathrm{d}s = \sigma_u^2(1 - e^{-2\Delta/\tau_u})\), which gives the square-root factor.
  4. Check the stationary variance: \(\mathrm{Var}(u) = \mathrm{Var}(u)e^{-2\Delta/\tau_u} + \sigma_u^2(1 - e^{-2\Delta/\tau_u})\) is satisfied by \(\mathrm{Var}(u) = \sigma_u^2\) ✓. Its autocorrelation after a lag \(s\) is \(e^{-s/\tau_u}\): readings a minute apart are still correlated, readings ten minutes apart are practically independent.
Assumptions and validity

Real control cycles are not sinusoids: on/off control produces triangular or saw-tooth waves whose harmonics (2f, 3f, …) alias too. Real diurnal curves are asymmetric (a fast morning rise, a slower evening fall). The simple forms are used so that each component appears as one clean line in the spectrum. The periods and amplitudes are illustrative defaults to be replaced with values from your own greenhouse log.

Follow the derivation
  1. Rounding to the nearest level leaves an error \(e\) between \(-q/2\) and \(+q/2\). When the signal moves over many levels, every value in this interval is equally likely: \(e\) is uniformly distributed with density \(1/q\).
  2. Its mean is 0 and its variance is \(\int_{-q/2}^{q/2} e^2\,\frac{1}{q}\,\mathrm{d}e = \frac{1}{q}\left[\frac{e^3}{3}\right]_{-q/2}^{q/2} = \frac{q^2}{12}\).
  3. Independent errors add their variances (the law of propagation of uncertainty, Lesson 9.3): \(\sigma_{\text{tot}}^2 = \sigma_v^2 + q^2/12\). This is the value the laboratory uses for the Kalman R when "R from the sensor specification" is on.
Assumptions and validity — when the uniform model fails

If the signal is almost constant and the noise is much smaller than q, the error is not random: the output sticks to one level and the error is a slowly varying bias (Widrow & Kollár, 2008). A little analogue noise ("dither") restores the uniform model, and averaging m readings then reduces the rounding error below q/√12 — oversampling buys resolution.

Sampling theorem, alias frequency and anti-aliasing gains(SP4)
\[ \begin{gathered} f_s = \frac{1}{\Delta t}, \qquad f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}, \qquad f_a = \left|\,f - f_s\,\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right)\right| \\[6pt] G_{\text{block}}(f) = \left|\frac{\sin(\pi f\Delta t)}{\pi f\Delta t}\right|, \qquad G_{\text{sensor}}(f) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (2\pi f\tau_s)^2}} \end{gathered} \]

the sampling frequency is the reciprocal of the logging interval and the Nyquist frequency is half of it; a sinusoid of frequency f appears at the alias frequency, its distance from the nearest whole multiple of the sampling frequency; block averaging over Δt passes a fraction |sin(πfΔt)/(πfΔt)| of the amplitude, and the sensor passes 1/√(1 + (2πfτₛ)²).

Follow the derivation
  1. Sampling \(\cos(2\pi f t)\) at \(t_k = k/f_s\) gives \(\cos(2\pi f k/f_s)\). For any integer \(n\), \(\cos(2\pi(f - nf_s)k/f_s) = \cos(2\pi fk/f_s - 2\pi nk)\) is the same number, because \(nk\) is an integer. All frequencies \(|f - nf_s|\) produce identical samples.

  4. Sensor. Inserting \(T = e^{i\omega t}\) into \(\tau_s\,\mathrm{d}T_s/\mathrm{d}t + T_s = T\) gives \(T_s = T/(1 + i\omega\tau_s)\), whose magnitude is the expression in the box, with \(\omega = 2\pi f\).

The rule "f < fN" is necessary but not sufficient for a readable record: with only 2–4 samples per cycle the shape of the cycle is lost. Aim for at least 5–10.

Moving average and exponential smoothing(SP5)
\[ \begin{gathered} y_k^{\text{MA}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} z_{k-j}, \qquad \left|H_{\text{MA}}(f)\right| = \left|\frac{\sin(\pi f N\Delta t)}{N\sin(\pi f\Delta t)}\right| \\[6pt] y_k^{\text{EMA}} = \alpha z_k + (1-\alpha)\,y_{k-1}, \qquad \left|H_{\text{EMA}}(f)\right| = \frac{\alpha}{\left|1 - (1-\alpha)\,e^{-i2\pi f\Delta t}\right|} \end{gathered} \]

Running median(SP6)
\[ \begin{gathered} y_k^{\text{med}} = \operatorname{median}\left\{z_{k-N+1}, \dots, z_{k}\right\} \\[6pt] \text{removes up to } \tfrac{N-1}{2} \text{ spikes per window}, \qquad t_{63} = \tfrac{N-1}{2}\,\Delta t \end{gathered} \]

the running median replaces each value by the middle value of the last N samples (N odd); up to (N − 1)/2 wild values in the window cannot move it, and a step passes through once more than half the window has the new level, after (N − 1)/2 samples.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Sort the N values; the median is the one in position \((N+1)/2\). Replacing any \((N-1)/2\) values by arbitrarily large or small numbers moves them to the ends of the sorted list; the middle value is still one of the unaffected readings. The median's breakdown point is 50 %, the mean's is 0 % (one value can move it anywhere).
  2. For a step from 0 to 1, the median jumps from 0 to 1 as soon as \((N+1)/2\) of the N values are 1, i.e. after \((N-1)/2\) further samples. Unlike linear filters it reproduces the step sharply, without a ramp.
  3. For Gaussian noise its variance for large N is \(\pi\sigma^2/(2N)\), 57 % larger than the mean's \(\sigma^2/N\): a small price for robustness.
Assumptions and validity

The median is non-linear: it has no frequency response, and it can cut the peaks of narrow real events (a short sunfleck, a one-minute heating pulse). The Hampel filter used in data-quality control (sensor anomaly laboratory) replaces a value by the median only when it is further than a few robust standard deviations from it, leaving normal data untouched.

One-dimensional Kalman filter with a random-walk model(SP7)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{predict:}\quad \hat x_k^- = \hat x_{k-1}, \qquad P_k^- = P_{k-1} + Q \\[6pt] \text{update:}\quad K_k = \frac{P_k^-}{P_k^- + R}, \qquad \hat x_k = \hat x_k^- + K_k\,(z_k - \hat x_k^-), \qquad P_k = (1 - K_k)\,P_k^- \\[6pt] \text{steady state:}\quad P_\infty^- = \tfrac12\left(Q + \sqrt{Q^2 + 4QR}\right), \qquad K_\infty = \frac{P_\infty^-}{P_\infty^- + R} \\[6pt] \text{gate: skip the update if } |z_k - \hat x_k^-| > 3\sqrt{P_k^- + R} \end{gathered} \]

predict that the temperature has not changed but let the variance grow by Q; weight the measurement by the gain, the predicted variance over the total variance; move the estimate by the gain times the innovation and shrink the variance by the factor 1 − K; with constant Q and R the variance and gain converge to the steady-state values; optionally ignore a measurement whose innovation exceeds three standard deviations of its predicted spread.

SymbolMeaningUnitHere
\(\hat x_k\), \(P_k\)estimate and its variance after sample k°C, °C²start: 20 °C, 1 °C²
\(Q\)process-noise variance: expected squared change of the truth per sample°C²(√Q)²
\(R\)measurement-noise variance°C²σv² + q²/12
\(K_k\), \(K_\infty\)gain and its steady-state value–
\(z_k - \hat x_k^-\)innovation: measurement minus prediction°C
Follow the derivation
  1. Model. Truth: \(x_k = x_{k-1} + w_k\), \(\mathrm{Var}(w) = Q\). Measurement: \(z_k = x_k + v_k\), \(\mathrm{Var}(v) = R\).
  2. Predict. Without new information the best guess is unchanged; the error now includes \(w_k\), so \(P^- = P + Q\).
  3. Combine. \(\hat x = (1-K)\hat x^- + Kz\) has error variance \((1-K)^2P^- + K^2R\). Setting the derivative with respect to K to zero gives \(K = P^-/(P^- + R)\) and then \(P = (1-K)P^-\).
  4. Steady state. Require \(P_{k+1}^- = P_k^- = P^-\): \(P^- = (1-K)P^- + Q = \frac{P^-R}{P^- + R} + Q\). Multiplying out gives \((P^-)^2 - QP^- - QR = 0\), whose positive root is in the box.
  5. Equivalence with the EMA. With \(K_k = K_\infty\), the update \(\hat x_k = (1-K_\infty)\hat x_{k-1} + K_\infty z_k\) is exactly Eq. SP5 with \(\alpha = K_\infty\).
Assumptions and validity

The filter is optimal only if the model is right: the truth really behaves like a random walk and the noises are Gaussian, independent and of the stated variances. A deterministic cycle or ramp violates the random-walk model, which is why the Kalman filter lags on the diurnal ramp just like an EMA. Adding the rate of change as a second state (a "constant-velocity" model) removes that lag. The 3σ gate protects against spikes but can also reject a genuine sudden step; P then grows by Q per rejected sample until the step is accepted.

Discrete Fourier transform and amplitude spectrum(SP8)
\[ \begin{gathered} X_j = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} w_k\,(z_k - \bar z)\,e^{-i2\pi jk/M}, \qquad w_k = \tfrac12\left(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi k}{n-1}\right) \\[6pt] A_j = \frac{2\,|X_j|}{\sum_k w_k}, \qquad f_j = \frac{j}{M\,\Delta t} \end{gathered} \]

the DFT correlates the mean-removed, windowed record with complex sinusoids of j cycles per record; twice the magnitude divided by the sum of the window weights is the amplitude of the component at frequency j/(MΔt); the Hann window tapers the record smoothly to zero at both ends.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(n\), \(M\)number of samples; transform length after zero-padding to a power of 2 (M ≥ n)–
\(w_k\)Hann window weight (1 for all k when the window is switched off)–
\(A_j\), \(f_j\)amplitude and frequency of component j°C, h⁻¹
Follow the reasoning
  1. A sinusoid \(A\cos(2\pi f_j t)\) that fits a whole number of cycles in the record gives \(|X_j| = A\sum w_k/2\): the factor 2/Σw in the box returns its amplitude A.
  2. The frequencies that can be distinguished are spaced by \(1/(n\Delta t)\), the reciprocal of the record length: two days of data resolve 0.021 h⁻¹. Zero-padding interpolates the spectrum but does not improve this resolution.
  3. Frequencies run from 0 to \(f_N = 1/(2\Delta t)\); the components \(j > M/2\) are mirror images (the same aliasing as in Eq. SP4).
  4. Without a window, a sinusoid that does not fit a whole number of cycles "leaks" into all bins, with side lobes falling only as 1/f; the Hann window lowers the side lobes to −31 dB and makes them fall much faster, at the cost of a slightly wider peak (Harris, 1978).
Assumptions and validity

The DFT assumes the record is one period of an infinitely repeating signal and that the samples are equally spaced and complete. Gaps must be filled (the laboratory interpolates pasted data linearly) or treated with methods for uneven sampling.

How the filters are scored(SP9)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=k_0}^{k_0+K-1}\left(y_k - T(t_k)\right)^2} \\[6pt] t_{63} = \Delta t\cdot\min\left\{k \ge 0 : y_k^{\text{step}} \ge 1 - e^{-1}\right\} \end{gathered} \]

the root-mean-square error is the square root of the mean squared difference between the filter output and the true temperature at the sampling times, ignoring the first hour; the lag is the time a filter needs, after a unit step in its input, to cover 63.2 % of the step.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(y_k\), \(T(t_k)\)filter output and true temperature at sample time°C
\(k_0\), \(K\)first scored sample (after 1 h) and number of scored samples–
\(y^{\text{step}}\)response of the filter (with its current parameters, Kalman gain at \(K_\infty\)) to a unit step at k = 0–
Why these two numbers?

RMSE measures total error — noise that got through plus real signal that was smoothed away or delayed — against the one thing a real logger never knows: the truth. The step lag measures responsiveness in a way that is independent of the signal. Together they draw the trade-off curve of each filter: a filter that is "better" in both at once exists only if another one was badly tuned. The 63 % definition matches the time constant of a first-order sensor, so filter lag and sensor lag can be added directly when you judge the whole chain.

Model assumptions

  • The true temperature is a sum of a diurnal cosine, one sinusoidal control cycle, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck turbulence (σu, τu = 60 s) and an optional 3-h heating boost. Real control cycles are rarely sinusoidal and their period drifts with weather; the defaults are illustrative, not measurements from a specific greenhouse.
  • The truth is simulated every 5 s; the logger's fastest possible reading interval is also 5 s. Components faster than 5 s (for example 50 Hz mains pick-up) are not modelled.
  • The sensor is a first-order system with a single time constant; radiation errors, self-heating and calibration offsets are not modelled (see Lesson 9.3 for those).
  • Measurement noise is white and Gaussian; spikes occur independently with a fixed probability per reading and have a random sign. The ADC is ideal (no differential non-linearity) and clips at −10 and +50 °C.
  • All filters are causal and start from the first sample (the moving average and median use the available samples while the window fills; the Kalman filter starts at 20 °C with P₀ = 1 °C² and does not gate the first 10 samples). Scores ignore the first hour.
  • The lag is the 63 % step response of the filter alone; the sensor adds its own τs and block averaging adds about Δt/2.
  • Pasted data are placed on a regular time grid by linear interpolation using the median interval; gaps longer than one interval are therefore bridged by straight lines.
