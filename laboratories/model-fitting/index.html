A guided tour in six steps

  1. Look before you fit. The default data are five simulated lettuces weighed twice a week. All six curves pass through the cloud and every R² in the results table is above 0.85 — yet the Akaike weights differ enormously. Click the Linear row in the table: its residuals form a U (positive, negative, positive), and the runs test turns red. A straight line has the wrong shape, however high its R².
  2. Log the y axis (Models → Logarithmic y axis). On a log scale exponential growth is a straight line: you can see that the relative growth rate is highest in the first week and falls steadily afterwards. The Relative growth rate against size chart shows the same thing directly.
  3. Change the error model to Proportional. With plants of 3 g and 300 g in one data set, an error of 10 g means very different things. Fitting \(\ln W\) weights every point by its relative error. Watch the ranking and the parameters change — and note that AICc values of the two error models cannot be compared with each other.
  4. Choose “First two weeks only” (or the preset Only the first two weeks). Put the logistic in focus. Its asymptote \(K\) is now about 90 g with a standard error of the same order of magnitude, and the 250 g target is never reached — although the full data set passes 250 g around day 30. The bootstrap histogram shows how little two weeks of data say about the harvest. A forecast is only as good as the phase of growth you have observed.
  5. Fit by eye. Tick Fit the focus model by hand. Drag the sliders until the magenta curve hugs the points; the pale squares are the squared residuals whose total (SSE) you are minimising. Then press Let Levenberg–Marquardt finish and watch the optimiser take over, iteration by iteration.
  6. Over-fit on purpose. Move the polynomial degree to 8 and draw it on the main chart. It hugs the data — and does something absurd beyond the last date. The U-shaped curve in Over-fitting with polynomials is the bias–variance trade-off of Lesson 10.1, measured on your data.
Key idea

A good growth model is not the one with the highest \(R^2\). It is the simplest model whose residuals show no pattern, whose parameters are well determined and biologically sensible, and which predicts data it has not seen — later dates, other plants — as well as the data it was fitted to.

Why fit a growth function at all?

A column of plant masses is hard to compare, to test and to extrapolate. A fitted growth function compresses it into two to four numbers that each answer a biological question: how fast does a small plant grow (the relative growth rate \(r\)), how large does it get (the asymptote \(K\)), when is it growing fastest (the inflection time \(t_i\)), what is its top speed (the maximum absolute growth rate). These numbers can be compared between treatments with ordinary statistics, and the curve can be extrapolated — carefully — to forecast the harvest (Hunt, 1982; Paine et al., 2012).

How the optimiser finds the parameters

“Best” means the parameters that make the sum of squared vertical distances between the data and the curve (SSE) as small as possible — least squares. For a straight line this minimum can be written down; for the sigmoid curves the parameters sit inside exponentials and the minimum must be found by iteration. This lab uses the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (Levenberg, 1944; Marquardt, 1963) implemented in the course library (fitLM): at each step it linearises the model around the current guess (the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives), solves a small linear least-squares problem for the correction, and blends this Gauss–Newton step with a cautious gradient-descent step whenever the linearisation is not trustworthy.

Iterative optimisers need a starting guess. Bad starts end in the wrong valley or not at all. The lab therefore uses self-starting rules taken from the shape of the data: for the logistic, for example, it tries several trial asymptotes \(K_0\) slightly above the largest observation, transforms the data to \(\ln[W/(K_0-W)]\) — a straight line in time for logistic growth — and reads \(r\) and \(t_i\) from a linear regression. It then runs Levenberg–Marquardt from every start and keeps the lowest SSE. Parameters are kept inside sensible bounds (positive rates, asymptotes above zero). When a parameter ends up on its bound — the Richards shape parameter \(\nu\) collapsing towards zero is the common case — the table flags it: the model has effectively turned into a simpler one (here the Gompertz) and its standard errors mean little.

Judging one fit: residuals, not \(R^2\)

\(R^2\) measures how much of the variation around the mean the curve explains. Growth data vary hugely over time, so almost any increasing curve scores above 0.9. The residuals are far more informative. Random scatter around zero means the shape is right; long runs of positive and negative residuals mean it is wrong — a straight line through accelerating growth data gives positive residuals at both ends and negative ones in the middle. The runs test counts those runs and asks whether there are fewer than chance would produce (Eq. MF13). Two other checks: a funnel (spread growing with size) calls for the proportional error model, and parameters with standard errors as large as themselves are not determined by your data.

Choosing between models: parsimony and prediction

Adding a parameter never increases the SSE, so in-sample fit always favours the most complex model. Two families of tools correct for this. Information criteria (AIC, AICc, BIC) add a penalty for every estimated parameter; Akaike weights turn the differences into the relative support for each model (Burnham & Anderson, 2002). Cross-validation estimates prediction error directly: refit the model without some of the data and predict what was left out. How you leave data out matters. Random folds let the model interpolate between neighbouring dates, which is easy. Leaving out the last 30 % of dates tests forecasting, and leaving out whole plants tests whether the curve generalises to a plant it has never seen — the questions a grower actually asks (Roberts et al., 2017).

From a fitted curve to a harvest forecast

Solving the fitted curve for the day on which it reaches a target size \(W^*\) gives a point forecast (Eq. MF14). Its uncertainty comes from the uncertainty of the parameters, which are correlated and enter non-linearly. The lab uses a parametric bootstrap (Efron & Tibshirani, 1994): it simulates hundreds of new data sets from the fitted curve plus noise with the fitted residual standard deviation, refits the model to each, and reads the forecast day from every refitted curve. The spread of those days is the forecast uncertainty; the pointwise 2.5–97.5 % range of the refitted curves is the darker band on the main chart. The lighter band adds the scatter of individual plants: it is where 95 % of single new plants should fall.

Common misconception

“The asymptote \(K\) is the final size of my lettuce.” Only if your data include the saturation phase. Commercial lettuce is harvested while still growing almost linearly, so \(K\) is usually far above the last observation and very poorly determined — look at its standard error and bootstrap SE. Forecasts that rely on \(K\) (targets close to the asymptote) are correspondingly fragile.

Additive or proportional errors?

Least squares on \(W\) assumes that every observation has the same scatter in grams. Plant-to-plant variation is usually proportional to size — a 10 % difference is 0.3 g on day 0 and 30 g on day 35 — so the late, large plants dominate the SSE and the early data hardly influence the fit. Fitting \(\ln W\) instead (the Proportional error model) gives every point equal weight in relative terms and is often the better choice for growth data (Paine et al., 2012). Because the two error models fit different responses, their SSE, AICc and RMSE are on different scales and must not be compared with each other.

The equations behind the lab

Notation: \(W\) is the size variable (fresh mass, projected area, height…) in its own unit, \(t\) time in days, \(n\) the number of observations and \(p\) the number of curve parameters. The growth functions use the same parameterisation as Lesson 10.1 (Eqs. 10.1.4–10.1.6).

Linear growth(MF1)
\[ W(t) = a + b\,t, \qquad \frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = b \]

size increases by the same amount every day: the absolute growth rate is the constant \(b\), and \(a\) is the size the line predicts at day zero.

Where does it come from?
  1. Assume the growth rate does not depend on size: \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = b\).
  2. Integrate: \(W = a + bt\). Least squares has the closed-form solution \(b = S_{tW}/S_{tt}\), \(a = \bar W - b\bar t\) (Lesson 10.1, Eq. 10.1.2); fitLM reproduces it in one iteration.
Assumptions and validity

Plausible only for a closed canopy that intercepts a constant amount of light each day (the late phase of the expolinear curve). Over a whole crop cycle it predicts negative sizes at the start — a sure sign of the wrong model.

\([b\,t] = (\text{unit d}^{-1})(\text{d}) = \text{unit}\) ✓
Exponential growth, relative growth rate and doubling time(MF2)
\[ W(t) = W_0\,e^{r t}, \qquad \text{RGR} = \frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = r, \qquad t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \]

every unit of plant produces new plant at the same relative rate \(r\), so the size doubles every \(\ln 2/r\) days.

Logistic growth(MF3)
\[ W(t) = \frac{K}{1+e^{-r(t-t_i)}}, \qquad \text{RGR} = r\Bigl(1-\frac{W}{K}\Bigr), \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}\Bigr)_{\max} = \frac{rK}{4} \]

the relative growth rate falls in a straight line from \(r\) (tiny plant) to zero (at the asymptote \(K\)); growth is fastest at the inflection time \(t_i\), when the plant is half its final size.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(K\)asymptote (final size)unit of \(W\)
\(r\)intrinsic rate = RGR of a very small plantd⁻¹
\(t_i\)inflection time (\(W = K/2\))d
Follow the derivation
  1. Start from \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = rW(1-W/K)\) and separate: \(\dfrac{\mathrm dW}{W(1-W/K)} = r\,\mathrm dt\).
  2. Partial fractions and integration give \(\ln\dfrac{W}{K-W} = r(t-t_i)\): the logit of \(W/K\) is linear in time — the lab uses exactly this to find starting values.
  3. Solving for \(W\) gives the curve. The growth rate \(rW - rW^2/K\) is maximal where its derivative \(r-2rW/K\) vanishes: \(W = K/2\), \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = rK/4\).
Assumptions and validity

Symmetric around \(t_i\): the approach to \(K\) mirrors the start. \(K\) is only determined if the data show the slowing down; with early data only it is almost unconstrained (the “first two weeks” data set).

\(r(t-t_i)\): d⁻¹ × d = 1 ✓; \(rK/4\): d⁻¹ × g = g d⁻¹ ✓
Gompertz growth(MF4)
\[ W(t) = K\exp\!\bigl[-e^{-r(t-t_i)}\bigr], \qquad \text{RGR} = r\,e^{-r(t-t_i)} = r\ln\frac{K}{W}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}\Bigr)_{\max} = \frac{rK}{e} \]

the relative growth rate decays exponentially with time; growth is fastest at \(t_i\), when the plant has reached only \(K/e\) ≈ 37 % of its final size, and the curve then creeps slowly towards \(K\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(K, r, t_i\)asymptote, rate constant of RGR decay, inflection timeunit of \(W\), d⁻¹, d
\(\lambda = t_i - 1/r\)lag time (Zwietering form): where the tangent at the inflection crosses \(W=0\)d
Follow the derivation
  1. Let the RGR decay: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d\ln W}{\mathrm dt} = r\,e^{-r(t-t_i)}\).
  2. Integrate: \(\ln W = \ln K - e^{-r(t-t_i)}\) ⇒ the formula. Therefore \(-\ln(-\ln(W/K)) = r(t-t_i)\) is linear in time (used for starting values).
  3. At \(t=t_i\): \(W = K/e\) and \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = W\cdot\text{RGR} = (K/e)\,r\).
  4. Zwietering et al. (1990) rewrite the same curve with \(\mu_m = rK/e\) and \(\lambda = t_i - 1/r\) so that each parameter answers a practical question.
Assumptions and validity

Asymmetric: often better than the logistic for leafy crops whose growth slows gradually. Like every sigmoid, \(K\) needs the saturation phase in the data.

\(e^{-r(t-t_i)}\) is dimensionless, so \(W\) has the unit of \(K\) ✓
Richards growth(MF5)
\[ W(t) = K\bigl[1+\nu\,e^{-r(t-t_i)}\bigr]^{-1/\nu}, \qquad W(t_i) = K(1+\nu)^{-1/\nu}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}\Bigr)_{\max} = rK(1+\nu)^{-(1+\nu)/\nu} \]

a fourth parameter \(\nu\) sets where the inflection lies: \(\nu = 1\) gives the logistic (inflection at \(K/2\)), \(\nu \to 0\) the Gompertz (\(K/e\)), large \(\nu\) a late, sharp inflection.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\nu\)shape parameter (bounded to 0.005–25 in this lab)–
\(K, r, t_i\)as in MF3unit of \(W\), d⁻¹, d
Follow the derivation
  1. With \(E = e^{-r(t-t_i)}\): \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = rKE(1+\nu E)^{-1/\nu-1}\).
  2. Setting the second derivative to zero gives \(E = 1\), i.e. \(t = t_i\), so \(t_i\) is exactly the inflection time and \(W(t_i) = K(1+\nu)^{-1/\nu}\).
  3. \(\nu = 1\): \(K/2\). \(\nu\to0\): \((1+\nu E)^{-1/\nu}\to e^{-E}\), the Gompertz curve (Richards, 1959; Tjørve & Tjørve, 2010).
Assumptions and validity

\(\nu\) is strongly correlated with \(r\) and \(t_i\) and is poorly determined unless many dates span the whole curve. When the lab flags “bound” for \(\nu\), the data prefer a Gompertz shape and the extra parameter is wasted — AICc then penalises it.

\((1+\nu E)\) is dimensionless, so \(W\) has the unit of \(K\) ✓
Expolinear growth (Goudriaan & Monteith)(MF6)
\[ W(t) = \frac{c_m}{r_m}\ln\!\bigl[1+e^{\,r_m(t-t_b)}\bigr] \;\xrightarrow{\;t\ll t_b\;}\; \frac{c_m}{r_m}e^{\,r_m(t-t_b)}, \qquad \xrightarrow{\;t\gg t_b\;}\; c_m\,(t-t_b) \]

growth starts exponentially with relative rate \(r_m\) while leaves are still far apart, and becomes linear with slope \(c_m\) once the canopy closes and intercepts almost all light; the linear asymptote crosses zero at the “lost time” \(t_b\).

\(c_m\)maximum absolute growth rate (crop growth rate of a closed canopy, per plant)unit of \(W\) d⁻¹

Follow the derivation
  1. Goudriaan and Monteith (1990) let the crop growth rate be proportional to intercepted light: exponential while leaf area is small, saturating at \(c_m\) when the canopy is closed. Integration gives the log-of-one-plus-exponential (“softplus”) form.
  2. For \(x = r_m(t-t_b) \ll 0\): \(\ln(1+e^x) \approx e^x\) ⇒ exponential phase with RGR \(r_m\).
  3. For \(x \gg 0\): \(\ln(1+e^x) \approx x\) ⇒ \(W \approx c_m(t-t_b)\).
Assumptions and validity

No asymptote: it describes growth up to commercial harvest, not senescence. That is exactly the situation of most lettuce crops, which is why the expolinear curve often wins on grow-log data.

\([c_m/r_m] = (\text{g d}^{-1})/(\text{d}^{-1}) = \text{g}\) ✓
Least squares and the Levenberg–Marquardt step(MF7)
\[ \text{SSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[y_i - f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr]^2, \qquad \bigl(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J + \lambda\,\operatorname{diag}\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\bigr)\,\Delta\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e \]

the best parameters minimise the sum of squared residuals; each iteration solves a small linear system for the correction \(\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\), built from the Jacobian (how the curve changes with each parameter at every data point) and the current residuals, with a damping \(\lambda\) that is decreased after a successful step and increased after a failed one.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\boldsymbol\theta\)parameter vector (e.g. \(K, r, t_i\))mixed
\(J_{ij} = \partial f(t_i)/\partial\theta_j\)Jacobian (computed by central differences in fitLM)unit of \(y\) / unit of \(\theta_j\)
\(\mathbf e\)residual vector \(y_i - f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta)\)unit of \(y\)
\(\lambda\)damping (starts at 10⁻³)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Linearise around the current guess: \(f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta+\Delta) \approx f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta) + \sum_j J_{ij}\Delta_j\).
  2. The SSE becomes \(\lVert\mathbf e - \mathbf J\Delta\rVert^2\), a quadratic in \(\Delta\); setting its gradient to zero gives the Gauss–Newton normal equations \(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\Delta = \mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e\).
  3. Far from the minimum the linearisation fails. Adding \(\lambda\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J)\) shortens the step and turns it towards steepest descent (large \(\lambda\)); Marquardt's diagonal scaling makes this independent of the parameter units.
  4. A step is accepted only if SSE falls; the iteration stops when the relative improvement is below 10⁻¹⁰ or after the iteration limit (flagged “slow”).
Additive versus proportional errors

Least squares is maximum likelihood for independent Gaussian errors of constant variance. For errors proportional to size (log-normal), the same holds for \(\ln y_i - \ln f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta)\), which is what the Proportional error model minimises. Back-transformed curves then describe the median plant rather than the mean.

SSE has the unit of \(y^2\) (g², or dimensionless on the ln scale); \(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\,\Delta\) and \(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e\) both have units of \(y^2/\theta\) ✓
Standard errors and confidence intervals of the parameters(MF8)
\[ s^2 = \frac{\text{SSE}}{n-p}, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) \approx s^2\bigl(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\bigr)^{-1}, \qquad \hat\theta_j \pm t_{0.975,\,n-p}\;\text{SE}(\hat\theta_j) \]

the residual variance is the SSE divided by the degrees of freedom; the parameter covariance matrix is that variance times the inverse of \(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\) at the optimum; square roots of its diagonal are the standard errors, and a 95 % confidence interval extends a t-quantile times the SE either side of the estimate.

\(s\)residual standard deviationunit of \(y\)
\(\text{SE}(\hat\theta_j)\)standard error of parameter \(j\)unit of \(\theta_j\)

Why this works — and when it does not

Near the optimum the model is approximately linear in its parameters, and for a linear model \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}) = \sigma^2(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\) exactly, with \(\mathbf J\) in the role of \(\mathbf X\). When the curvature of the model is strong over the range of plausible parameters — typically \(K\) when the asymptote is not observed — the linear approximation fails and the SE understates the uncertainty. Compare the bootstrap SE column in the parameter table: large disagreement is the warning sign.

\(s^2(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J)^{-1}\): \(y^2 \cdot (\theta/y)^2 = \theta^2\) ✓
Coefficient of determination and RMSE(MF9)
\[ R^2 = 1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}} \]

\(R^2\) is the fraction of the variation around the mean that the curve accounts for; the root-mean-square error is the typical size of a residual, in the units of the data.

Why a high \(R^2\) is cheap for growth data

SST is enormous for data that span two orders of magnitude, so even a poorly shaped curve leaves an SSE that is small relative to SST. Compare models with RMSE, AICc and cross-validation, and check the residual pattern. RMSE computed on the training data is optimistic; the cross-validated RMSE is the honest version (MF11).

SSE/SST is dimensionless ✓; \(\sqrt{\text{unit}^2} = \text{unit}\) ✓
AIC, AICc, BIC and Akaike weights(MF10)
\[ \text{AIC} = n\ln\frac{\text{SSE}}{n} + 2K, \quad \text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2K(K+1)}{n-K-1}, \quad \text{BIC} = n\ln\frac{\text{SSE}}{n} + K\ln n, \quad w_m = \frac{e^{-\Delta_m/2}}{\sum_j e^{-\Delta_j/2}} \]

each criterion adds a penalty for the number of estimated quantities \(K = p+1\) (the curve parameters plus the residual variance) to the lack of fit; the model with the lowest AICc is preferred, \(\Delta_m\) is each model's distance from it, and the Akaike weight \(w_m\) is the relative support for model \(m\) within the candidate set.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(K = p + 1\)estimated parameters including \(\sigma^2\)–
\(\Delta_m\)\(\text{AIC}_{c,m} - \min_j\text{AIC}_{c,j}\)–
\(w_m\)Akaike weight–
Follow the reasoning
  1. For Gaussian errors, \(-2\ln\hat L = n\ln(\text{SSE}/n)\) + a constant that is equal for all models fitted to the same data.
  2. Akaike (1974) showed that the in-sample \(-2\ln\hat L\) underestimates its expected value on new data by about \(2K\); adding \(2K\) corrects that optimism.
  3. Hurvich and Tsai (1989) derived the small-sample correction of AICc; use it whenever \(n/K < 40\) (Burnham & Anderson, 2002) — always, for grow-log data.
  4. BIC (Schwarz, 1978) penalises \(\ln n\) per parameter instead of 2, so with more than seven observations it prefers simpler models than AIC does.
  5. Rules of thumb: \(\Delta < 2\) substantial support, 4–7 considerably less, > 10 essentially none.
Assumptions and validity

Only differences between models fitted to exactly the same response and observations are meaningful — never compare the additive and proportional error models, or different “Fit to” selections. AICc ranks the models you supplied; if all are poor, it still picks one: check the residuals.

\(\ln(\text{SSE}/n)\) of a dimensional quantity adds a constant \(n\ln(\text{unit})\) that cancels in every \(\Delta\) ✓
Cross-validated prediction error(MF11)
\[ \text{RMSE}_{\text{CV}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\;\sum_{i\in F_j}\bigl[y_i - \hat f^{(-j)}(t_i)\bigr]^2} \]

split the observations into folds \(F_1\ldots F_k\); for each fold, refit the model without it (\(\hat f^{(-j)}\)) and predict the left-out points; the cross-validated RMSE is the root of the mean squared prediction error over all points.

Why cross-validation and AICc usually agree — and when they do not

For large samples leave-one-out CV and AIC are asymptotically equivalent ways of estimating the same out-of-sample error (Stone, 1977). They disagree when the data are structured: random folds ignore that the dates of one plant are correlated and that the future is not like the past. That is why the lab also offers the future and by-plant schemes (Roberts et al., 2017).

Polynomial regression and over-fitting(MF12)
\[ \hat W(t) = \sum_{m=0}^{d}\beta_m P_m(x), \qquad x = \frac{2(t-t_{\min})}{t_{\max}-t_{\min}}-1, \qquad p = d+1 \]

a polynomial of degree \(d\) is a weighted sum of \(d+1\) basis functions; the lab uses Legendre polynomials of the rescaled time \(x\) because they are nearly orthogonal on [−1, 1], which keeps the least-squares problem well conditioned up to degree 10.

The bias–variance trade-off in one sentence

Low degrees are biased (too stiff to follow the S-shape), high degrees have high variance (they follow the noise, and a different sample of plants would give a different wiggle); the expected prediction error, estimated by the cross-validated RMSE, is lowest in between (James et al., 2021). A three-parameter growth function has a built-in, biologically motivated shape — which is why it usually predicts better than any polynomial and extrapolates without exploding.

Basis functions are dimensionless, so \(\beta_m\) carry the unit of \(W\) ✓
Runs test for residual patterns(MF13)
\[ E[R] = \frac{2n_+n_-}{n}+1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[R] = \frac{2n_+n_-(2n_+n_- - n)}{n^2(n-1)}, \qquad z = \frac{R-E[R]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[R]}} \]

count the runs \(R\) — unbroken sequences of residuals with the same sign, in time order; if the signs were random, \(R\) would be close to its expected value; far too few runs (a large negative \(z\)) mean the curve is systematically too high in one period and too low in another.

the forecast day is the first time the fitted curve reaches the target; to find its uncertainty, simulate \(B\) new data sets from the fitted curve plus Gaussian noise with the residual SD, refit each one, compute its forecast day, and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those days.

Closed forms and the sensitivity to \(K\)
  1. Logistic: \(t^* = t_i - \ln(K/W^*-1)/r\). Gompertz: \(t^* = t_i - \ln[-\ln(W^*/K)]/r\). Exponential: \(t^* = \ln(W^*/W_0)/r\). The lab solves every model numerically by bisection, which works for all six.
  2. For the logistic, \(\partial t^*/\partial K = -1/[r(K-W^*)]\): when the target approaches the asymptote a small error in \(K\) moves the forecast by days — visible as a skewed bootstrap histogram.
  3. The bootstrap needs no derivatives and captures parameter correlations and non-linearity automatically (Efron & Tibshirani, 1994); it inherits the assumptions of the fitted model, including its shape.
Confidence band versus prediction band

The darker band shows where the mean curve may lie (uncertainty of the parameters). The lighter band adds the residual scatter and shows where a single new plant may lie. A grower planning a harvest of many plants cares about the first; a quality inspector about the second.

Model and method assumptions

  • Growth functions are empirical descriptions of size against time; only the expolinear curve is derived from a (simple) physiological mechanism. None of them contains climate: a change in light or temperature during the experiment shows up as a misfit.
  • Least squares assumes independent errors of constant variance (additive model) or constant coefficient of variation (proportional model). Repeated measurements of the same plants are correlated, so pooled fits understate the uncertainty; per-plant fits followed by a comparison of parameters are the sound alternative.
  • Standard errors come from the linearisation at the optimum (MF8) and can be far too small for poorly determined parameters; the bootstrap SE is shown for comparison. Parameters on a bound are flagged and their SEs are not meaningful.
  • Starting values and bounds: the self-starting rules and bounds are described in the Explain tab (see also models.js). A fit that stops at the iteration limit is flagged “slow”. Rarely, a different start may find a lower SSE — try Fit by eye followed by Levenberg–Marquardt.
  • Cross-validation: 5-fold uses a fixed random seed, so results are reproducible; leave-one-out is replaced by 5-fold for more than 60 observations to keep the page responsive. Failed refits during cross-validation predict the mean of the training data (a heavy but honest penalty).
  • Bootstrap: parametric, Gaussian noise with the fitted residual SD (log-normal for proportional errors), refitting from the original estimates; it assumes that the chosen model is correct.
  • Example data are either illustrative (leaf area, identical to Lesson 10.1) or simulated with a light-use-efficiency lettuce model: canopy photosynthesis of Van Henten (1994) at 20 °C, a cardinal-temperature factor (Yan & Hunt, 1999), interception by the projected leaf area via the illustrative calibration of Lesson 10.3, maintenance respiration, an RUE calibration factor of 1.3, 10–15 % plant-to-plant variation and 5–6 % measurement noise. They are realistic in shape and order of magnitude but are not measurements.
