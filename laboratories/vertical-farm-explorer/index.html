Vertical farm explorer

Step inside a plant factory with artificial lighting: rows of white racks, tier upon tier of lettuce glowing under LEDs, air ducts overhead and a plant room humming next door. Every number here comes from an annual balance of the building — photons to lettuce, electricity to heat, heat to cooling, transpired water to condensate, and money to kilograms. Change the number of tiers, the aisles, the light and the prices, and watch the building, the kilowatt-hours per kilogram and the cost per kilogram respond. Then walk through it.

What to look at first

  1. Take the tour. Use the Aisle, Tier close-up and Plant room buttons, then press Walk through the farm and walk down an aisle with W A S D (drag to look around). Each rack carries lettuce at four growth stages, from transplants at the west end to heads ready for harvest at the east end; a scissor lift reaches the upper tiers and an automated guided vehicle carries trays to packing. In the plant room, the air-handling units remove the heat of the LEDs and collect the transpired water as condensate.
  2. Stack the tiers. Move Growing tiers from 4 to 14. The building grows taller, the cultivation area and the yield per square metre of floor rise in proportion — but look at the Electricity per kg readout: it does not change. Stacking saves land, not energy.
  3. Turn up the light. Raise the PPFD from 150 to 350 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The LEDs glow brighter, the daily light integral and the yield increase, and so does the cooling load. Check the head weight and the DLI warning: lettuce above about 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ risks tip-burn.
  4. Follow the kilowatt-hours. In the Sankey diagram, only about 60 % of the electricity leaves the LEDs as light, most of the light is absorbed by leaves, and 2–4 % ends up as chemical energy in lettuce. The rest is heat that the HVAC removes — at the cost of more electricity.
  5. Move the farm. Compare the presets Swedish grid & price, Japanese PFAL, High-cost start-up and 2030 efficient. Which cost items change most, and why is electricity only 10–20 % of the cost?
  6. Watch day and night. The farm clock runs at half an hour per second: at 06:00 the LEDs switch on, and after the photoperiod they go out and only the work lights remain.
Key idea

A plant factory converts electricity into lettuce with an overall efficiency of only 1–3 %. Its advantages — tiny land and water use, year-round production, no pesticides — are real, but every kilogram carries a large and unavoidable energy bill (light), a heat-removal bill (HVAC) and, above all, fixed costs for labour and the building that only a high yield per square metre can dilute.

The plant factory as a balance of photons, heat, water and money

Kozai (2013) described the closed plant production system as a box with six components — an insulated, nearly airtight room, multi-tier racks, LED lamps, air conditioners, CO₂ supply and a nutrient-solution system — and defined an efficiency for each resource it uses. This laboratory follows that approach for one year of lettuce production.

Photons make lettuce: light-use efficiency

The single most important crop number is the light-use efficiency (LUE): grams of harvested dry matter per mole of photons reaching the crop. Jin et al. (2023) collected published data and found an average of 0.55 g mol⁻¹ for lettuce in vertical farms, 0.39 g mol⁻¹ in greenhouses and 0.23 g mol⁻¹ in the field, against a theoretical maximum of 1.26–1.81 g mol⁻¹. Kozai (2013) expressed the same idea as an energy ratio: 3.2–4.3 % of the photosynthetically active radiation reaching a plant community ends up as chemical energy in dry matter. With 20 MJ per kg of dry matter and ≈ 0.22 MJ per mole of PAR photons, that is 0.35–0.47 g mol⁻¹ — the same order of magnitude. Because lettuce is about 95 % water, 0.55 g DW mol⁻¹ becomes about 11 g of fresh lettuce per mole of photons.

Tiers save land, not energy

Stacking tiers multiplies the cultivation area on the same floor: the space-use efficiency is the cultivation area divided by the floor area. Every square metre of cultivation area, however, needs the same photons, so the electricity per kilogram is independent of the number of tiers. What stacking does change is the yield per square metre of land, and the capital and labour cost per kilogram if the building cost scales with floor area.

Every kilowatt-hour becomes heat

LEDs with a photon efficacy of 2.8 µmol J⁻¹ turn about 57 % of their electricity into PAR light (4.9 µmol J⁻¹ of radiant PAR); the rest is heat at the fixture. The photons are absorbed by leaves, walls and trays and also become heat — sensible heat that warms the air and latent heat carried by transpired water vapour — except for the 2–4 % stored as chemical energy in the crop. The HVAC must remove all this heat. With a cooling COP of 4 it needs one kilowatt-hour of electricity for every four kilowatt-hours of heat. In Tokyo, Kozai (2013) reported that lighting, air conditioning and other equipment used 85, 10 and 5 % of the electricity, with an annual mean COP of 5–6.

Water: condensate is the secret

In a sealed plant factory almost all transpired water condenses on the cooling coil and can be reused as irrigation water. Kozai (2013) gives a water-use efficiency of 0.95–0.98, against 0.02–0.03 for a ventilated greenhouse, where the vapour leaves through the vents. The net water use per kilogram is therefore dominated by the water that leaves in the product itself — about one litre per kilogram — compared with 20 L kg⁻¹ in a hydroponic greenhouse and 250 L kg⁻¹ in the field in Arizona (Barbosa et al., 2015).

The money

Zhuang et al. (2022) analysed the construction and operating costs of plant factories in Japan. Per square metre of plantable area and year, lettuce production cost about US$ 279 for labour, 166 for electricity, 86 for seeds and nutrients and 157 for packaging and logistics, on top of an investment of US$ 668–3409 per m² (US$ 1808 at a scale of 3000 m²). A 30 % fall in yield or price would push a typical factory into loss. Lovat et al. (2025) showed from first principles that current vertical farms need about 250 kWh per kg of dry matter — roughly 10 kWh per kg of lettuce — and that electricity alone makes dry staples such as wheat far too expensive to grow this way.

Common misconception

"With cheap renewable electricity, vertical farms become cheap." In the Swedish preset electricity is only about a tenth of the cost; labour and capital dominate. Cheap, clean power removes the carbon problem (the grid emission factor), but profitability depends at least as much on yield per square metre, automation and building cost.

Frontier 2024–2026

Research aims at higher light-use efficiency (dynamic spacing to keep the canopy closed, far-red and dynamic light recipes, cultivars bred for vertical farms), LEDs approaching 3.5–4 µmol J⁻¹, heat pumps that sell the waste heat to district-heating networks — attractive in Sweden — and automation to cut labour. Several high-profile vertical-farm companies went bankrupt in 2023–2025, a reminder that the economics in this laboratory are unforgiving.

Model assumptions

  • A 22 m × 14 m grow room with two 8.75 m × 1.25 m racks per row, tier pitch 0.45 m, 1.5 m end and cross aisles and 1.2 m side aisles; the plant room and other support areas are a chosen share of the building. The 3D farm shows the racks, tiers and LED intensity of the model; plants are drawn at a representative spacing.
  • Lettuce yield is proportional to the photons received (constant light-use efficiency, Jin et al. 2023). There is no response to CO₂, temperature or VPD, no tip-burn and no light saturation; a DLI above ≈ 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ is flagged.
  • All electricity used in the room becomes heat, apart from the chemical energy in harvested dry matter (17.5 kJ g⁻¹); envelope losses and gains are neglected; the HVAC works at a constant annual COP; auxiliaries are a fixed fraction of the lighting electricity.
  • Transpiration takes 60 % of the absorbed, non-stored light energy (as in Lesson 8.1); 4.9 µmol of PAR photons per joule of radiant PAR; 90 % canopy absorption.
  • Costs per m² of cultivation area and consumables per kg follow the Japanese data of Zhuang et al. (2022), converted at ≈ 0.92 € per US$; the start-up and 2030 presets change them as scenario assumptions, not data. Revenues, taxes, subsidies, heat sales and land costs are not included.
  • The Swedish greenhouse yield in the comparison is a light-based estimate: Västerås monthly PAR (NASA POWER climatology, as in the Sun-path laboratory) × 0.65 transmission, lamps topping up to 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (max. 180 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 18 h), LUE 0.39 g mol⁻¹ (greenhouse mean of Jin et al. 2023).
  • Grid emission factors are 2024 location-based life-cycle averages (Ember, via Our World in Data): Sweden 35 and EU-27 211 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹. They change from year to year, so use current national factors for real assessments.
