Lettuce digital twin

A lettuce crop grows under LEDs — and beside it grows its digital twin: an ensemble of Eldert van Henten's classic lettuce growth model, each copy slightly different. Every day at noon a camera photographs the tray. An ensemble Kalman filter uses the picture to correct the twin's state and to learn a parameter nobody knows exactly, and the twin forecasts the day on which the heads reach their harvest weight. Watch the forecast uncertainty shrink as data arrive, discover when a camera stops being informative, and see what it takes for a digital model to become a digital shadow and, finally, a true digital twin.

A guided tour of one crop

The simulation starts automatically at one day per second, with plants transplanted at 20 plants m⁻² under 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 16 h (a daily light integral of 11.5 mol m⁻² d⁻¹), 20 °C and 600 µmol mol⁻¹ CO₂.

  1. Two lettuces. On the left, the physical crop in a deep-water-culture tray. On the right, its digital twin: the cyan hologram is the ensemble's mean state, and each glowing dot on the glass gauge is one ensemble member, placed at its fresh weight as a fraction of the harvest target (amber ring). With the teacher view on, the green ring marks the truth.
  2. Noon. Every day at 12:00 the top camera flashes, a green stream of data flows to the twin, and the segmentation mask in the corner updates. The filter compares the measured and predicted structural dry weight and nudges every member towards the data.
  3. Learning. The physical crop's light-use efficiency differs from Van Henten's value by a hidden factor. Watch the magenta band in Learning the uncertain parameter close in on the green line during the first week, and the dots on the gauge bunch together.
  4. Forecasting. In How the harvest forecast converges the grey band (the model without data) stays wide, while the magenta band narrows around the true harvest day.
  5. Canopy closure. Around day 10–12 the heads touch: the projected area stops growing, and the pipeline rejects the images (ticks along the bottom of the growth chart, canopy closed in the mask). From now on the twin predicts without correction, and its uncertainty stops shrinking.
  6. A better sensor. Select the Weighing gutter and press Reset (the same physical twin is kept). Fresh weight stays informative until harvest, and the forecast band keeps narrowing.
  7. Model, shadow, twin. Choose Model: no data flow in and the twin's band equals the grey one. Choose Twin and a delivery day: now the twin also acts on the crop — a magenta stream flows from the twin to the lamps, the PPFD slider moves by itself every noon, and the crop is steered to its delivery date.
  8. Draw a new physical twin and repeat. Then try the other uncertain parameters: the leaf-area ratio (which also changes what the camera sees) and the maximum growth rate (which the camera reveals surprisingly well, although it hardly changes the harvest date).
Key idea

A mechanistic model tells you what should happen; measurements tell you what is happening, noisily and incompletely. Data assimilation combines the two, weighting each by its uncertainty. The result — a model that tracks one particular crop and knows how uncertain it is — is what makes a twin useful for decisions such as the harvest date.

Model, shadow or twin?

“Digital twin” is used loosely. Kritzinger et al. (2018) proposed a useful, strict classification based on how data flow between the physical object and its digital counterpart (Figure 1). A digital model exchanges no data automatically: it is set up by hand and run. A digital shadow receives data from the physical object automatically — its state follows reality — but acts on nothing. Only when data also flow back automatically, so that the digital object steers the physical one, do we speak of a digital twin. Reviews of agricultural “digital twins” find that many are, in this sense, models or shadows (Pylianidis et al., 2021), and that the added value lies in the combination of real-time data, a trustworthy model and the ability to act (Verdouw et al., 2021).

Common misconception

“A digital twin is a detailed 3D model.” The hologram in this laboratory is decoration. What makes the twin a twin is invisible: a validated process model, a stream of measurements, an estimate of uncertainty, and a feedback path to the real system. A crude model that is continuously corrected by data can be far more useful than a beautiful one that is not.

The crop model: structure and reserves

The twin's engine is the two-state lettuce model that Eldert van Henten developed for the optimal control of greenhouse climate (Van Henten, 1994; Van Henten & Van Straten, 1994). It divides the dry matter of the crop into two pools (Figure 2). Photosynthesis fills the non-structural pool \(X_n\) — sugars and starch. From it the plant builds the structural pool \(X_s\) — cell walls and cytoplasm — at a rate that grows with the reserve fraction and with temperature, and pays for maintenance and for the construction costs of new tissue. Leaf area, and thus light interception, is proportional to the structural pool. This feedback — more structure, more leaf area, more light caught, more sugar — produces the exponential early growth of a young crop; once the canopy closes, interception saturates and growth becomes nearly linear.

All parameters come from Table 3.2 of Van Henten's thesis: most from the crop-physiology literature, the structural leaf-area ratio \(c_{\text{lar,s}}\) by calibration on a greenhouse experiment with the cultivar ‘Berlo’, validated on a second experiment with ‘Norden’, where the model slightly underestimated growth — Van Henten suspected exactly that calibrated leaf-area ratio. Van Henten and Van Straten (1994) showed that growth in the model is controlled by only a handful of parameters: the yield factor, the light-use efficiency, the maximum growth rate, the extinction coefficient and the leaf-area ratio. These are the natural candidates for a twin to learn.

Using a model outside its calibration

Van Henten calibrated the model for Dutch winter greenhouses: low light and short days. Under the high, constant LED light of a plant factory it produces plausible growth rates, but its non-structural pool swells to 60–70 % of the dry weight in the first week — far more than the sugars measured in lettuce. That is a sign that conversion into structure (\(c_{\text{r,gr,max}}\)), rather than photosynthesis, limits growth in the model under these conditions. Every model has such a domain of validity; one of the jobs of a digital twin is to reveal where it ends.

Measuring growth without harvesting

A twin needs frequent data about the very crop it represents, so the measurement must be non-destructive. A top-view camera and colour segmentation (plant vision laboratory) give the projected leaf area and the fraction of the tray covered by leaves. In the model, the cover follows the same exponential law as light interception, so the image can be inverted into an estimate of structural dry weight. The inversion works beautifully for young, separate plants — and breaks down as the canopy closes: when 90 % of the tray is green, a 1 % error in the green area becomes a 4 % error in mass; at 99 % cover, the image says almost nothing (Eq. T6). This is a real limitation of top-view imaging (Zhang et al., 2020), which is why commercial systems add 3D cameras, re-space the plants or weigh them.

What a sensor cannot see, a twin cannot learn

In Van Henten's model, extra photosynthesis first fills the reserve pool; as long as reserves are plentiful, structure grows at a rate set by \(c_{\text{r,gr,max}}\) rather than by light. A top camera sees only structure. A crop whose light-use efficiency is 15–20 % below the model's can therefore look exactly like the model for a week or more — and sometimes the canopy closes before the difference becomes visible. Draw several physical twins and you will see the twin learn \(c_\varepsilon\) quickly for some and hardly at all for others. The weighing gutter, which feels reserves as well as structure, learns it within days, and the camera learns the leaf-area ratio — the parameter it literally looks at — very well. This is identifiability: whether data can pin down a parameter depends on what the sensors observe and on when they observe it.

Data assimilation with an ensemble Kalman filter

Imagine sixty versions of the crop, all started from slightly different transplant sizes and each with its own value of the uncertain parameter drawn from a prior distribution. The ensemble is the twin's uncertainty made concrete. Between measurements, every member is simply run forward with the model and its own random day-to-day variation, so the ensemble spreads (the forecast step). When a measurement arrives, the filter compares it with what each member predicts and shifts every member towards the data (the analysis step) by an amount set by the Kalman gain: large if the ensemble is uncertain and the measurement precise, small in the opposite case (Figure 3). Because the correlations inside the ensemble link the measured quantity to everything else, the analysis also corrects unmeasured variables — the reserve pool and the parameter. This is the ensemble Kalman filter (Evensen, 1994; Burgers et al., 1998), used for weather forecasting and for crop-yield forecasting alike (de Wit & van Diepen, 2007); treating a parameter as an extra state variable is called state augmentation (Evensen, 2009).

Two practical details matter. First, positive quantities such as masses are assimilated as logarithms, so that a 10 % error means the same at 1 g and at 100 g. Second, an ensemble that has learned a parameter tends to become over-confident and stop learning (filter collapse); a small random walk of the parameter keeps it alert, and also lets it follow a parameter that drifts, for example as the crop ages.

From state to decision: forecasting and steering

To forecast the harvest, the twin runs every member forward — with its own state, its own parameter and fresh random weather-like variation — until its fresh weight reaches the target. The distribution of these first-passage times is the harvest forecast, and its width tells the grower how far ahead a sale can be promised. In Twin mode the laboratory closes the loop: every noon the twin searches for the light intensity that brings its median forecast onto the delivery day, and the lamps follow. It is a simple predictive controller — the same idea as the MPC of the climate controller laboratory — and it shows the twin's economic value: when the crop is ahead of schedule, the twin dims the lamps and saves electricity.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Research groups and vertical-farm companies are coupling crop models, 3D imaging and data assimilation into operational twins, and autonomous-greenhouse competitions have shown that algorithms can grow complete crop cycles in research-scale greenhouse compartments (96 m²) with little human intervention (see Lesson 10.4 and Lesson 10.5). The open problems are less about algorithms than about trustworthy models, calibrated sensors, data ownership and knowing when a twin is wrong.

The equations behind the twin

Equations T1–T5 are Van Henten's two-state lettuce model exactly as published (thesis Eqs. 3.10–3.21, parameters of Table 3.2), with the radiation input written as PAR at the canopy instead of outside global radiation. They are integrated per square metre of ground with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (steps ≤ 1 h, split at lamp switching). Equations T6–T9 describe the measurements, the filter and the forecasts.

\[ \frac{\mathrm dX_n}{\mathrm dt} = c_\alpha\,\varphi_{\text{phot}} - r_{\text{gr}}X_s - \varphi_{\text{resp}} - \frac{1-c_\beta}{c_\beta}\,r_{\text{gr}}X_s,\qquad \frac{\mathrm dX_s}{\mathrm dt} = r_{\text{gr}}X_s \]

Ensemble Kalman filter analysis (state augmentation)(T8)
\[ \mathbf z^{(i)}=\bigl[\ln X_n^{(i)},\ \ln X_s^{(i)},\ \ln m^{(i)}\bigr]^{\top},\qquad \mathbf K=\frac{\operatorname{cov}\bigl(\mathbf z,h(\mathbf z)\bigr)}{\operatorname{var}\bigl(h(\mathbf z)\bigr)+R} \] \[ \mathbf z^{(i)}_{\text{a}}=\mathbf z^{(i)}+\mathbf K\left(y+\sqrt R\,\epsilon^{(i)}-h\bigl(\mathbf z^{(i)}\bigr)\right),\qquad \epsilon^{(i)}\sim\mathcal N(0,1) \]

each member's augmented state is moved towards the measurement by the gain times the difference between a perturbed copy of the measurement and what the member predicts; the gain is the ensemble covariance between the state and the predicted observation divided by the total variance of the predicted observation plus the measurement error.

Follow the derivation for one variable
  1. For a Gaussian prior \(\mathcal N(\mu_p,P)\) and a measurement \(y\) with variance \(R\) of the same quantity, Bayes' rule gives a Gaussian posterior with mean \(\mu_p + K(y-\mu_p)\), \(K = P/(P+R)\), and variance \((1-K)P = PR/(P+R)\) (Kalman, 1960).
  2. The EnKF estimates \(P\) and the cross-covariances with unobserved variables from the ensemble. A variable that is correlated with the predicted observation (such as the parameter, through its effect on growth) is corrected in proportion to that correlation.
  3. Perturbing the measurement for each member (Burgers et al., 1998) makes the posterior ensemble spread match the theoretical posterior variance.
  4. Early in the crop the parameter and the predicted measurement are only weakly correlated (the members have had little time to diverge); the correlation, and hence the learning rate, increases over the first days.
Assumptions and validity

Linear update, Gaussian errors in log space, a scalar observation per day, no localisation or covariance inflation beyond the parameter random walk. With small ensembles the sampled covariances are noisy and the filter can become over-confident.

\[ t_h^{(i)}=\min\Bigl\{t\ge t_k:\ \text{FW}_{\text{head}}\bigl(\mathbf x^{(i)}(t)\bigr)\ge \text{FW}^\ast\Bigr\} \] \[ \hat t_h=\operatorname{median}_i\,t_h^{(i)},\qquad \text{PI}_{90}=\bigl[q_{0.05},\,q_{0.95}\bigr] \] \[ \operatorname{median}_i\,t_h^{(i)}(\text{PPFD}_k)=D^\ast \] \[ 60\le\text{PPFD}_k\le 500,\qquad \bigl|\text{PPFD}_k-\text{PPFD}_{k-1}\bigr|\le 60 \]

each member is run forward until its fresh weight per head reaches the target; the median of these first-passage times is the forecast harvest day and their 5th and 95th percentiles bound the 90 % prediction interval. In twin mode, the PPFD for the coming day is chosen so that the median forecast equals the delivery day \(D^\ast\).

Follow the reasoning
  1. The forecast integrates the members with today's climate held constant and with fresh random daily factors, so the interval contains state, parameter and future-weather uncertainty.
  2. The median forecast is a decreasing function of PPFD, so the equation for the light level is solved by bisection on a subset of 24 members (deterministic, to keep it smooth). The change is limited to ±60 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ per day, a rate constraint that avoids stressing the crop and keeps the controller from over-reacting to one uncertain forecast.
  3. This is a receding-horizon controller: only tomorrow's light level is applied, and the plan is recomputed after the next measurement.
Assumptions and validity

The controller ignores electricity prices, light-use efficiency changes with PPFD and quality effects such as tipburn at high light. The prediction interval is only as good as the model and the noise assumptions: with the camera after canopy closure, it no longer shrinks.

Investigation tasks

  1. How fast does the twin learn?

    With the default settings, note the day on which the 90 % band of the learned parameter first becomes narrower than ±10 % of its mean, and the harvest-forecast error on days 5, 10 and 15. Repeat for three different physical twins. Is the parameter learned faster when the hidden deviation is large? Relate the learning speed to the growth stage and to Eq. T8.

  2. When does the camera go blind?

    For plant densities of 10, 20 and 30 plants m⁻², find the day on which the canopy closes (the first rejected image). Use Eq. T6 to compute the error of the dry-weight estimate at 50 %, 80 % and 90 % cover. Then compare the width of the harvest-forecast interval on day 18 with the camera and with the weighing gutter (σ = 4 %). What would you advise a vertical farm that plans to rely on top-view imaging?

  3. How many members are enough?

    Run the same physical twin with N = 10, 20, 60 and 200. Compare the final parameter estimate, the width of the forecast interval and whether the true harvest day lies inside it. What goes wrong with very small ensembles, and how does the parameter random walk help or hurt?

  4. Which parameters can be learned?

    Repeat task 1 for the leaf-area ratio and the maximum growth rate. Which parameter is learned fastest, and which matters most for the harvest date? Compare your ranking with Van Henten and Van Straten's (1994) sensitivity analysis. Why is the leaf-area ratio special for a camera-based twin (hint: Eq. T6)?

  5. Model, shadow, twin

    Keep one physical twin and run it three times: as a digital model, as a digital shadow and as a digital twin with a delivery day of 30. Tabulate the harvest-forecast error on day 10, the actual harvest day and the lamp electricity per kilogram. Then set the delivery day to 26 and to 34: what does punctuality cost or save in electricity? Which of the three modes would you call a digital twin in a funding proposal, and why?

    Photograph your own lettuce plants from above every day for a week, measure the projected leaf area (for example in the plant vision laboratory) and harvest a few plants at the end. Fit the relation between cover and dry weight and compare it with Eq. T6. Which of the twin's assumptions does your crop violate?

Model assumptions

  • The crop model is Van Henten's (1994) two-state lettuce model with all parameters from Table 3.2 of the thesis (root fraction 0.07 for NFT-grown lettuce; leaf-area ratio read as 75 m² kg⁻¹). The initial dry weight (2.7 g m⁻² at 20 plants m⁻², 75 % structural) is taken from his first experiment and scaled with plant density.
  • Radiation enters as PAR at the canopy from constant LEDs, converted with the daylight factor 4.57 µmol J⁻¹; the model was calibrated for greenhouse sunlight. At plant-factory light levels the model accumulates an unrealistically large reserve pool (see Explain).
  • Temperature, CO₂ and light are uniform and exactly known to the twin; plants are identical; there is no tipburn, bolting, disease or nutrient and water limitation.
  • The physical crop is generated with the same model structure (identical-twin experiment) but a hidden parameter multiplier, a different transplant size and daily multiplicative noise on photosynthesis. Real crops also differ in structure, which makes real twins less accurate than this one.
  • The camera measures cover with log-normal segmentation error; the pipeline inverts Van Henten's cover law with the nominal leaf-area ratio and rejects images above 95 % cover. The weighing gutter measures fresh weight per head with log-normal error and no water or substrate artefacts.
  • The filter is a stochastic ensemble Kalman filter with perturbed observations on log-transformed states and parameter, a parameter random walk and no other inflation or localisation. The prior of the open-loop “model without data” ensemble is the same as the twin's.
  • Forecasts hold today's climate constant; the closed-loop controller chooses a PPFD between 60 and 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ by bisection, changes it by at most 60 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ per day and ignores prices and quality effects. Lamp electricity assumes 2.8 µmol J⁻¹.
  • The 3D scene is illustrative: plant size follows the simulated fresh weight, the hologram follows the ensemble mean, and the dots on the gauge follow the members.
