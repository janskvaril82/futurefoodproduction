Spectral signatures

Every satellite and drone image used in precision agriculture starts here: with the way leaves, soils, water and snow reflect sunlight between 400 and 2500 nm. In this laboratory the crop spectra are not drawings — they are computed from leaf chemistry with the PROSPECT-D leaf model and a physically based canopy model, so you can add chlorophyll, dry the leaves or thin the canopy and watch the green peak, the red edge and the water bands respond. Overlay the bands of Sentinel-2, Landsat, a multispectral drone camera or an ordinary RGB camera, and see exactly what each sensor “sees” and which vegetation indices it can compute.

Indices of the healthy canopy as its leaf area index grows, for the selected sensor. NDVI tracks how much ground is covered and flattens above LAI ≈ 2–3; red-edge and EVI-type indices keep responding for longer.

A guided tour of the spectrum

  1. Read the healthy crop from left to right. Hover over the thick green curve. In the blue (450 nm) and the red (670 nm) reflectance is only 2–4 %: chlorophyll absorbs almost all of it. Between them is the small green peak (≈ 8 % at 550 nm) that makes plants look green to us. At ≈ 700–740 nm the curve shoots up — the red edge — to a NIR plateau of 50–60 %. Further right, deep troughs at 1450 and 1940 nm and shallower ones at 970 and 1200 nm are the fingerprints of liquid water in the leaves.
  2. Remove chlorophyll. Drag Cab from 45 to 15 µg cm⁻². The red well fills in, the green peak widens into yellow and the red edge moves to shorter wavelengths (watch the red-edge inflection point readout drop by ≈ 20 nm). The NIR plateau hardly moves: NIR reflectance is controlled by leaf structure, not pigments.
  3. Dry the leaves. Drag Cw from 0.015 to 0.004 cm. Only the SWIR changes: reflectance at 1600 and 2200 nm rises and the NDWI readout falls. NDVI does not notice — a drought-stressed crop can look perfectly “green” to NDVI until it wilts.
  4. Thin the canopy. Set LAI to 0.5. The canopy spectrum drifts towards the soil spectrum: the red rises, the NIR falls and NDVI drops sharply. Now raise LAI step by step and watch the first chart: NDVI climbs quickly and then saturates, while NDRE and EVI keep rising.
  5. Switch sensors. Choose Landsat 8/9 OLI: the red-edge bands disappear and NDRE shows “—”. Choose the RGB camera: without a NIR band none of the classic indices can be computed. Choose the Mavic 3M: its red-edge band at 730 nm sits higher up the red edge than Sentinel-2's B5, so the same canopy gives a lower NDRE — indices from different sensors are not interchangeable.
  6. Mix a pixel. Set f = 0.3 and compare the dashed mixed-pixel spectrum with the canopy and the soil. Change the soil from dry to wet and see the mixed-pixel NDVI change even though the crop is identical: the soil background is part of every crop signal until the canopy closes.
  7. Look at water and snow. Clear water is dark everywhere and almost black beyond 750 nm; add sediment (bbp) and it brightens in the green and red. Fresh snow reflects ≈ 99 % of visible light but almost nothing at 1500–1650 nm; coarse, old snow is darker in the NIR.
Key idea

A reflectance spectrum is a chemical and structural fingerprint. Pigments write in the visible, cell structure in the NIR and water and dry matter in the SWIR. A sensor with a few bands reads only a few letters of that fingerprint — so choosing a sensor means choosing which crop properties you will be able to see.

How a leaf treats sunlight

Light entering a leaf meets two kinds of obstacles. Absorbers — chlorophylls and carotenoids in the chloroplasts, anthocyanins in vacuoles, water everywhere and the dry matter of the cell walls — remove photons at wavelengths that match their electronic or vibrational transitions. Scatterers — the thousands of interfaces between hydrated cell walls (refractive index ≈ 1.4) and the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll (1.0) — change the direction of photons without absorbing them (Knipling, 1970; Gates et al., 1965). What leaves the leaf upwards is reflected; what leaves downwards is transmitted.

This division gives the three spectral domains every remote-sensing scientist learns:

From a leaf to a canopy

A canopy is a pile of leaves. Photons that pass through one leaf may be reflected back up by the next, and so on: in the NIR, where leaves absorb only about 5 % of what hits them, this multiple scattering makes a dense canopy brighter than a single leaf. In the red, where a leaf absorbs 95 %, the canopy is darker than one leaf and quickly becomes opaque. The laboratory uses the classic two-stream (Kubelka–Munk) description of this process for horizontal leaves (Allen & Richardson, 1968; Eq. S3): a downward and an upward flux exchange photons with the leaves in every thin layer, and the soil reflects at the bottom.

Two consequences drive much of precision agriculture. First, the soil background is part of every crop signal until the canopy closes: the same crop gives a different NDVI over a wet, dark soil than over a dry, bright one. Second, saturation: once the red reflectance has fallen to ≈ 2 %, extra leaves hardly change it, so NDVI flattens above LAI ≈ 2–3 (Baret & Guyot, 1991; Carlson & Ripley, 1997). Indices built on the red edge or on the NIR difference (NDRE, EVI) stay sensitive for longer, which is why tractor-mounted nitrogen sensors and the Sentinel-2 red-edge bands matter for nitrogen management in dense cereal crops.

Soils, water and snow

Soils rise gently from the blue to the SWIR; iron oxides make them reddish, organic matter darkens them, and moisture darkens them at all wavelengths while adding water-absorption troughs at 1450 and 1940 nm. The laboratory uses the two measured reference soils distributed with the PROSAIL model and mixes them linearly with a moisture parameter (Jacquemoud et al., 2009). Water absorbs strongly beyond ≈ 600 nm, so clear lakes are dark blue-green and almost black in the NIR — the reason NDVI is negative over water. Suspended sediment backscatters light and brightens the green–red, while coloured dissolved organic matter (the brown humic substances typical of Swedish forest lakes) absorbs in the blue (Eq. S9). Snow is the brightest natural surface in the visible, but ice absorbs in the NIR and SWIR; the larger the grains, the longer the path through ice and the darker the snow beyond 900 nm (Eq. S8). This SWIR darkness is how satellites tell snow from clouds.

What a sensor sees

A sensor does not record the whole spectrum but its average over each band's spectral response (Eq. S5). Band positions and widths differ between sensors, so the “same” index is not identical across them.

Common misconception

“A higher NDVI always means a healthier crop.” NDVI measures green cover and, above LAI ≈ 3, barely changes. A lush weed patch, a crop with plenty of leaves but too little nitrogen for grain filling, or a water-stressed crop that has not yet wilted can all have high NDVI. Interpret an index only together with the crop stage, the soil and — ideally — a second, differently sensitive index.

The equations behind the spectra

The crop spectra are computed at 421 wavelengths (every 5 nm) from Eqs. S1–S3; soils are measured spectra; water and snow follow Eqs. S8–S9; the sensor values and indices follow Eqs. S5–S7.

Absorption in one elementary leaf layer (PROSPECT)(S1)
\[ k(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i C_i\,K_i(\lambda),\qquad \tau(\lambda)=(1-k)\,e^{-k}+k^{2}\,E_1(k),\qquad E_1(k)=\int_k^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\,\mathrm{d}x \]

the absorption thickness of one of the N elementary layers of a leaf is the sum, over all absorbing constituents, of their content per leaf area times their specific absorption coefficient, divided by N; the fraction of diffuse light that crosses the layer without being absorbed is given by the exponential-integral formula τ.

\(C_{ab}, C_{car}, C_{anth}\)chlorophyll a+b, carotenoid, anthocyanin content per leaf areaµg cm⁻²45, 10, 0
\(C_w\)equivalent water thickness (water mass per area ÷ density)cm0.015
\(C_m\)dry matter per leaf area (leaf mass per area)g cm⁻²0.005
\(K_i(\lambda)\)specific absorption coefficient of constituent i (published PROSPECT-D tables)cm² µg⁻¹, cm⁻¹, cm² g⁻¹\(K_{ab}(670)=0.066\)

Follow the derivation of τ
  1. Beer–Lambert: a ray crossing the layer at angle θ from its normal travels a path \(1/\cos\theta\) times the thickness and is transmitted with probability \(e^{-k/\cos\theta}\).
  2. Inside a leaf light is nearly isotropic (diffuse). For isotropic radiance, the flux crossing a plane from directions around \(\mu=\cos\theta\) is proportional to \(\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu\), normalised by \(\int_0^1 2\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mu=1\).
  3. Average the transmission over this distribution: \(\tau = 2\int_0^1 \mu\,e^{-k/\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\). With \(s=1/\mu\): \(\tau=2\int_1^\infty s^{-3}e^{-ks}\,\mathrm{d}s = 2E_3(k)\), the third exponential integral.
  4. Integrate by parts twice (recurrence \(nE_{n+1}(k)=e^{-k}-kE_n(k)\)): \(2E_3(k)=(1-k)e^{-k}+k^2E_1(k)\). ✓
  5. Constituents absorb independently, so their absorption thicknesses add: \(k=\sum_i C_iK_i/N\). Worked number at 670 nm with the default leaf: \(k=(45\times0.0661+10\times0+0.015\times0.0041+0.005\times2.3)/1.5=1.99\), \(E_1(1.99)=0.0495\), \(\tau=(1-1.99)e^{-1.99}+1.99^2\times0.0495=0.061\): a single elementary layer already absorbs 94 % of red light.
Assumptions and validity

Absorbers are uniformly distributed within each layer and scattering happens only at the layer surfaces. The specific absorption coefficients were calibrated by inverting PROSPECT against hundreds of measured leaves (Féret et al., 2017), so they are “effective” in-vivo coefficients, not the absorption of extracted pigments in solvent. Brown pigments are in arbitrary units.

\(C_{ab}K_{ab}\): \(\mu\text{g cm}^{-2}\times\text{cm}^2\,\mu\text{g}^{-1}=1\); \(C_wK_w\): \(\text{cm}\times\text{cm}^{-1}=1\); \(C_mK_m\): \(\text{g cm}^{-2}\times\text{cm}^2\text{g}^{-1}=1\) — k is dimensionless ✓
A leaf as a pile of plates (Allen–Stokes)(S2)
\[ R_1=r_{\alpha}+\frac{t_{\alpha}\,t_{21}\,r_{21}\,\tau^2}{1-r_{21}^2\tau^2},\qquad T_1=\frac{t_{\alpha}\,t_{21}\,\tau}{1-r_{21}^2\tau^2} \] \[ R=R_1+\frac{T_1\,R_{N-1}\,t}{1-R_{N-1}\,r},\qquad T=\frac{T_1\,T_{N-1}}{1-R_{N-1}\,r} \]

the first layer reflects what its top surface reflects plus light that crosses the layer, bounces off the bottom surface and comes back out; it transmits what survives crossing both surfaces and the absorbing interior, with all repeated internal reflections summed. The remaining N − 1 layers, stacked below, add their reflectance and transmittance through the same bookkeeping.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(t_\alpha, r_\alpha=1-t_\alpha\)transmissivity/reflectivity of the leaf surface for light arriving within a cone of 40° (Stern 1964; Allen 1973)–
\(t_{12}, t_{21}=t_{12}/n^2\)transmissivity of the surface for isotropic light, outside→inside and inside→outside–
\(r_{21}=1-t_{21}\)internal reflectivity (includes total internal reflection)–
\(n(\lambda)\)refractive index of the leaf material (≈ 1.51 at 400 nm, 1.29 at 2200 nm)–
\(r, t\)reflectance/transmittance of one inner elementary layer (isotropic light on both sides)–
\(R_{N-1}, T_{N-1}\)reflectance/transmittance of a pile of N − 1 such layers (Stokes 1862)–
\(R, T\)reflectance and transmittance of the whole leaf–
Follow the derivation
  1. Light entering through the top surface (probability \(t_\alpha\)) crosses the layer (\(\tau\)) and meets the bottom surface: it leaves with probability \(t_{21}\) or is reflected back (\(r_{21}\)).
  2. Every round trip inside the plate multiplies the surviving light by \(r_{21}^2\tau^2\). Summing the geometric series \(1+r_{21}^2\tau^2+r_{21}^4\tau^4+\dots=1/(1-r_{21}^2\tau^2)\) gives \(T_1\).
  3. The reflected part is the direct surface reflection \(r_\alpha\) plus light that went down, was reflected at the bottom (\(r_{21}\tau\)) and escaped upwards — again summed over all internal bounces.
  4. Stokes (1862) solved the pile of identical plates in closed form: with \(\Delta=\sqrt{(1+r+t)(1+r-t)(1-r+t)(1-r-t)}\), \(a=(1+r^2-t^2+\Delta)/(2r)\) and \(b=(1-r^2+t^2+\Delta)/(2t)\), a pile of M plates has \(R_M=a(b^{2M}-1)/(a^2b^{2M}-1)\) and \(T_M=b^M(a^2-1)/(a^2b^{2M}-1)\). N need not be an integer.
  5. Finally the top layer and the pile are combined, again summing the inter-reflections between them (denominator \(1-R_{N-1}r\)).
Assumptions and validity

The leaf is a stack of identical, flat, absorbing plates separated by air — a caricature of the mesophyll that works remarkably well: PROSPECT reproduces measured leaf spectra from 400 to 2500 nm to within a few per cent reflectance (Jacquemoud & Baret, 1990; Féret et al., 2017). It ignores leaf-surface gloss direction (specular reflection), hairs and wax, and assumes the leaf is laterally uniform.

All quantities are fractions of incident flux — dimensionless ✓. Energy conservation: \(R+T+A=1\) with absorptance \(A\ge0\) (see the “Inside one leaf” chart).
Canopy reflectance (Kubelka–Munk two-stream)(S3)
\[ R_c=\frac{1-R_s\left(a-b\coth(b\,r\,L)\right)}{a-R_s+b\coth(b\,r\,L)},\qquad a=\frac{1-t}{r},\quad b=\sqrt{a^2-1},\qquad R_\infty=a-b \]

the reflectance of a canopy of leaf area index L lying on a soil of reflectance Rs depends on the leaf reflectance r and transmittance t through the two combinations a and b; as the canopy gets deeper the hyperbolic cotangent tends to one and the reflectance tends to the value R∞ of an infinitely deep canopy.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical (red / NIR)
\(r, t\)leaf reflectance and transmittance at λ (from PROSPECT)–0.036, 0.004 / 0.46, 0.49
\(L\)leaf area index (one-sided leaf area per ground area)m² m⁻²0–8
\(R_s\)soil reflectance–0.24 / 0.31
\(R_c\), \(R_\infty\)canopy reflectance; its limit for a deep canopy–0.018 / 0.56 at L = 3
Follow the derivation
  1. Let \(E_\downarrow\) and \(E_\uparrow\) be the downward and upward diffuse fluxes at cumulative leaf area \(L'\) below the canopy top. A layer \(\mathrm{d}L'\) of horizontal leaves intercepts a fraction \(\mathrm{d}L'\) of each flux.
  2. Of the intercepted downward flux, a fraction t continues downwards, r is sent upwards and \(1-r-t\) is absorbed. Hence \(\mathrm{d}E_\downarrow/\mathrm{d}L'=-(1-t)E_\downarrow+rE_\uparrow\) and \(-\mathrm{d}E_\uparrow/\mathrm{d}L'=-(1-t)E_\uparrow+rE_\downarrow\).
  3. These are the Kubelka–Munk equations with scattering coefficient \(S=r\) and absorption coefficient \(K=1-r-t\), so \((K+S)/S=(1-t)/r=a\).
  4. Solve with the boundary condition \(E_\uparrow=R_sE_\downarrow\) at \(L'=L\): the standard solution (Kubelka, 1948) is the equation above, with \(\coth\) arising from the \(e^{\pm bSL}\) terms.
  5. Check the limits: \(L\to0\) gives \(\coth\to\infty\) and \(R_c\to R_s\) (bare soil) ✓; \(L\to\infty\) gives \(\coth\to1\) and \(R_c\to 1/(a+b)=a-b=R_\infty\) ✓ (since \((a+b)(a-b)=1\)).
  6. Worked number in the NIR: \(r=0.459\), \(t=0.491\): \(a=0.509/0.459=1.109\), \(b=\sqrt{1.109^2-1}=0.479\), \(R_\infty=0.630\) — higher than the leaf's 0.459, because photons transmitted by the top leaves are reflected back by lower ones. In the red, \(r=0.036\), \(t=0.004\): \(R_\infty=0.018\), lower than one leaf.
Assumptions and validity

Leaves are horizontal, randomly placed and small compared with the canopy; light is diffuse; there is no specular reflection, no shadow hot-spot and no clumping into rows. Real canopies with inclined leaves, stems, ears and shadows reflect 10–30 % less in the NIR than this idealised canopy, and their reflectance depends on the sun and view angles (models such as SAIL treat this; Jacquemoud et al., 2009). The qualitative behaviour — soil influence at low LAI, saturation at high LAI — is robust.

\(a\), \(b\), \(rL\) and \(R\) are all dimensionless (L is m² m⁻²) ✓
Linear mixing and the NDVI of a mixed pixel(S4)
\[ \rho_{\text{pix}}=f\,\rho_v+(1-f)\,\rho_s \] \[ \text{NDVI}_{\text{pix}}=w\,\text{NDVI}_v+(1-w)\,\text{NDVI}_s,\qquad w=\frac{f\,\Sigma_v}{f\,\Sigma_v+(1-f)\,\Sigma_s} \]

NDVI and the normalised-difference family(S6)
\[ \text{ND}(i,j)=\frac{\rho_i-\rho_j}{\rho_i+\rho_j} \] \[ \text{NDVI}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{red}),\quad \text{NDRE}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{RE}) \] \[ \text{GNDVI}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{green}),\quad \text{NDWI}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{SWIR}) \]

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\rho_i, \rho_j\)band-averaged reflectances (Eq. S5)–
NDnormalised difference, between −1 and +1–
Why normalise, and why these pairs?
  1. A common multiplicative factor k (uneven illumination, sensor gain) cancels: \((k\rho_i-k\rho_j)/(k\rho_i+k\rho_j)=\text{ND}(i,j)\).
  2. NDVI pairs the strongest chlorophyll absorption (red) with the strongest scattering (NIR): maximal contrast between vegetation and soil.
  3. NDRE replaces the red by a red-edge band where chlorophyll absorbs less, so light penetrates deeper and the index keeps responding to chlorophyll at high LAI. GNDVI does the same with green.
  4. NDWI (Gao, 1996) pairs two bands that are both scattered by the canopy structure but of which only the SWIR one (≈ 1610 nm) is absorbed by water — so it tracks canopy water content.

Spectral albedo of clean snow(S8)
\[ A(\lambda)=\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{\gamma(\lambda)\,a_{\text{opt}}}\right),\qquad \gamma=\frac{4\pi\chi(\lambda)}{\lambda} \] \[ a_{\text{opt}}=P^2a_{\text{ef}},\qquad r(\mu_0)=A^{\frac{3}{7}(1+2\mu_0)} \]

Where it comes from
  1. Snow is a weakly absorbing, strongly scattering medium. In asymptotic radiative-transfer theory the albedo can be written as an average over the number of scattering events m of the survival probability \(e^{-\beta m}\), where β is the probability of absorption per event.
  2. Random-walk theory gives the distribution of m, and the average evaluates to \(A=\exp(-\text{const}\sqrt{\beta})\) (Kokhanovsky, 2013).
  3. For large ice grains, β is proportional to the absorption coefficient of ice times the grain size: bigger grains mean longer paths through ice between scattering events, so \(\beta\propto\gamma a_{\text{ef}}\) and \(A=\exp(-\sqrt{\gamma P^2 a_{\text{ef}}})\).
  4. Worked number at 1030 nm, \(a_{\text{ef}}=150\) µm: \(\gamma=4\pi\times2.33\times10^{-6}/1.03\times10^{-6}=28.4\) m⁻¹, \(a_{\text{opt}}=6.3^2\times150\times10^{-6}=5.95\times10^{-3}\) m, \(A=\exp(-\sqrt{0.169})=0.66\).
Assumptions and validity

Clean, deep, dry snow; impurities (soot, dust, algae) that darken snow in the visible are ignored; the snow's angular reflection pattern is replaced by the plane albedo for a 45° sun.

\(\gamma a_{\text{opt}}\): \(\text{m}^{-1}\times\text{m}=1\) ✓
Reflectance of optically deep water (approximation)(S9)
\[ \rho_w(\lambda)\approx\rho_{\text{surf}}+t_s\,f\,\frac{b_b}{a+b_b} \] \[ a=a_w(\lambda)+a_g(440)\,e^{-0.014(\lambda-440)},\qquad b_b=b_{bw}(\lambda)+b_{bp}(550)\frac{550}{\lambda} \]

water reflects a little at its surface and returns from below a fraction of light that grows with backscattering and falls with absorption; absorption is the sum of pure water and coloured dissolved organic matter, backscattering the sum of water molecules and suspended particles.

Assumptions and validity

This is a deliberately simple parametric approximation, not a full radiative-transfer model: the bottom is invisible (deep water), phytoplankton absorption is not included separately, and the interface factor ts lumps together refraction and internal reflection (literature values 0.4–0.55). It is good enough to show why water is dark in the NIR, why humic lakes look brown and why turbid rivers are bright in the red — not for water-quality retrievals.

Model assumptions

  • Leaves are computed with PROSPECT-D using the published refractive index and specific absorption coefficients (Féret et al., 2017), sampled every 5 nm. The leaf is laterally uniform and has no specular surface reflection.
  • Canopies are horizontal-leaf, turbid-medium canopies under diffuse light (Kubelka–Munk two-stream; Allen & Richardson, 1968). Leaf angles, clumping in rows, stems and ears, shadows, the hot-spot and the sun–view geometry are ignored; the NIR plateau is therefore 10–30 % brighter than in many field measurements.
  • Stressed and senescent canopies share the healthy canopy's LAI and soil and differ only in leaf traits (Table 2); in reality stress also reduces LAI.
  • Soils are the two measured reference spectra of the PROSAIL distribution mixed linearly with a moisture parameter and scaled by a brightness factor; other mineralogies or organic contents are not represented.
  • Water is a parametric approximation for optically deep water (Eq. S9) based on the absorption of pure water, CDOM and particle backscattering; phytoplankton pigments and the bottom are ignored.
  • Snow is clean, deep and dry (Eq. S8); impurities and liquid water are ignored.
  • Sensors are represented by top-hat responses of the stated width (Gaussian for the illustrative RGB camera) without solar-irradiance weighting; spectra are surface reflectances (no atmosphere). Indices therefore correspond to atmospherically corrected (Level-2A) data.
