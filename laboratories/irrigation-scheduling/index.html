Irrigation scheduling (FAO-56)

When should you irrigate, and how much? Here you run the same daily root-zone water balance that irrigation advisers use worldwide — FAO Paper 56 — for potato, spring wheat or lettuce, with a full season of realistic weather for Västerås or Almería (or your own weather data). Watch the soil profile dry out and the crop wilt, then let a calendar, a soil-moisture sensor, a weather-forecast model or a deficit strategy decide — and compare how much water each one uses, how much drains below the roots and how much yield it saves.

What to look at first

  1. Play a rainfed season. Choose the preset Västerås potato on sand, set the strategy to Rainfed and press ▶ under the soil profile. After planting the roots are shallow, so the soil “bucket” (right) is small. Watch the depletion line climb during dry spells: when it passes RAW the plants droop, turn olive and the actual evapotranspiration falls below the crop's demand.
  2. Let a sensor decide. Switch to Soil-moisture sensor threshold. Every time the sensor reports that the depletion has reached RAW, the sprinkler refills the root zone. The depletion now saw-tooths between 0 and RAW and the stress days almost vanish.
  3. Try the calendar. Choose Calendar: 25 mm every 7 days, whatever the weather. On this sandy soil (TAW ≈ 45 mm at full rooting) much of the water is lost as drainage — see the cumulative chart and the strategy table. Rain that falls just after an irrigation has nowhere to go.
  4. Use the forecast. Choose Water-balance model + weather forecast. The scheduler looks three days ahead and skips an irrigation when rain is forecast. Compare its water use and drainage with the sensor strategy in the table.
  5. Go south. Load Almería winter lettuce, drip. The season is short and mostly rainless; drip irrigation (90 % efficient) carries the whole crop. Then load Almería spring potato, deficit: the crop is deliberately allowed into the stress zone and the yield loss is computed with the FAO-33 yield-response factor.
  6. Change the weather year. Move Weather year: every seed is a different, statistically realistic season. Rainfed yield in Västerås varies a lot from year to year — that variability is what makes irrigation an insurance.
Key idea

The root zone is a bucket: rain and irrigation fill it, evapotranspiration empties it, and anything above field capacity drains away. Good scheduling keeps the depletion between zero and RAW — irrigating when the crop needs it (timing) and only as much as the bucket can hold (amount).

The FAO-56 water balance in five steps

The FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper No. 56 (Allen et al., 1998) is the international standard for computing crop water requirements. Its single crop-coefficient method, used here, combines five pieces (see also Lesson 11.3):

  1. Weather → ET₀. The Penman–Monteith equation turns temperature, humidity, wind and solar radiation into the evapotranspiration of a standard, well-watered grass (Eq. I1). Solar and net radiation are estimated from sunshine duration (Eq. I2).
  2. Crop → ETc. A crop coefficient Kc, changing over four growth stages, scales ET₀ to the crop (Eq. I3). Tabulated values come from FAO-56 Tables 11 and 12 and are adjusted for wind and humidity.
  3. Soil and roots → TAW and RAW. The total available water is the water held between field capacity and wilting point over the rooting depth; the readily available water is the part the crop can extract without stress (Eq. I4). Roots grow, so the bucket grows.
  4. Daily bookkeeping. Every day, rain and irrigation reduce the depletion and evapotranspiration increases it; water beyond field capacity drains (Eqs. I5–I6). When the depletion exceeds RAW, a stress coefficient Ks reduces transpiration.
  5. Yield. The seasonal shortfall of ETa relative to ETc translates into a relative yield loss through the yield-response factor Ky (Eq. I9; Doorenbos & Kassam, 1979).

The weather

Irrigation decisions are made in real weather: dry spells, heat waves, rainy weeks. The laboratory therefore does not use average weather but a stochastic weather generator (Eq. I8; Richardson, 1981; Wilks & Wilby, 1999) whose statistics were estimated from 20 years (2004–2023) of daily data: precipitation at the SMHI station Västerås (96350) and, for all other variables and for Almería, the NASA POWER data set. The generator reproduces the monthly mean temperatures to within about 1 °C, the monthly radiation and ET₀ to within about 5 % and the annual precipitation to within about 10 % (small daily amounts below 1 mm are not generated). Each weather year is a different plausible season — which is what a farmer faces.

Common misconception

“More irrigation is always safer.” Once the root zone is at field capacity, extra water simply drains — carrying nitrate towards groundwater and the Baltic Sea — and every millimetre was pumped and paid for. On sandy soils with small TAW, frequent light irrigations beat large ones.

Frontier

Irrigation advisory services now combine FAO-56 balances with satellite estimates of crop coefficients and evapotranspiration, and with probabilistic weather forecasts. Variable-rate irrigation (VRI) centre pivots apply different depths to different management zones — the water equivalent of the variable-rate nitrogen in the Drone NDVI survey.

The equations behind the simulation

FAO-56 Penman–Monteith reference evapotranspiration(I1)
\[ ET_0=\frac{0.408\,\Delta\,(R_n-G)+\gamma\,\dfrac{900}{T+273}\,u_2\,(e_s-e_a)}{\Delta+\gamma\,(1+0.34\,u_2)} \] \[ e_s=\frac{e^\circ(T_{\max})+e^\circ(T_{\min})}{2},\qquad e_a=e^\circ(T_{\text{dew}}) \]

reference evapotranspiration combines an energy term (net radiation) and an aerodynamic term (wind times vapour pressure deficit), weighted by the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve and the psychrometric constant.

Derivation and check

The derivation from the Penman–Monteith combination equation with the standard grass resistances (rs = 70 s m⁻¹, ra = 208/u2) is given step by step in Lesson 11.3 (Eq. 11.3.1). The code uses the shared physics.et0PenmanMonteith function. Check: for FAO-56 Example 18 (Brussels, 6 July: Tmax 21.5 °C, Tmin 12.3 °C, u2 2.08 m s⁻¹, 9.25 h sunshine, 50.8° N, 100 m) the laboratory computes ET₀ = 3.88 mm d⁻¹; FAO-56 gives 3.9 mm d⁻¹ ✓.

Assumptions and validity

Daily time step with G = 0; the weather is assumed to be measured over a well-watered grass reference. Over dry surroundings (Almería in summer) measured air is warmer and drier than a true reference site, and FAO-56 recommends correcting Tdew; the generator's statistics come from gridded data rather than a reference station.

\(\dfrac{\text{kPa}\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}^{-1}\cdot\text{MJ m}^{-2}\text{d}^{-1}\cdot\text{mm MJ}^{-1}\text{m}^2}{\text{kPa}\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}^{-1}}=\text{mm d}^{-1}\) ✓ (0.408 = 1/λ with λ = 2.45 MJ kg⁻¹; 900 carries the unit conversions of the aerodynamic term)
Net radiation from sunshine duration(I2)
\[ R_s=\left(a_s+b_s\frac{n}{N}\right)R_a,\qquad R_{so}=(0.75+2\cdot10^{-5}z)R_a \] \[ R_n=(1-\alpha)R_s-R_{nl} \] \[ R_{nl}=\sigma\,\frac{T_{\max,K}^4+T_{\min,K}^4}{2}\left(0.34-0.14\sqrt{e_a}\right)\left(1.35\frac{R_s}{R_{so}}-0.35\right) \]

global radiation is a fraction of the extraterrestrial radiation that grows with the relative sunshine duration; net radiation is the absorbed shortwave radiation minus the net longwave loss, which is larger in warm, dry and clear weather.

The Ångström coefficients vary with climate and should be calibrated locally where pyranometer data exist. If only temperatures are available (pasted data), Rs = 0.16 √(Tmax − Tmin) Ra (Hargreaves' radiation formula, FAO-56 eq. 50, interior locations).

Crop coefficient curve and climate adjustment(I3)
\[ ET_c=K_c\,ET_0,\qquad K_{c}(i)=K_{c,\text{prev}}+\frac{i-\sum L_{\text{prev}}}{L_{\text{stage}}}\left(K_{c,\text{next}}-K_{c,\text{prev}}\right) \] \[ K_{c,\text{mid}}=K_{c,\text{mid}}^{\text{tab}}+\left[0.04(u_2-2)-0.004(RH_{\min}-45)\right]\left(\frac{h}{3}\right)^{0.3} \]

the crop's evapotranspiration without stress is the crop coefficient times ET₀; the coefficient is constant in the initial and mid-season stages and changes linearly in the development and late stages; the tabulated mid- and end-season values are corrected for climates windier or drier than the standard (u₂ = 2 m s⁻¹, RHmin = 45 %).

\(L_{\text{ini}},L_{\text{dev}},L_{\text{mid}},L_{\text{late}}\)stage lengths (FAO-56 Table 11)d
\(RH_{\min}\)mean minimum relative humidity, \(100\,e^\circ(T_{\text{dew}})/e^\circ(T_{\max})\), over the stage%

Assumptions and validity

The end-season value is adjusted only if the tabulated Kc end ≥ 0.45 (FAO-56 eq. 65), so not for dry-harvested wheat. Kc ini is taken from the tables; FAO-56 recommends raising it when the soil surface is wetted often (its Figs. 29–30), which the single-coefficient approach here ignores. In Västerås the correction is about −0.03 (calm, humid); in Almería about +0.01 to +0.04.

\(K_c\) dimensionless; \(ET_c\) in mm d⁻¹ ✓
Root growth, TAW and RAW(I4)
\[ Z_r(i)=Z_{r,\min}+\left(Z_{r,\max}-Z_{r,\min}\right)\min\!\left(1,\frac{i}{L_{\text{ini}}+L_{\text{dev}}}\right) \] \[ \text{TAW}=1000(\theta_{FC}-\theta_{WP})Z_r \] \[ \text{RAW}=p\,\text{TAW},\qquad p=p_{\text{tab}}+0.04(5-ET_c) \]

the rooting depth grows linearly from 0.15 m at planting to its maximum at the start of mid-season; the total available water is the water held between field capacity and wilting point over that depth; the readily available water is the fraction p of it, larger on days of low demand.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\theta_{FC},\theta_{WP}\)volumetric water content at field capacity and wilting point (FAO-56 Table 19 ranges)m³ m⁻³

Assumptions and validity

A uniform, single-layer root zone. When the roots deepen they are assumed to enter soil at field capacity, so the depletion in millimetres is unchanged while TAW grows. Linear root growth to the start of mid-season is a common simplification; FAO-56 does not prescribe the shape.

Daily root-zone water balance and water-stress coefficient(I5)
\[ D_{r,i}=D_{r,i-1}-P_{e,i}-I_i+ET_{a,i}+DP_i,\qquad ET_{a}=K_s\,K_c\,ET_0 \] \[ K_s=\begin{cases}1 & D_{r,i-1}\le\text{RAW}\\ \dfrac{\text{TAW}-D_{r,i-1}}{(1-p)\,\text{TAW}} & D_{r,i-1}>\text{RAW}\end{cases} \]

today's depletion is yesterday's minus effective rain and net irrigation, plus actual evapotranspiration and any drainage; transpiration is reduced linearly once the depletion at the start of the day exceeds RAW, reaching zero at TAW.

Follow the derivation
  1. Conservation of water in the root zone: \(\Delta S=P_e+I+CR-ET_a-DP-RO\). Capillary rise CR and runoff RO are set to zero (deep water table, flat field).
  2. Depletion is the deficit below field capacity, \(D_r=S_{FC}-S\), so \(\Delta D_r=-\Delta S\) — the equation above (FAO-56 eq. 85).
  3. Between RAW and TAW the water still available, TAW − Dr, shrinks from (1 − p)TAW to 0; Ks is that fraction (FAO-56 eq. 84).
  4. Worked number: loamy sand (θFC 0.15, θWP 0.06), Zr = 0.5 m: TAW = 45 mm; p = 0.35 → RAW = 15.75 mm. With Dr = 30 mm: Ks = (45 − 30)/(0.65 × 45) = 0.51 — the crop transpires half its demand.
every term in mm (per day) ✓
Effective rain and deep percolation(I6)
\[ P_e=\begin{cases}P & P\ge0.2\,ET_0\\ 0 & P<0.2\,ET_0\end{cases},\qquad DP_i=\max\!\left(0,\;P_{e,i}+I_i-ET_{a,i}-D_{r,i-1}\right) \]

small showers of less than a fifth of the day's ET₀ wet only the surface and evaporate; water in excess of what the root zone can hold drains below it the same day.

Assumptions and validity

Both rules follow FAO-56 Chapter 8: “Daily precipitation in amounts less than about 0.2 ET₀ is normally entirely evaporated and can usually be ignored”, and water above field capacity is assumed lost the same day by deep percolation. Runoff is ignored (flat field, infiltration faster than rainfall); on sloping or crusted soils heavy rain would partly run off.

Scheduling rules and gross irrigation(I7)
\[ \begin{aligned}&\text{sensor: } \hat D_r=1000(\theta_{FC}-\hat\theta)Z_r,\quad \hat\theta=\theta+b+\varepsilon\\ &\qquad I=\min(I_{\max},\hat D_r)\ \text{if}\ \hat D_r\ge f\,\text{RAW}\\ &\text{model: } D_{r}+\textstyle\sum_{k<H}\left(K_c\,\widehat{ET}_{0,k}-\hat P_{e,k}\right)\ge f\,\text{RAW}\\ &\qquad\Rightarrow\ I=\min\!\left(I_{\max},D_r+K_c\widehat{ET}_{0}-\hat P_{0}-\hat P_{1}\right)\\ &\text{deficit: } D_r\ge\text{RAW}+\delta(\text{TAW}-\text{RAW})\ \Rightarrow\ I=\min(I_{\max},\phi\,D_r)\\ &I_{\text{gross}}=I/E_a\end{aligned} \]

the sensor strategy converts a measured water content (with bias b and random error ε) into a depletion and refills when it reaches the trigger; the model strategy projects the depletion over the forecast horizon H and refills minus the rain forecast for today and tomorrow; the deficit strategy waits until the crop is part of the way into the stress zone and refills only a fraction φ; the water pumped is the net depth divided by the application efficiency.

All strategies stop irrigating a chosen number of days before harvest and irrigate at most once a day. The forecast error model is illustrative, not calibrated against a real forecasting system. Stochastic weather generator(I8)
\[ \Pr(\text{wet}_i\mid\text{dry}_{i-1})=p_{WD},\qquad \Pr(\text{wet}_i\mid\text{wet}_{i-1})=p_{WW},\qquad P_i\sim\Gamma(k,\theta) \] \[ \chi_i=\rho\,\chi_{i-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\varepsilon_i,\qquad X_i=\mu_{X}^{\text{wet/dry}}+\sigma_X\,\chi_i \]

whether it rains today depends on whether it rained yesterday (a two-state Markov chain); wet-day amounts are drawn from a gamma distribution; the standardised departures of maximum temperature, minimum temperature and sunshine follow correlated first-order autoregressive processes around means that differ between wet and dry days.

Yield response to water and water productivity(I9)
\[ 1-\frac{Y_a}{Y_m}=K_y\left(1-\frac{\sum ET_a}{\sum ET_c}\right),\qquad \text{IWP}=\frac{(Y_a-Y_{a,\text{rainfed}})\cdot1000}{10\,\sum I_{\text{gross}}} \]

the relative yield loss equals the yield-response factor times the relative shortfall of seasonal evapotranspiration; irrigation water productivity is the extra yield in kilograms per cubic metre of water pumped.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator to answer. Save investigation stores the settings and readouts; Download season (CSV) gives you every daily value for your own analysis.

  1. Anatomy of a season

    Load Västerås potato on sand with the sensor strategy. Play the season and note, for each growth stage, the Kc, the root depth and the TAW. Explain why irrigations are small and frequent early in the season and larger later. Check one day's balance by hand with Eq. I5, using values from the CSV download.

  2. Calendar or sensor?

    On the loamy sand, compare the calendar (25 mm every 7 days) with the sensor strategy for weather years 1–5. Tabulate irrigation, drainage and relative yield. Then change the soil to silt loam and repeat. On which soil is the calendar least wasteful, and why (think TAW)?

  3. A bad sensor

    With the sensor strategy, set the bias to +0.02 m³ m⁻³ (reads too wet), then to −0.02 (reads too dry). What happens to the number of irrigations, the drainage and the yield on sand and on silt loam? How large an error in θ corresponds to RAW on each soil? What does this tell you about sensor calibration (Lesson 9.1)?

  4. What is a forecast worth?

    Compare Model + forecast with horizons of 1, 3 and 7 days against the sensor strategy, for five weather years in Västerås. Does looking further ahead save water, and when does it start to cost yield because forecast rain did not come?

  5. Deficit irrigation in Almería

    Load Almería spring potato, deficit. Vary δ from 0 to 0.8 and φ from 0.5 to 1. Plot relative yield against gross irrigation. Where is the water productivity highest? Would you recommend deficit irrigation for potato (Ky = 1.1)? Compare with the argument of Fereres & Soriano (2007) that deficit irrigation suits crops and stages with Ky < 1.

  6. Your own weather

    Download daily data for a station near you (e.g. SMHI open data, or your own weather station from the course project), paste them below the charts and run your crop. How much irrigation would it have needed in that season?

Model assumptions

  • FAO-56 single crop-coefficient method with a daily time step; soil evaporation after wetting is lumped into Kc (no dual coefficient), Kc ini is tabulated rather than computed from wetting frequency.
  • A single, homogeneous root-zone layer; roots deepen linearly into soil at field capacity; no capillary rise, no runoff, no salinity; excess water drains the same day.
  • Irrigation is applied at the start of the day and infiltrates completely; application losses (1 − Ea) are counted in the gross water but not added to the drainage.
  • The weather generator reproduces the 2004–2023 statistics of the two sites (Eq. I8), not any particular year or a future climate; small rain amounts (< 1 mm) are not generated. Pasted data are used as given.
  • The forecast error model, sensor errors, maximum irrigation depths and minimum interval are illustrative assumptions.
  • Yield follows the seasonal FAO-33 relation (Eq. I9) without stage sensitivity or quality effects; the lettuce Ky comes from a single study.
  • The soil profile is a visualisation of the bucket model; real soils have layers and wetting fronts.
