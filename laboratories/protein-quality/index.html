Protein quality calculator

Is a pea-protein burger as good as beef for a growing child? Can bread and peas together beat either alone? Mix up to four proteins from a database of 17 ingredients — legumes, cereals, potato and rapeseed, mycoprotein, spirulina, insects, egg, milk and beef — whose amino acids and ileal digestibilities were measured in published studies. The amino-acid flower shows, petal by petal, how far each indispensable amino acid covers the FAO reference pattern; the calculator returns DIAAS, PDCAAS, the limiting amino acid, how much of the blend a person would have to eat, and searches thousands of blends for the best complementary combination.

What to look at first

  1. Read the flower. The default blend is 76 % whole-grain wheat and 24 % pea protein concentrate by mass. Each of the nine petals is one indispensable amino acid (sulfur amino acids, SAA = methionine + cysteine, and aromatic amino acids, AAA = phenylalanine + tyrosine, are counted together, as FAO requires). The glowing ring is 100 % of the reference pattern: a petal that reaches it supplies, per gram of protein, as much of that amino acid as the reference person needs. The shortest petal — outlined in red — is the first-limiting amino acid, and its length is the DIAAS in the centre.
  2. Remove the peas. Set ingredient 2 to none. Wheat alone has a stunted lysine petal (≈ 45 % for a young child) while its sulfur-amino-acid petal overshoots the ring. Now choose pea for ingredient 1 alone: the picture flips — plenty of lysine, too little SAA (DIAAS ≈ 62). This is the classic cereal–legume complementarity.
  3. Blend them back and move the slider. Watch the stacked colours in each petal: the lysine petal is mostly pea-coloured, the SAA petal mostly wheat-coloured. The complementation chart shows the lysine and SAA lines crossing; the best blend sits near their crossing — but a third amino acid, tryptophan, caps the score at about 90.
  4. Change the reference pattern. Switch from 6 mo–3 y to Older/adult. Every petal lengthens, because older children and adults need less of every indispensable amino acid per gram of protein; the same blend now scores about 100. DIAAS always refers to a specific age group.
  5. Look at the dashed outline. It shows the amino acids that were eaten; the coloured petal shows what was absorbed by the end of the small intestine. Try rapeseed meal, crickets or mealworms: the gap is large. For egg, milk and beef it is almost invisible.
  6. Check the quantity. Open the bar chart How much would you have to eat?. Maize, rice and wheat need roughly 1–2 kg per day to cover the quality-adjusted protein need of a 70 kg person, because they are both low in protein and low in lysine. Protein quality and protein quantity are different things.
Key idea

Protein quality is decided by the scarcest digestible indispensable amino acid relative to need — Liebig's barrel applied to nutrition. Because each ingredient is short of different amino acids, a blend is scored after summing the digestible amino acids on a protein basis. That is why blends can beat every one of their ingredients, and why the best plant-only blends reach the scores of animal proteins.

Common misconception

"Plant proteins are incomplete." Every ingredient in the database contains all nine indispensable amino acids; none has a petal of zero. What differs is the proportion of each amino acid and how much is digested — which is exactly what this laboratory measures.

From amino acids to a single quality score

The body cannot make nine of the twenty amino acids fast enough: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Two dispensable amino acids are made from indispensable ones (cysteine from methionine, tyrosine from phenylalanine), so requirements are set for the sums SAA and AAA. Protein synthesis needs all of them at the same time; surplus amino acids cannot be stored and are oxidised. A protein therefore feeds growth and repair only as far as its scarcest indispensable amino acid allows.

The reference patterns

FAO (2013) turned the WHO/FAO/UNU (2007) amino-acid requirements into three scoring patterns — the amount of each amino acid a gram of "ideal" protein must contain for the person to meet all needs when eating exactly the required amount of protein. For regulatory claims, FAO recommends the young-child pattern for all foods except infant formula, because it is the most demanding one after infancy.

Digestibility: ileal, not faecal

Measuring ileal digestibility in people requires ileostomy patients or stable-isotope methods, so FAO (2013) recommends true ileal digestibility "preferably determined in humans, but if this is not possible, in growing pigs or in growing rats in that order". Fifteen of the seventeen ingredients in the database were measured in cannulated growing pigs (twelve in single studies, three as means of the INRA–CIRAD–AFZ feed tables); spirulina comes from a 15N-labelling study in rats, and mycoprotein from a standardised in vitro digestion (INFOGEST). Their scores are therefore less certain, which the Assumptions & sources tab states explicitly.

DIAAS versus PDCAAS

PDCAAS (FAO/WHO, 1991) multiplies the amino-acid score of the undigested protein by one faecal crude-protein digestibility and truncates the result at 1.0. DIAAS (FAO, 2013) multiplies each amino acid by its own ileal digestibility before taking the minimum, and is not truncated when a single ingredient is scored. FAO (2013) adds that DIAAS values above 100 % "should not be truncated except where calculating DIAAS for protein or amino acid intakes for mixed diets or sole source foods". The simulator follows that rule: DIAAS is shown untruncated, but the protein need uses min(1, DIAAS/100). Where amino-acid-specific digestibility is unavailable, FAO allowed faecal crude-protein digestibility to be applied to all amino acids as an interim measure — exactly the PDCAAS shortcut.

Claims

FAO (2013) suggested DIAAS cut-offs for protein claims: no quality claim below 75, "good" (source) quality for 75–99 and "excellent/high" quality from 100. A food must first qualify on protein quantity (e.g., 10 % of the 50 g reference value per 100 g); quality cannot substitute for quantity.

Complementation and the protein basis

Cereals are short of lysine but rich in sulfur amino acids; legumes are the reverse; potato protein is well balanced; insects and mycoprotein are most often limited by sulfur amino acids. Because scores are defined per gram of protein, a blend must be computed on a protein basis: 50 % wheat and 50 % pea protein concentrate by mass is only 18 % wheat protein, because the concentrate contains 4.7 times more protein per gram. The mass sliders therefore drive a protein-share calculation, shown in the flower's side panel.

The optimiser exploits a simple geometric fact. As the share of one ingredient rises, every amino-acid ratio changes linearly (Eq. P4); the DIAAS is the lowest of nine straight lines, a concave "tent" whose peak lies where two lines cross — or on a third, flat line such as tryptophan in wheat–pea blends. With three ingredients the lines become planes and the landscape a triangle, which the DIAAS-landscape chart draws in full.

Quality meets quantity

The quality-adjusted protein need divides the requirement by min(1, DIAAS/100): a protein with DIAAS 50 must be eaten in double amount to deliver the same limiting amino acid. Dividing by the protein content of the ingredient converts protein to grams of food. Scored with the adult pattern, rice (9 % protein, DIAAS 64) then needs about 1.0 kg per day for a 70 kg adult at 0.83 g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹; soy protein isolate (93 % protein, DIAAS 98) needs about 64 g.

Frontier 2024–2026

Growing-pig DIAAS databases have expanded rapidly (Stein, 2024), mixed-meal DIAAS has been shown to be additive in pigs (Fanelli et al., 2022, 2024), and human dual-isotope methods are being used to measure amino-acid digestibility directly in people. Standardised in vitro digestion (INFOGEST) is being tested as a cheaper screen for novel proteins such as mycoprotein, but in vitro scores still need validation against in vivo data (Ariëns et al., 2026).

The equations behind the calculator

Amino-acid content per gram of protein(P1)
\[ c_i = 1000\,\frac{a_i}{\text{CP}}, \qquad c_{\text{SAA}} = c_{\text{Met}} + c_{\text{Cys}},\quad c_{\text{AAA}} = c_{\text{Phe}} + c_{\text{Tyr}} \]

the content of amino acid \(i\) in milligrams per gram of protein equals a thousand times its concentration in the food divided by the crude-protein concentration, both on the same basis; methionine and cysteine, and phenylalanine and tyrosine, are added.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a_i\)analysed amino acid in the food (as fed or dry matter)g per 100 g
\(\text{CP}\)crude protein, nitrogen × 6.25, same basis as \(a_i\)g per 100 g

Assumptions and validity

All scores use the conventional nitrogen-to-protein factor 6.25. Insects contain nitrogen in chitin and mycoprotein in chitin, glucosamine and RNA; with a lower factor (e.g., 5.33 for insects) the "protein" shrinks and every \(c_i\) rises. Malla et al. (2022) report both; the database uses 6.25 so that all ingredients are compared on the same basis.

(g per 100 g) / (g per 100 g) × 1000 mg g⁻¹ = mg g⁻¹ ✓

\[ c^{\text{dig}}_i = d_i\,c_i, \qquad c^{\text{dig}}_{\text{SAA}} = d_{\text{Met}}\,c_{\text{Met}} + d_{\text{Cys}}\,c_{\text{Cys}} \]

the digestible content equals the content multiplied by that amino acid's ileal digestibility; for the sums SAA and AAA, each member is multiplied by its own digestibility before adding.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Of \(c_i\) milligrams eaten per gram of protein, a fraction \(d_i\) has been absorbed by the end of the ileum.
  2. Standardised digestibility adds back the amino acids the gut secretes regardless of diet (basal endogenous losses), so it describes the food, not the experiment, and is additive in mixtures (Stein, 2024).
  3. Cysteine is usually less digestible than methionine (e.g., pea concentrate 0.75 vs 0.90), so SAA must be summed after weighting, not before.

From mass shares to protein shares(P3)
\[ w_k = \frac{m_k\,x_k}{\sum_j m_j\,x_j}, \qquad x_{\text{mix}} = \frac{\sum_j m_j\,x_j}{\sum_j m_j} \]

the share of the blend's protein that comes from ingredient \(k\) is its mass times its protein content, divided by the total protein; the protein content of the blend is total protein over total mass.

Digestible amino acids of a blend(P4)
\[ c^{\text{dig}}_{\text{mix},i} = \sum_k w_k\,c^{\text{dig}}_{k,i} \]

the digestible content of amino acid \(i\) per gram of blend protein is the protein-share-weighted sum of the ingredients' digestible contents.

Follow the derivation
  1. Take 1 g of blend protein. Ingredient \(k\) supplies \(w_k\) grams of it.
  2. Those \(w_k\) grams carry \(w_k c^{\text{dig}}_{k,i}\) mg of digestible amino acid \(i\).
  3. Add over the ingredients. The result is linear in the shares, so every amino-acid ratio in the complementation chart is a straight line.
Assumptions and validity

Digestibility is assumed additive: an ingredient is digested the same way alone and in a meal. Fanelli et al. (2022, 2024) found no difference between measured and predicted DIAAS of combined meals in pigs, which supports the assumption for cooked mixed meals. Interactions (e.g., antinutritional factors from one ingredient reducing the digestibility of another) are ignored.

– × mg g⁻¹ = mg g⁻¹ ✓
Digestible indispensable amino-acid score(P5)
\[ \text{DIAAS} = 100\,\min_{i}\ \frac{c^{\text{dig}}_{\text{mix},i}}{r_i} \]

DIAAS is one hundred times the smallest ratio between the digestible content of an indispensable amino acid and the same amino acid in the reference pattern; that amino acid is the first-limiting one.

Why PDCAAS and DIAAS differ
  1. One digestibility for all amino acids hides poorly digested ones (lysine damaged by heat, cysteine in legumes).
  2. Faecal digestibility counts nitrogen used by colonic bacteria as "digested", overestimating absorption — Mathai et al. (2017) found pig PDCAAS-like values above DIAAS for pea, soy and wheat.
  3. Truncation at 1 hides the surplus of high-quality proteins that can compensate for poorer ones in a meal.
  4. In the simulator PDCAAS is scored against the same pattern as DIAAS; the original 1991 method used the FAO/WHO/UNU (1985) pre-school child pattern.

Quality-adjusted protein need and mass of blend(P7)
\[ P_{\text{need}} = \frac{m_{\text{body}}\,r_P}{\min\!\left(1,\ \text{DIAAS}/100\right)}, \qquad M_{\text{blend}} = \frac{P_{\text{need}}}{x_{\text{mix}}} \]

the protein a person must eat equals body mass times the protein requirement, divided by the DIAAS as a fraction (capped at one); the mass of blend is that protein divided by the blend's protein content.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(m_{\text{body}}\)body masskg
\(r_P\)protein requirement for high-quality protein (0.66 average, 0.83 safe level for adults)g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹
\(P_{\text{need}}\)quality-adjusted protein intakeg d⁻¹
\(M_{\text{blend}}\)mass of blend to eat per dayg d⁻¹
Assumptions and validity

The whole day's protein is assumed to come from the blend. The FAO scoring patterns are the amino-acid requirements divided by the protein requirement, so dividing the protein need by DIAAS is equivalent to meeting the need for the limiting amino acid. A DIAAS above 100 cannot reduce the need below the protein requirement itself — hence the cap. Energy, fibre and satiety limits (can anyone eat 2 kg of maize a day?) are not considered.

(kg × g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹)/– = g d⁻¹; (g d⁻¹)/(g g⁻¹) = g d⁻¹ ✓
The best two-way blend: where the limiting lines cross(P8)
\[ w^{*} = \frac{b_2/r_2 - b_1/r_1}{(a_1 - b_1)/r_1 - (a_2 - b_2)/r_2} \]

for two ingredients A (share \(w\)) and B (share \(1-w\)) whose limiting amino acids are 1 and 2, the protein share of A that maximises the lower of the two ratios is found by setting the two ratios equal.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a_1, a_2\)digestible contents of amino acids 1 and 2 in Amg g⁻¹
\(b_1, b_2\)the same in Bmg g⁻¹
\(w^{*}\)optimal protein share of A–
Follow the derivation
  1. Ratio 1 is \(\rho_1(w) = [w a_1 + (1-w) b_1]/r_1\), ratio 2 is \(\rho_2(w) = [w a_2 + (1-w) b_2]/r_2\) (Eq. P4 with two ingredients).
  2. If A is rich in amino acid 2 but poor in 1, \(\rho_1\) falls and \(\rho_2\) rises with \(w\); the minimum of a falling and a rising line is largest where they meet.
  3. Setting \(\rho_1 = \rho_2\) and solving the linear equation for \(w\) gives \(w^*\).
  4. With nine amino acids the true optimum is the peak of the lower envelope of nine lines — a small linear programme. Herreman et al. (2020) solved it with a spreadsheet solver; this laboratory uses an exhaustive grid search, which is simpler to understand and fast enough for 17 ingredients.
Worked example — wheat and pea concentrate, young-child pattern
  1. Digestible Lys: wheat 25.7, pea 72.4 mg g⁻¹; digestible SAA: wheat 34.3, pea 16.8 mg g⁻¹; \(r_{\text{Lys}} = 57\), \(r_{\text{SAA}} = 27\).
  2. \(w^* = (16.8/27 - 72.4/57)/[(25.7-72.4)/57 - (34.3-16.8)/27] = (0.622 - 1.270)/(-0.819 - 0.648) = 0.442\): 44 % of the protein from wheat.
  3. Both ratios then equal \([0.442\times34.3 + 0.558\times16.8]/27 = 0.91\).
  4. But digestible tryptophan is about 7.6 mg g⁻¹ in both ingredients (ratio ≈ 0.90 whatever the mix), so the blend's DIAAS is capped at ≈ 90 by tryptophan. In mass terms: \(0.442/0.1167 : 0.558/0.5446 = 3.7 : 1.0\), i.e. about 79 % wheat — compare the optimiser.

  2. Quality is not quantity

    For a 70 kg adult (0.83 g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹, older-child/adult pattern), record the grams per day needed for rice, whole-grain wheat, pea concentrate, soy isolate, egg and beef.   3. Design an excellent plant-only blend

    Set the optimiser to plant ingredients only, 3 components. Find a blend with DIAAS ≥ 100 for young children. Then switch the objective to fewest grams per day. Do the two objectives agree? Propose a real food (bread, pasta, drink, burger) that could be made from your blend and discuss taste, texture and cost.

  4. New proteins in the mix

    Crickets, mealworms and mycoprotein are mainly limited by sulfur amino acids. Which single ingredient from the database complements each of them best? Compare the resulting DIAAS with egg. Then read the data notes for these three ingredients: which result would you trust least, and why?

  5. Digestibility is a design variable

Model assumptions

  • Scores follow FAO (2013): nine indispensable amino acids with SAA and AAA summed, age-specific scoring patterns (Table 1 of the Explain tab), amino-acid-specific ileal digestibility, crude protein = N × 6.25.
  • Blends are scored on a protein basis (Eqs. P3–P4) and digestibility is assumed additive (no interactions between ingredients), which pig studies of mixed meals support (Fanelli et al., 2022, 2024).
  • Each ingredient is one specific product from one study (or a feed-table mean). Real products vary with cultivar, processing and cooking in digestible amino acids: across published pig datasets, Herreman et al. (2020) found DIAAS ranges of 53, 43 and 44 points within wheat, rapeseed and maize, respectively. Treat the scores as representative, not as properties of every product with that name.
  • Pig standardised ileal digestibility is used as a proxy for human true ileal digestibility (FAO, 2013; Stein, 2024). Exceptions: spirulina (rats; one mean digestibility, 82.8 %, applied to all amino acids) and mycoprotein (in vitro INFOGEST digestion; composition back-calculated from digestible amounts assuming a uniform 79 % digestibility). These two are the least certain entries.
  • Rapeseed is represented by defatted rapeseed meal (feed grade), the form on which most published digestibility data are based; food-grade rapeseed protein isolates are much more digestible when heat-treated (Stein, 2024).
  • PDCAAS uses a faecal crude-protein digestibility per ingredient: pig total-tract values where available, human values from FAO/WHO/UNU (1985) for rice, oats, maize, egg and beef, the rat value for spirulina, and — for insects, where faecal data were not reported — the ileal digestibility of crude protein as a proxy. PDCAAS is scored against the same pattern as DIAAS.
  • Reactive lysine (lysine damaged by Maillard reactions during heating) is not distinguished from total lysine; heat-processed products could therefore be overestimated.
  • The protein need uses one requirement per kilogram of body mass for the whole day's protein; energy intake, meal distribution and non-protein benefits of foods (micronutrients, fibre) are outside the model.
