pH, speciation and nutrient availability

Why do hydroponic growers obsess over keeping the pH between 5.5 and 6.5? Because a single number — the concentration of hydrogen ions — decides in which chemical form phosphate and carbon exist, whether iron stays held by its chelate or rusts out as a precipitate, and how much acid your tap water needs. Drag the pH across the scale and watch the solution respond, titrate your own water to pH 5.8 with and without CO₂ escaping, and let a crop take up nitrate or ammonium to see why the pH of a hydroponic tank never stays where you set it.

What to look at first

  1. Drag the pH marker along the coloured scale at the top of the stage (or use the slider). Watch the particles in the beaker: below pH 7.2 phosphate is almost all H₂PO₄⁻ (pink dots); above it, HPO₄²⁻ (violet diamonds) takes over — the form that precipitates with calcium. Carbon is mostly dissolved CO₂ (grey rings) below pH 6.35 and bicarbonate above.
  2. Watch the iron. The three rings of "claws" are iron chelates. Push the pH above 7: EDTA drops its iron first, rust-coloured Fe(OH)₃ flakes fall and settle, then DTPA follows near pH 8, while EDDHA holds on to about pH 10. The chelate chart below shows the same thing quantitatively.
  3. Read the availability chart on the right of the stage. Iron, manganese, zinc, copper and boron shrink above 6.5; calcium, magnesium and molybdenum shrink below 5.5. The chart is qualitative — a summary of many experiments — but it explains the hydroponic window.
  4. Titrate your tap water. Apply Soft water + nitric acid and press Animate. The pH falls slowly at first — bicarbonate is buffering — then crashes once the bicarbonate is gone. Compare the two doses in the readouts: an aerated tank needs about a quarter more acid, because the CO₂ that is formed escapes.
  5. See the pH creep. The small curve on the titration bench shows what happens if you dose for a closed tank and then aerate: within a few hours the pH rises from 5.8 to above 7. This is the everyday experience of DWC and NFT growers.
  6. Feed the roots. Switch the stage to Root zone, apply Pure nitrate feeding and animate: nitrate goes into the root, bicarbonate comes out, and the pH climbs by about half a unit per day. Try Too much ammonium: now H⁺ comes out and the solution acidifies within a day.
Key idea

pH is a logarithm, so each unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ions — and each acid–base pair switches form within about one pH unit of its pK. The hydroponic window 5.5–6.5 is where phosphate is still H₂PO₄⁻, bicarbonate is mostly gone, the cheap iron chelates still hold their iron, and calcium and magnesium are fully available.

One number, many chemical switches

pH is minus the decimal logarithm of the hydrogen-ion activity: at pH 5.8 the solution holds 1.6 µmol L⁻¹ of H⁺, at pH 6.8 ten times less. Hydrogen ions are tiny in number compared with nitrate or potassium, yet they control every acid–base pair in the tank. Each pair has a pK: the pH at which acid and base forms are equally abundant. One pH unit below the pK the acid form makes up 91 %; one unit above, the base form does (Lesson 5.2).

The classic availability chart — and its limits

Horticultural textbooks summarise the pH effects in a chart of bands that widen and narrow with pH. For soilless, peat-based media the familiar version goes back to Peterson (1981): the micronutrient cations Fe, Mn, Zn and Cu, and boron, become less available above about pH 6.5; calcium, magnesium and molybdenum below about 5.5; phosphorus is best near 5.5–6.5. The stage redraws such a chart schematically. It is qualitative: the shapes combine solubility, speciation and plant physiology, and they depend on the medium. Huang et al. (2025) measured macronutrient solubility in four peat-based substrates across pH 3–7.5 and concluded that real responses — for example phosphorus falling with calcium hydroxide liming as calcium phosphates form — are more complex than the reference charts. Use the chart to remember the pattern, and the speciation and chelate panels to understand the mechanism.

Frontier · low pH against root rot

The window 5.5–6.5 is a compromise, not a law. Gillespie, Kubota and Miller (2020) grew basil in deep-water culture at pH 4.0 or 5.5, with and without inoculation by Pythium aphanidermatum zoospores, and found that keeping the solution at pH 4.0 effectively suppressed the root rot. The price is chemistry you can explore here: at pH 4 calcium and magnesium uptake suffer and the micronutrient balance has to be adjusted.

Iron chelates: a tug of war for the ligand

A chelate is an organic ligand that grips Fe³⁺ with several bonds. In a nutrient solution it is pulled on from two sides: hydroxide ions, whose concentration rises tenfold per pH unit, would precipitate the iron as Fe(OH)₃; and calcium, a hundred times more abundant than iron, binds any ligand that lets go. The laboratory computes the resulting equilibrium with stability constants from critically evaluated compilations (Eq. P5). Table 2 compares the model with the stability ranges quoted in practice.

The equilibrium limits sit a few tenths of a unit above the practical ones, as they should: in a real tank iron is also lost by photodegradation of the chelate in light (Albano & Miller, 2001), by displacement with copper and zinc from sulfate salts, and by precipitation with phosphate, and aged precipitates are less soluble than fresh ones (select aged "soil-Fe"). Practical guidance therefore keeps a safety margin.

Alkalinity, acid and the escaping CO₂

Most tap water is slightly alkaline because it carries bicarbonate. Each proton you add converts one HCO₃⁻ into CO₂, so the acid dose is fixed by how much bicarbonate must go. How much must go depends on what happens to the CO₂. In a closed vessel it stays dissolved and acidifies the solution itself, so stopping at pH 5.8 still leaves about a fifth of the carbon as bicarbonate — the chemical basis of the growers' rule "leave ≈ 0.5 mmol L⁻¹ of bicarbonate as a buffer" (van der Lugt, 2016). In an aerated DWC tank or a splashing NFT return, CO₂ escapes towards equilibrium with air, where it is only about 0.014 mmol L⁻¹ at 425 ppm (Sander, 2023; NOAA, 2025); the remaining bicarbonate then pushes the pH back up. Holding pH 5.8 in an aerated tank requires neutralising almost all the alkalinity. In a greenhouse enriched to 1000 ppm CO₂ the equilibrium CO₂ is 2.4 times higher and the drift smaller — try the CO₂ slider.

Roots move the pH too

A crop fed only nitrate takes up more negative than positive charge, and its roots release bicarbonate: the tank alkalinises. Ammonium does the opposite. The other ions matter too: potassium, calcium and magnesium bring positive charge, phosphate and sulfate negative. The model lumps these into one number, ε, the excess of other cations over other anions per millimole of nitrogen; the NH₄⁺ share that exactly cancels the drift is then \((1-\varepsilon)/2\). With ε between 0.6 and 0.9 this is 5–20 % — the range in which growers keep ammonium (Lesson 5.2; the Dutch standard solutions have 6–7 %). Because phosphate is almost the only buffer left once bicarbonate has been neutralised, even a slow net release of a few tenths of a millimole per litre per day moves the pH by tenths of a unit.

The equations behind the laboratory

Speciation fractions of a polyprotic acid(P1)
\[ \alpha_k = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}]^{\,n-k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}K_i}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}[\mathrm{H^+}]^{\,n-j}\prod_{i=1}^{j}K_i}, \qquad \alpha_{\mathrm{HPO_4}} = \frac{1}{10^{\,\text{p}K_1+\text{p}K_2-2\text{pH}} + 10^{\,\text{p}K_2-\text{pH}} + 1 + 10^{\,\text{pH}-\text{p}K_3}} \]

the fraction of a polyprotic acid present with k protons removed equals its term — the hydrogen-ion concentration to the power of the protons it still carries times the product of the first k dissociation constants — divided by the sum of all such terms. For phosphate the HPO₄²⁻ fraction simplifies to the reciprocal of four powers of ten.

Follow the derivation

  2. Express every species through \([\mathrm{H_2A}]\): \([\mathrm{HA^-}] = K_1[\mathrm{H_2A}]/[\mathrm{H^+}]\), \([\mathrm{A^{2-}}] = K_1K_2[\mathrm{H_2A}]/[\mathrm{H^+}]^2\).
  3. Divide each by the total \(C_T\) and multiply numerator and denominator by \([\mathrm{H^+}]^2\): \(\alpha_0 = [\mathrm{H^+}]^2/D\), \(\alpha_1 = [\mathrm{H^+}]K_1/D\), \(\alpha_2 = K_1K_2/D\) with \(D = [\mathrm{H^+}]^2 + [\mathrm{H^+}]K_1 + K_1K_2\) — the general form above.
  4. At pH = p\(K_i\) the two neighbouring forms are equal; each pH unit away shifts their ratio tenfold.

Alkalinity (proton balance)(P2)
\[ A = C_T\,(\alpha_{\mathrm{HCO_3}} + 2\,\alpha_{\mathrm{CO_3}}) + P_T\,(\alpha_{\mathrm{HPO_4}} + 2\,\alpha_{\mathrm{PO_4}} - \alpha_{\mathrm{H_3PO_4}}) + [\mathrm{OH^-}] - [\mathrm{H^+}] \]

the alkalinity — the acid a solution can neutralise before reaching its reference state — is the total inorganic carbon times the average number of protons it can still accept, plus the corresponding phosphate term measured from H₂PO₄⁻, plus hydroxide minus hydrogen ions.

\(A\)alkalinity (acid-neutralising capacity) relative to CO₂ and H₂PO₄⁻mmol L⁻¹

Follow the derivation
  1. Choose reference species that are not changed by the salts we add: CO₂ for carbon, H₂PO₄⁻ for phosphate (KH₂PO₄ adds phosphate at the reference level), H₂O for water.
  2. Alkalinity counts protons that the solution can accept relative to that reference: each HCO₃⁻ one, each CO₃²⁻ two, each HPO₄²⁻ one, each PO₄³⁻ two, each OH⁻ one; species with more protons than the reference (H₃PO₄, H⁺) count negatively.
  3. Adding a strong acid lowers A one-for-one; adding CO₂ (or letting it escape) does not change A at all — it changes \(C_T\), and therefore the pH at which the balance is satisfied.
  4. The laboratory solves Eq. P2 for pH by bisection: closed tank with \(C_T\) fixed, or open tank with \([\mathrm{CO_2}]\) fixed by air (\(C_T = [\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{air}}/\alpha_0\)).
Assumptions and validity

Only carbonate, phosphate and water are considered; silicate, borate, ammonia and organic acids are neglected. They matter little between pH 5 and 7.

Acid dose to a target pH(P4)
\[ \begin{gathered} a = A_{\text{w}} - A(\text{pH}^{*};\,C_T^{*}), \qquad V_{\text{acid}} = \frac{a\,V_{\text{w}}}{h\,C_{\text{acid}}} \\[6pt] C_T^{*} = \begin{cases} C_{T,\text{w}} & \text{closed tank} \\ [\mathrm{CO_2}]_{\text{air}}/\alpha_0(\text{pH}^*) & \text{aerated tank} \end{cases} \end{gathered} \]

the acid needed per litre, in millimoles of H⁺, is the alkalinity of the water minus the alkalinity the solution keeps at the target pH, with total carbon either conserved or set by the air. The volume of concentrated acid is that amount times the water volume divided by the protons per acid molecule and by the molarity of the acid.

Safety

Concentrated acids cause severe burns: goggles and gloves, add acid to water, never the reverse. Nitric acid above 3 % is not sold to private persons in the EU (Regulation (EU) 2019/1148); phosphoric acid is.

Conditional stability of an iron chelate(P5)
\[ f_{\text{chel}} = \frac{R}{1+R}, \qquad R = \frac{K'_{\mathrm{FeL}}\;{}^{*}K_s\,[\mathrm{H^+}]^{3}}{\alpha_L}, \qquad \alpha_L = 1 + \sum_n \beta_{\mathrm{H}_n\mathrm{L}}[\mathrm{H^+}]^{n} + \sum_{M=\mathrm{Ca,Mg}} K_{ML}'\,[M] \]

once iron hydroxide can form, the fraction of iron that stays chelated is R over one plus R, where R is the chelate's stability constant times the solubility constant of the hydroxide times the cube of the hydrogen-ion concentration, divided by the side-reaction coefficient of the ligand — how many other ways the freed ligand has to exist: protonated, or bound to calcium and magnesium.

  2. The chelate: \([\mathrm{FeL}] = K_{\mathrm{FeL}}[\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}][\mathrm{L}]\), where \([\mathrm{L}]\) is the fully deprotonated free ligand.

  4. For a 1 : 1 product, ligand and iron totals are equal; the ligand released by iron equals the iron precipitated. Hence \([\mathrm{FeL}]/L' = K_{\mathrm{FeL}}{}^{*}K_s[\mathrm{H^+}]^3/\alpha_L = R\) and \(f = R/(1+R)\).

Assumptions and validity

Thermodynamic equilibrium only; the kinetics of iron release can take days. Mixed hydroxo complexes such as Fe(OH)EDTA²⁻ are neglected, which slightly underestimates EDTA's stability above pH 7. Competition by Cu, Zn and Mn, photodegradation and precipitation of iron phosphates are not included — reasons why practical limits are lower than these.

Root-driven change of alkalinity(P6)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\mathrm{d}A}{\mathrm{d}t} = U_N\,\big[(1-f_{\mathrm{NH_4}}) - f_{\mathrm{NH_4}} - \varepsilon\big], \qquad f^{\,0}_{\mathrm{NH_4}} = \frac{1-\varepsilon}{2} \\[6pt] \varepsilon = \frac{U_{\mathrm{K}} + 2U_{\mathrm{Ca}} + 2U_{\mathrm{Mg}} - U_{\mathrm{P}} - 2U_{\mathrm{S}}}{U_N} \end{gathered} \]

the alkalinity of the solution changes at the rate of nitrogen uptake times the nitrate share, minus the ammonium share, minus the excess of other cations over other anions per unit of nitrogen. The drift vanishes when the ammonium share equals one minus ε, halved.

Follow the derivation

  2. Per mmol N: nitrate brings −(1 − f), ammonium +f; the other ions bring +ε. Net OH⁻ release = (1 − f) − f − ε mmol, which adds directly to the alkalinity of Eq. P2.
  3. Uptake estimate: six lettuces near harvest each gain ≈ 0.6 g dry mass per day (Lesson 5.3); at an assumed 4.5 % N in dry mass that is 27 mg N d⁻¹ per plant, 11.6 mmol N d⁻¹ in 40 L ≈ 0.3 mmol L⁻¹ d⁻¹.
  4. ε from composition (illustrative): a crop whose tissue contains, per mol N, 0.5 mol K, 0.08 mol Ca, 0.04 mol Mg, 0.05 mol P and 0.03 mol S gives ε = 0.5 + 0.16 + 0.08 − 0.05 − 0.06 = 0.63.
  5. The pH follows from the new alkalinity by Eq. P2 (open or closed tank).
Assumptions and validity

Uptake is treated as steady; organic acids exuded by roots, nitrification of ammonium and microbial activity are ignored, as is any pH correction by the grower. The value of ε differs between crops and stages and is the largest uncertainty; measure your own drift and fit it.

Investigation tasks

  1. Your water's acid demand

    Enter the alkalinity and pH of your own tap water (Swedish utilities report "alkalinitet" in mg HCO₃⁻ L⁻¹). Find the dose of the acid you can actually buy that brings 100 L to pH 5.8 in a closed tank and in your aerated project tank. Then check it in reality: titrate 1 L of your water with a dilute acid while logging pH, and compare the curve with the simulated one.

  2. How long does pH 5.8 last?

    Dose for a closed tank, then read the creep curve on the titration bench. Vary the aeration \(k_La\) from 0.2 to 6 h⁻¹. How long before the pH leaves the window? Would you rather acidify further, lower the aeration or buffer with phosphate? Justify with Eqs. P2 and P7.

  3. Chelate choice for your system

    Using the chelate chart, find the highest pH at which each chelate still holds 90 % of its iron for your water's calcium level. Your DWC drifts up to pH 7 between corrections: which chelate do you need, and how much more would it cost if Fe-DTPA costs twice and Fe-EDDHA four times as much as Fe-EDTA (Hershkowitz et al., 2025)?

  4. The neutral ammonium share

    With the root-zone view, find the NH₄⁺ share that keeps the pH within ±0.2 units for 7 days at ε = 0.6, 0.7 and 0.8. Compare with the 6–7 % of the Dutch standard recipes. What would you change in a recipe if your tank drifted up by 0.4 units per day?

  5. Greenhouse CO₂ and pH

    Raise the CO₂ in the air from 425 to 1000 ppm (a CO₂-enriched greenhouse). How do the aerated acid dose and the root-driven drift change? Explain with Eq. P3.

  6. Question the chart

    Read Huang et al. (2025) or Gillespie et al. (2020). Which parts of the qualitative availability chart do their measurements support, and which do they contradict? Write three sentences you would add to the chart's caption.

Model assumptions

  • All equilibria at 25 °C; concentrations are used in place of activities for the acid–base chemistry (pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺]). Carbonate constants from Plummer and Busenberg (1982) via the course library; phosphate pKs 2.15, 7.20, 12.35.
  • Iron chelates: 1 : 1 chelate supplied as its iron salt, equilibrium with fresh amorphous or aged Fe(OH)₃, competition by free Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ (assumed constant, as they are in large excess) and by protons; Fe(III) hydrolysis species included. Constants at I = 0.1 M: EDTA from Martell and Smith as tabulated by Skoog et al. (2014), DTPA from the IUPAC evaluation (Anderegg et al., 2005), o,o-EDDHA from Yunta et al. (2012, converted from I = 0 with the Davies equation; consistent with log K = 35.09 of Yunta et al., 2003), hydrolysis from Baes and Mesmer (1976), Fe(OH)₃ solubility from Lindsay (1979) and Lindsay and Schwab (1982). Mixed hydroxo-chelates of EDTA, competition by Cu/Zn/Mn, photodegradation and kinetics are not modelled.
  • The availability chart is a qualitative, schematic redrawing of the classic chart for soilless media (Peterson, 1981) and of the chemistry in Sonneveld and Voogt (2009); its band widths are not measured concentrations.
  • Titration: water alkalinity treated as bicarbonate plus carbonate at the given pH; closed tank conserves total carbon; aerated tank in equilibrium with 425 ppm CO₂ (adjustable; Henry constant 0.0334 mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹, Sander, 2023); phosphoric acid counted as one proton to H₂PO₄⁻, sulfuric acid as two.
  • Root drift: steady nitrogen uptake with a fixed NH₄⁺ share and a lumped excess-cation parameter ε; no nitrification, exudation or grower correction; tank either in equilibrium with air or closed with the initial carbon.
