CO₂ enrichment balance

Every greenhouse grower faces the same question on every sunny morning: how much CO₂ should I buy? Extra CO₂ makes the crop grow faster, but whatever the plants do not take up disappears through the vents within minutes. In this laboratory you run the dynamic CO₂ mass balance of a greenhouse — or of a closed plant factory — through a whole day, watch the CO₂ molecules pour in from the dosing tubes, vanish into the leaves and escape through the vents, and find the set-point that makes the most money. You will rediscover a classic result of greenhouse engineering: when the vents are wide open, enrichment does not pay.

What to look at first

  1. Watch an April day in Västerås. Before sunrise the greenhouse is closed and the crop respires: the grey molecules become slightly denser. When the sun comes up, dosing starts: pink molecules rise from the tubes under the crop, and green rings mark molecules taken up by leaves. Around midday the vents open with the sun and blue molecules stream out of the roof. Read the NDIR sensor: the concentration sags below the set-point of 800 µmol mol⁻¹ because the dosing system runs at its full capacity of 200 kg ha⁻¹ h⁻¹.
  2. Compare the two curves in the first chart. Without dosing the concentration falls to about 320 µmol mol⁻¹ in the morning, when the crop is photosynthesising in a closed greenhouse: CO₂ depletion costs growth even before we think about enrichment.
  3. Read the budget. About 1.9 t of CO₂ is dosed per hectare on this day, but the crop takes up only about 50 kg more than it would have without dosing: about 88 % of the dosed CO₂ leaves through the vents. Van Tuyll et al. (2022) estimated that about 85 % of the CO₂ supplied to Dutch greenhouses is lost by ventilation.
  4. Try the three strategies. Lower when vents open cuts the CO₂ bill by almost three quarters and still gives a positive margin; the Economic optimum doses hard in the morning while the vents are closed and throttles back when they open.
  5. Open the vents wide. Choose Summer, vents wide open: a fixed set-point of 800 µmol mol⁻¹ now loses money every day, whereas the economic controller still earns a little. The fourth chart shows why: the optimal set-point falls from about 1180 µmol mol⁻¹ at 2 air changes per hour to about 970 at 10 h⁻¹ and below 500 at 20 h⁻¹, and above about 25–30 h⁻¹ it is not worth dosing at all.
  6. Close the room. Choose Plant factory room: with 0.02 air changes per hour almost all dosed CO₂ ends up in the crop, which is why every plant factory enriches to 1000 µmol mol⁻¹ or more.
Key idea

CO₂ enrichment is a trade-off between two rates that both depend on the concentration: the crop's extra uptake, which grows with diminishing returns, and the loss to the outside, which grows linearly with the enrichment above outdoor air and in proportion to the ventilation rate. The economic optimum is where one extra µmol mol⁻¹ adds as much value in growth as it costs in lost CO₂.

A tank with a leak and a sink

Think of the greenhouse air as a tank of CO₂ (Figure 1). The dosing system fills it, the crop drains it by photosynthesis (and refills it a little by respiration), and the air exchange with the outside pulls it towards the outdoor concentration of 427 µmol mol⁻¹. The concentration you measure is the level in that tank. Because the tank is small — about 10 g of CO₂ per square metre at 1000 µmol mol⁻¹ in a 5.5 m-high greenhouse — it responds within minutes to a change in any of the three flows. That is why a greenhouse without dosing is depleted on a sunny morning, and why CO₂ dosed with the vents open is gone in minutes.

How much CO₂ does the crop take?

The crop model is the canopy photosynthesis of the lettuce model of van Henten (1994), which has been used for three decades to study the optimal control of greenhouse climate. It combines two limits in the way first proposed by Acock et al. (1978): a light-limited rate, proportional to the photons reaching the canopy, and a CO₂-limited rate, proportional to the CO₂ concentration above the compensation point. The canopy follows the smaller of the two, smoothly: \(\Phi = P_I P_C/(P_I + P_C)\).

This co-limitation has an important consequence. In dim light the crop is light-limited and extra CO₂ achieves little; in bright light the crop is CO₂-limited and enrichment pays. In the model, raising CO₂ from 427 to 1000 µmol mol⁻¹ increases canopy uptake by about 26 % at 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ but by about 57 % at 600 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. At a fixed light level the co-limitation curve has exactly the shape of the empirical response of Lesson 8.3 (Eq. 8.3.2), with a half-saturation constant \(K\) that increases with light (Eq. CB4). Kimball's (1983) average of +33 % for a doubling of CO₂ corresponds to a moderately bright day.

Why growers dose in the morning

On a sunny spring morning the light is already strong, the vents are still closed, and the crop quickly depletes the air. The marginal value of CO₂ is high and its marginal cost low: this is the most profitable hour of the day for enrichment. Wang et al. (2022) review strategies that concentrate dosing in these hours, and Kläring et al. (2007) showed that simply keeping the concentration at the outdoor level with the vents open increased cucumber yield.

Where the dosed CO₂ goes

In steady state the supply needed to hold a set-point \(C\) is the crop's uptake plus \(kN\bar h\,(C - C_{\text{out}})\). The second term is proportional to both the enrichment and the ventilation rate. With \(\bar h\) = 5.5 m, holding 800 µmol mol⁻¹ costs 3.8 g m⁻² h⁻¹ of ventilation loss at 1 air change per hour — about the crop's uptake — but 75 g m⁻² h⁻¹ at 20 air changes per hour, twenty-five times what the crop can take up. Greenhouses ventilate at 20–60 air changes per hour on warm days (Lesson 8.1), whereas plant factories leak only 0.01–0.02 (Kozai, 2013). That factor of a thousand explains why plant factories enrich to 1000–2000 µmol mol⁻¹ with a CO₂-use efficiency near 90 %, while summer enrichment in a ventilated greenhouse is mostly a way of enriching the atmosphere.

Finding the economic optimum

Raise the set-point by one µmol mol⁻¹. The crop takes up a little more CO₂ — worth \(v\) euros per kg, the value of the lettuce this CO₂ becomes — and the vents lose a little more — costing \(p\) euros per kg. The optimum is where the two marginal effects balance (Eq. CB9). Because the marginal gain falls with concentration and the marginal loss is constant, the balance point moves down as ventilation rises, and eventually falls below the concentration the greenhouse would have anyway. There, the best decision is not to dose at all — the classic result reproduced in the fourth chart. The same analysis explains why the optimum rises with light, with the product price and with cheaper CO₂.

Van Henten (1994) solved this problem properly, as an optimal-control problem over a whole growing season with heating, ventilation and CO₂ as controls; our economic controller is the "quasi-steady" simplification that applies the marginal rule instant by instant. It is a good approximation because the air responds in minutes, much faster than the crop grows (the time-scale decomposition of Van Henten & Bontsema, 2009, rests on the same idea).

Common misconception

"If CO₂ increases yield, more CO₂ is always better." The yield response saturates (Lesson 8.3), and every extra µmol mol⁻¹ above outdoor air increases the loss through the vents in proportion. With open vents the best set-point can be below the outdoor concentration — meaning: do not dose.

Research frontier

As natural gas disappears from greenhouse heating, CO₂ no longer comes "for free" with the heat. Growers must buy it from industry, biogas plants or direct air capture (Lesson 8.3), so dosing is becoming a purchase decision that must be optimised hour by hour — exactly the calculation of this laboratory. Van Tuyll et al. (2022) explore lower set-points, targeted dosing under the canopy and more closed greenhouses for a decarbonised future.

The equations behind the simulation

The day is integrated with a 60 s time step, after a warm-up day so that the result is periodic. All fluxes are per m² of floor; multiply g m⁻² h⁻¹ by 10 to obtain kg ha⁻¹ h⁻¹.

Canopy CO₂ uptake (van Henten, 1994)(CB3)
\[ \begin{gathered} \Phi_{\text{up}} = \left(1 - e^{-c_{\text{pl}}X}\right)\frac{P_I\,P_C}{P_I + P_C} \\[6pt] P_I = \varepsilon\,I, \qquad P_C = \sigma(T)\,k\,\left(C - \Gamma(T)\right) \end{gathered} \]
\[ \sigma(T) = -c_1T^2 + c_2T - c_3, \qquad \Gamma(T) = \frac{c_\Gamma}{k}\,2^{(T-20)/10} \]

Why the break-even cost is \(v\) per kg of extra CO₂

Enrichment pays if the cost per extra kg of CO₂ fixed, \(p/\eta_{\text{dose}}\), is smaller than its value \(v\). With \(v\) = €9.8 kg⁻¹ and \(p\) = €0.15 kg⁻¹ this requires \(\eta_{\text{dose}} > 1.5\) % — a low bar that open vents nevertheless fail to clear on warm days.

The economically optimal set-point(CB9)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\partial \pi}{\partial C} = (v-p)\,\Phi_{\text{up}}^{\prime}(C) - p\,kN\bar h = 0 \\[6pt] \Longrightarrow\quad C^{*} = \Gamma - K + \sqrt{\frac{(v-p)\,A_s\,K}{p\,k\,N\,\bar h}} \end{gathered} \]

at each moment the best set-point is the one where the value of the extra photosynthesis from one more µmol mol⁻¹, net of its purchase price, equals the cost of the extra CO₂ lost by ventilation; with the co-limitation curve this gives a closed formula in which the optimum rises with light and product value and falls with the CO₂ price and the ventilation rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. In quasi-steady state all uptake and all ventilation loss must be bought: \(\Phi_{\text{inj}} = \Phi_{\text{up}} - \Phi_{\text{resp}} + kN\bar h(C - C_{\text{out}})\) (CB7). Then \(\pi(C) = v\Phi_{\text{up}} - p[\Phi_{\text{up}} - \Phi_{\text{resp}} + kN\bar h(C - C_{\text{out}})]\).
  2. Differentiate: \(\pi'(C) = (v - p)\Phi_{\text{up}}'(C) - pkN\bar h\).
  3. With \(\Phi_{\text{up}} = A_s(C-\Gamma)/(C-\Gamma+K)\): \(\Phi_{\text{up}}' = A_sK/(C-\Gamma+K)^2\). Setting \(\pi' = 0\) and solving gives the formula — identical to Lesson 8.3, Eq. 8.3.7, but with \(A_s\) and \(K\) now following the light hour by hour.
  4. If \(C^{*}\) is below the concentration the greenhouse has without dosing, dosing loses money: the controller does nothing. Because \(C^{*} \propto 1/\sqrt{N}\) for large \(N\), this always happens when the vents are open wide enough.
Assumptions and validity

Instantaneous optimisation ignores CO₂ stored in the air between hours and the effect of CO₂ on transpiration, temperature and quality; the full dynamic problem is solved by optimal control (van Henten, 1994; Van Henten, 2003). Prices are assumed constant.

Investigation tasks

Start each task from the preset Swedish spring day unless stated otherwise. Use CSV (day) to export the time series and Save investigation to store your settings.

  1. Depletion first

    Switch the strategy to a set-point of 430 µmol mol⁻¹ (just above outdoor air). How much CO₂ does it take per day simply to prevent depletion, and what share of it ends up in the crop? Compare the dosing efficiency with that of 800 µmol mol⁻¹. Relate your answer to Kläring et al. (2007), who kept cucumbers at the ambient level.

  2. The season

    With the economic controller, record the daily margin, the CO₂ supplied and the mean concentration in January, April, July and October. When in the year is enrichment most profitable per kg of CO₂, and why? What changes if you add 150 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of supplementary light in January?

  3. Rediscover the classic result

    In the fourth chart, read the optimal set-point at 1, 5, 10 and 20 air changes per hour. Check two of the values with the closed formula of Eq. CB9 (use the mini-calculator at 600 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Then double the CO₂ price and halve it: how does the air exchange rate at which dosing stops move? Explain with the \(1/\sqrt{N}\) dependence.

  4. Strategies compared

    For a July day with vents opening to 40 h⁻¹, compare the fixed set-point (800), the vent-coupled strategy (450 above 2 h⁻¹) and the economic controller: CO₂ supplied, extra yield, margin and share lost through the vents. Which would you recommend to a Swedish lettuce grower buying liquid CO₂? What information would you need to be confident?

  5. Capacity

    On the spring day, reduce the dosing capacity from 200 to 50 kg ha⁻¹ h⁻¹ and increase it to 400. How do the achieved concentration and the margin change? Is a bigger dosing system worth buying if it costs €2 000 per hectare per year more in capital charges?

  6. Plant factory versus greenhouse

    Choose Plant factory room. Record the dosing efficiency and the CO₂ lost at 0.02, 0.2 and 2 air changes per hour. At what leakage rate would a plant factory become as wasteful as a closed greenhouse? Compare with Kozai's (2013) CUE of 0.87–0.89.

Model assumptions

  • One representative day in the middle of the chosen month, integrated with a 60 s step after a warm-up day; the crop's dry weight (canopy closure) and the temperature are constant during the day.
  • Light. Clear-sky global radiation from solar geometry (course library), scaled by a clearness factor; 2.02 µmol of PAR photons per joule of global radiation; greenhouse transmission 0.7 by default. The closed room has constant LED light over the photoperiod and the uptake of all tiers adds up per m² of floor.
  • Crop. Canopy photosynthesis, respiration and conversion factors of the lettuce model of van Henten (1994), with the canopy light-use efficiency converted to photons (4.57 µmol J⁻¹). The model describes a healthy, well-fed crop without photosynthetic acclimation; for a fully closed canopy it gives high growth rates, so absolute benefits are an upper estimate.
  • Ventilation. Vents open linearly with outdoor radiation between 150 and 550 W m⁻² (a proxy for temperature control), or a constant air exchange rate. The air is well mixed.
  • Economics. Constant CO₂ price and lettuce price; the value of CO₂ is derived from the lettuce price, so it ignores quality effects, labour and the fact that growers are often paid per head rather than per kilogram. The default lettuce price of €1 kg⁻¹ is an assumption for illustration.
  • Not included. Effects of CO₂ on stomata, transpiration and temperature; CO₂ from heating flue gases (free CO₂ in winter); worker respiration; CO₂ gradients within the crop; the carbon footprint of the CO₂ (see Lesson 8.3).
