High-moisture extrusion

Step up to a lab-scale co-rotating twin-screw extruder — the machine that turns pea and soy protein into chicken-like fibres. Dose powder and water, set the screw speed and the barrel and cooling-die temperatures, and watch the powder become a dough and then a hot protein melt that sets into layered fibres in the cooling die. The simulator solves the mass and energy balances along the barrel, computes the specific mechanical energy, the viscous heating and the residence time, cools the melt in the die and places your operating point on a process-window map. Cut the barrel open, then tear the product to see whether you made meat-like fibres, a porous sponge, an unstructured paste or a burnt strand.

A guided tour of the line

  1. Follow the material. Protein powder falls from the gravimetric feeder through the sight glass into zone 1. Water is pumped into zone 2 (blue particles in the stainless line). Click Kneading in the controls: the camera flies to the kneading zone and the barrel opens (cut-away). The two screws turn in the same direction and wipe each other clean; the pale granular powder turns into a moist dough and, after the second kneading block, into a glossy brown melt. Switch Material colours to Temperature to see where the material heats up.
  2. Read the operator panel. The screen on the swivel arm shows screw speed, torque, SME, melt temperature and every barrel zone (set-point line, actual bar). Press Skip warm-up to jump to steady state, or leave it and watch the zones heat up with a time constant of about a minute.
  3. Inspect the cooling die. Click Die & cutter. In cut-away or X-ray view a panel above the die shows the temperature field across the 6 mm gap along the whole die: hot core, cold walls. The strand leaves the die and a guillotine cuts it into 80 mm pieces.
  4. Break it on purpose. Apply the preset Throughput too high. The melt now spends too little time in the die to cool below 100 °C: steam puffs appear, and after one residence time the pieces on the belt become porous. The process-window map explains why — the amber “expanded” region has grown over your operating point.
  5. Tear the product. Under the charts, press Tear the sample. A fibrous product separates into bundles of fibres bridging the crack; an unstructured product breaks cleanly; an expanded one crumbles.
Key idea

An extruder is a continuous heat-and-shear reactor. Three numbers summarise most of what happens inside it: the moisture of the melt (set by the water pump), the melt temperature (set by the barrel heaters and by viscous dissipation of the drive power) and the thermal history in the cooling die (set by die length, gap, throughput and coolant temperature). Fibres form only when all three fall inside a window.

What you are looking at

The virtual machine has the geometry of the 26 mm, L/D ≈ 29, seven-section co-rotating extruder with a 380 × 30 × 6 mm cooling die used by Wittek et al. (2021) at Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. The Soy HME, lab scale preset is close to their operating point (5 kg h⁻¹ protein powder, about 50 % water, 250 rpm, coolant 50 °C); they measured material temperatures of 124 °C and 135 °C for their two barrel profiles (maximum set-points 124 °C and 140 °C), and the model gives 127 °C and 141 °C.

From powder to fibres in about a minute

High-moisture extrusion (HME) processes a protein ingredient — soy protein concentrate, pea protein isolate, wheat gluten or blends — at roughly 40–80 % water. Inside the barrel the material passes through four functional zones (Zhang et al., 2019): a feeding zone where dry powder is conveyed, a mixing zone where injected water hydrates it into a dough, a melting zone where kneading blocks and heat unfold (denature) the proteins and turn the dough into a viscous, continuous melt, and finally the die. What makes HME special is the long cooling die: a narrow, cooled slit in which the melt flows slowly in laminar flow and sets from the wall inwards before it leaves the machine.

Why twin screws, why kneading blocks?

Two intermeshing, co-rotating screws with the self-wiping Erdmenger profile scrape each other and the barrel wall on every turn, so no material can stagnate and burn, and the heat-transfer film at the wall is continually renewed. Conveying elements are only partly filled when the extruder is starve-fed (the feeder, not the screw, sets the throughput); kneading blocks — stacks of staggered discs — have little conveying capacity, run completely full and impose high shear. That is where most of the drive power is dissipated and where the dough melts (see the temperature chart: the red curve steepens after each magenta band).

The role of water

Water is the plasticiser of proteins. More water lowers the melt viscosity exponentially, so the torque and the SME fall (Chen et al., 2010, measured SME and residence time falling significantly between 28 % and 60 % moisture); it lowers the temperature needed to denature and melt the proteins; and it is the reason for the cooling die: a melt at 140 °C contains superheated water, which would flash to steam at the exit and blow the structure apart. That is exactly what low-moisture extrusion exploits to make porous textured vegetable protein (TVP).

How do the fibres form?

The mechanism is still debated (Cornet et al., 2022; van der Sman & van der Goot, 2023). Current evidence favours a multiphase picture: the melt contains a protein-rich continuous phase and a water-rich dispersed phase (Wittek et al., 2021 found no change in protein–protein interactions, but a distinct two-phase morphology); in the laminar, sheared flow of the cooling die the dispersed domains are stretched along the flow, and cooling from the wall inwards freezes the elongated morphology in layers parallel to the walls. Simulations by Sandoval Murillo et al. (2019) reproduced fibre-like structures only when the rates of phase separation, heat conduction and flow were matched within a narrow window — the physical reason why HME has a process window.

Reading the process window

The map combines four limits, each documented in the literature: (i) the melt must be hot enough — for soy, fibrous structures need melt temperatures of about 130 °C or more (Cheftel et al., 1992, reviewed by Zhang et al., 2019), and anisotropy grew between 124 and 135 °C in Wittek et al. (2021); (ii) above about 150–160 °C the degree of texturisation falls again and the product browns, and wheat gluten turns black above 180 °C (Zhang et al., 2019); (iii) the moisture must be high enough for a fibrous melt (texturisation rises sharply above ≈ 38–40 %, Zhang et al., 2019) but not so high that the protein network is too weak; and (iv) the core must leave the die below 100 °C. The first three limits are semi-quantitative syntheses; the fourth is computed exactly from heat conduction (Eq. E8).

Common misconception

“The barrel temperature is the product temperature.” It is not: the melt temperature results from an energy balance. At high screw speed or low moisture, viscous dissipation can push the melt well above the barrel set-point — Lin et al. (2002) measured higher product temperatures at lower moisture for exactly this reason — and the barrel zones then have to cool. Try 800 rpm in the simulator and watch the specific thermal energy become negative in the last zones.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Research is moving from trial-and-error towards predictive process design: closed-cavity rheometry of protein melts, in-line measurement of die pressure and melt temperature, computational fluid dynamics of cooling dies and phase-field models of fibre formation. Industry combines HME with less refined, lower-footprint ingredients (dry-fractionated concentrates, blends with fibre) — which changes the window you explore here.

The equations behind the simulation

The model solves a steady-state mass and energy balance along the screws every time you change a setting (and again as the barrel zones warm up), then the transient heat conduction in the cooling die. Equations E1–E8 are physics; E9–E10 encode the semi-quantitative process window.

Mass balance: melt moisture and water pump rate(E1)
\[ X = \frac{\dot m_{\text{s}}\,w_{\text{p}} + \dot m_{\text{w}}}{\dot m_{\text{s}} + \dot m_{\text{w}}} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \dot m_{\text{w}} = \dot m_{\text{s}}\,\frac{X - w_{\text{p}}}{1 - X} \]

the moisture of the melt (wet basis) is all the water that enters — the water already in the powder plus the injected water — divided by everything that enters; solving for the injected water gives the pump rate needed for a target moisture.

\(X\)moisture content of the melt, wet basiskg kg⁻¹0.40–0.80 (HME); 0.20–0.40 (TVP)
\(\dot m_{\text{s}}\)powder feed rate (as-is)kg h⁻¹2–20 (lab); 100–1000 (industrial)
\(w_{\text{p}}\)moisture of the powder, wet basiskg kg⁻¹≈ 0.07
\(\dot m_{\text{w}}\)injected waterkg h⁻¹–
Follow the derivation
  1. At steady state nothing accumulates, so mass in = mass out: \(\dot m = \dot m_{\text{s}} + \dot m_{\text{w}}\).
  2. Water balance: water in = \(\dot m_{\text{s}}w_{\text{p}} + \dot m_{\text{w}}\) = water out = \(X\dot m\). Dividing gives the left-hand form.
  3. Multiply out: \(X\dot m_{\text{s}} + X\dot m_{\text{w}} = \dot m_{\text{s}}w_{\text{p}} + \dot m_{\text{w}}\) ⇒ \(\dot m_{\text{w}}(1 - X) = \dot m_{\text{s}}(X - w_{\text{p}})\).
  4. Note the \(1/(1-X)\): going from 60 % to 70 % moisture needs 58 % more water, not 17 %. At high moisture the water pump, not the feeder, dominates the throughput — and hence the load on the cooling die.
Assumptions and validity

No evaporation in the barrel (the barrel is closed and pressurised); vented extruders lose some water at vent ports. Wittek et al. (2021) quote “50 % water content” for 5 kg h⁻¹ solids + 5 kg h⁻¹ water, i.e. they counted the powder as dry; with 7 % powder moisture Eq. E1 gives 53.5 %.

(kg h⁻¹ × kg kg⁻¹ + kg h⁻¹)/(kg h⁻¹) = kg kg⁻¹ ✓
Specific mechanical energy(E2)
\[ \text{SME} = \frac{P_{\text{mech}}}{\dot m} = \frac{2\pi N_{\text{s}}\,T_{\text{q}}}{\dot m},\qquad 1\ \text{kWh t}^{-1} = 3.6\ \text{kJ kg}^{-1} \]

the mechanical energy delivered to each kilogram of material equals the shaft power — two pi times the rotational speed times the torque — divided by the mass flow rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. Rotational power is torque × angular velocity: \(P = T_{\text{q}}\,\omega = 2\pi N_{\text{s}}T_{\text{q}}\).
  2. Divide by the mass flow to get energy per mass: J s⁻¹ / (kg s⁻¹) = J kg⁻¹.
  3. In the simulator the torque is not an input: it follows from the viscous dissipation in the filled screw volume (Eq. E4), \(T_{\text{q}} = P_{\text{mech}}/(2\pi N_{\text{s}})\). The readout “torque” shows it as a percentage of an assumed drive limit of 120 N m.
  4. Converting: 1 kWh t⁻¹ = 3.6 × 10⁶ J / 1000 kg = 3.6 kJ kg⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

Gearbox and no-load (friction) losses are not included; on a real machine they are measured by running empty and subtracted. SME is an average: it says nothing about where along the screw the energy was dissipated.

Melt viscosity: power law with temperature and moisture(E3)
\[ \eta = K\,\dot\gamma^{\,n-1},\qquad K = K_{\text{ref}}\,\exp\!\left[\frac{E_{\text{a}}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{T_{\text{ref}}}\right)\right]\exp\!\left[-\beta\,(X - X_{\text{ref}})\right] \]

the apparent viscosity of the protein melt falls with shear rate (shear thinning, n < 1); its consistency falls exponentially with rising temperature (Arrhenius) and with rising moisture.

Where does this form come from?
  1. Protein and starch doughs in extruders are shear-thinning; the power law \(\tau = K\dot\gamma^{n}\) captures this over one to two decades of shear rate.
  2. Temperature and moisture enter the consistency multiplicatively; this is the classical structure of extrusion viscosity models for food doughs, e.g. the generalised model of Morgan, Steffe & Ofoli (1989) for protein doughs, which also adds a time–temperature history term omitted here.
  3. The parameter values are not measured for a particular ingredient: \(K_{\text{ref}}\) was calibrated so that the model reproduces the melt temperatures reported by Wittek et al. (2021) and SME values inside the 85–350 kJ kg⁻¹ range of Pietsch et al. (2019). Treat them as plausible, not as data.
\([\eta] = \text{Pa s}^{n}\cdot(\text{s}^{-1})^{n-1} = \text{Pa s}\) ✓; the exponents are dimensionless (K/K, kg kg⁻¹).
Viscous dissipation and shear rate in the screw channels(E4)
\[ \Phi = \tau\,\dot\gamma = K\,\dot\gamma^{\,n+1},\qquad \dot\gamma \approx S\,\frac{\pi D N_{\text{s}}}{h},\qquad P_{\text{mech}} = \int_0^{L}\Phi\,A_{\text{free}}\,f\,\mathrm{d}x \]

the heat generated per unit volume by viscous friction equals the shear stress times the shear rate; the shear rate is the screw tip speed divided by the channel depth, multiplied by a factor that is larger in kneading blocks; integrating over the filled volume gives the drive power.

Follow the derivation
  1. In simple shear the rate of work done on a fluid element per unit volume is \(\tau\dot\gamma\); all of it is dissipated as heat in a purely viscous fluid.
  2. With \(\tau = K\dot\gamma^{n}\) this gives \(K\dot\gamma^{n+1}\): dissipation rises faster than linearly with screw speed (\(n+1 = 1.4\)).
  3. The flight tip moves at \(\pi D N_{\text{s}}\) relative to the barrel; dividing by the channel depth gives an order-of-magnitude shear rate (68 s⁻¹ at 250 rpm). Real flow fields contain much higher shear in the tip clearance and between kneading discs — the factor \(S\) lumps this.
  4. The free area \(A_{\text{free}}\) was computed numerically from the Erdmenger self-wiping profile (tip, flank and root arcs; Booy, 1978) used to draw the 3-D screws.

Degree of fill and residence time in a starve-fed extruder(E5)
\[ f = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\dot V}{\dot V_{\max}}\right),\quad \dot V_{\max} = \tfrac12 A_{\text{free}}\,t\,N_{\text{s}},\qquad \bar t = \int_0^{L}\frac{A_{\text{free}}\,f}{\dot V}\,\mathrm{d}x + \frac{L_{\text{d}}}{\bar u} \]

a conveying element is filled in proportion to the volume flow divided by its drag capacity, which is about half the free volume swept per revolution times the screw speed; the mean residence time is the filled volume divided by the volume flow, plus the time in the die.

\(\dot V\)volumetric flow, \(\dot m/\rho\)m³ s⁻¹

Consequences you can test
  1. In a partly filled conveying element \(A_{\text{free}}f/\dot V = A_{\text{free}}/\dot V_{\max}\): the residence time there does not depend on the feed rate, only on the screw speed.
  2. Kneading blocks (\(f = 1\) for the neutral block; the forward block is set to \(f = 0.35 + 1.5\,\dot V/\dot V_{\max}\)) and the pressure build-up zone before the die are full, so their residence time scales with \(1/\dot V\).
  3. Hence faster screws shorten the residence time only a little (the full zones dominate), whereas a higher feed rate shortens it a lot — and with it the time available for cooling in the die.
Assumptions and validity

Residence-time distributions (which are broad in twin-screw extruders) are replaced by their mean; axial mixing is ignored. The drag-capacity factor ½ is a simplification of the specific-throughput concept used in twin-screw design.

m² × m × s⁻¹ = m³ s⁻¹ ✓; m² × m / (m³ s⁻¹) = s ✓
Energy balance along the barrel(E6)
\[ \dot m\,c_p\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}x} = U\,P_{\text{w}}\,\varphi(f)\,\big(T_{\text{b}}(x) - T\big) + \Phi\,A_{\text{free}}\,f,\qquad \varphi = 0.6 + 0.4f \]

along the barrel, the enthalpy of the material rises by heat conducted from the barrel wall (proportional to the temperature difference) plus the heat generated by viscous friction in the filled volume.

\(c_p\)specific heat of the mixture (Eq. E7)J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹≈ 3100 at 50 %
\(U\)effective barrel-to-melt heat-transfer coefficientW m⁻² K⁻¹800 (assumed)
\(P_{\text{w}}\)wetted perimeter of the figure-eight borem0.1315
\(\varphi\)fraction of the wall covered by a renewed melt film–0.6–1

Follow the derivation
  1. Take a slice of barrel of length \(\mathrm{d}x\). In steady state, energy in = energy out: \(\dot m c_pT(x) + \mathrm{d}Q_{\text{wall}} + \mathrm{d}Q_{\text{visc}} = \dot m c_pT(x+\mathrm{d}x)\).
  2. Wall heat flow: \(\mathrm{d}Q_{\text{wall}} = U\,(P_{\text{w}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x)\,(T_{\text{b}} - T)\). Viscous heat: \(\mathrm{d}Q_{\text{visc}} = \Phi\,A_{\text{free}}f\,\mathrm{d}x\).
  3. Divide by \(\mathrm{d}x\). The simulator integrates this ODE with 2 mm steps (Euler), updating \(K(T)\) and \(c_p(T)\) at every step; water is mixed in at the injection port by an enthalpy balance.
  4. If the coefficients were constant the solution would relax exponentially towards \(T_\infty = T_{\text{b}} + \Phi A_{\text{free}}f/(UP_{\text{w}}\varphi)\) with a length scale \(\dot m c_p/(UP_{\text{w}}\varphi)\) — the melt settles above the barrel temperature when dissipation is strong.
  5. Integrating over the whole barrel: \(\text{SME} + \text{STE} = \int c_p\,\mathrm{d}T\) — the two readouts must add up to the enthalpy rise of the melt (check it!).
Assumptions and validity

One-dimensional (mean temperature over the cross-section), steady state within the barrel (the zones themselves change slowly with a first-order lag of 60 s), no heat loss through the die adapter, no latent heat. \(U\) is not measured for this machine: because the flights wipe the wall on every turn the wall film is continuously renewed, as in a scraped-surface heat exchanger, so a high value was chosen.

\[ \frac{T(y,t)-T_{\text{w}}}{T_{\text{in}}-T_{\text{w}}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{4(-1)^n}{(2n+1)\pi}\cos\frac{(2n+1)\pi y}{H}\,e^{-(2n+1)^2\pi^2\alpha t/H^2},\qquad t = \frac{x}{\bar u},\quad \bar u = \frac{\dot m}{\rho\,W H} \]

Why smooth memberships?

Real boundaries between “fibrous” and “not fibrous” are gradual and depend on the ingredient, the screw configuration and the die. A product of smooth functions gives a continuous, differentiable score and lets you see which limit is binding: the readout “Predicted product” names the smallest factor. The classification is: burnt if the degradation score exceeds 0.5, otherwise expanded if \(E > 0.5\), otherwise fibrous if \(S_{\text{fib}} > 0.45\), otherwise no texture.

Where does the “−10 K per doubling” come from?
  1. Browning (Maillard) and degradation reactions follow Arrhenius kinetics; over a narrow range they can be summarised by a \(Q_{10}\): the rate multiplies by \(Q_{10}\) for every 10 K.
  2. For a first-order reaction the extent depends on rate × time. Keeping the extent constant while doubling the time requires halving the rate, i.e. lowering the temperature by \(10\log_2 2/\log_2 Q_{10}\) K.
  3. With \(Q_{10} = 2\) that is exactly 10 K per doubling. This is an illustrative assumption (Maillard reactions often have \(Q_{10}\) of 2–4), consistent with the observation that low feed rates — long residence times — darken extrudates (Zhang et al., 2019).
°C + dimensionless × K − K = °C ✓

Model assumptions

  • Machine. Co-rotating, self-wiping twin-screw extruder with the geometry of a 26 mm lab machine (L/D ≈ 29, seven 108 mm barrel sections; Wittek et al., 2021). Screw profile: two-flight Erdmenger profile (Booy, 1978) with centre distance 21 mm. Screw configuration (illustrative): conveying elements of 39 and 26 mm pitch, a forward kneading block in zone 3, forward + neutral kneading blocks in zone 5, a filled pressure build-up zone of 2 D before the die. Starve-fed operation.
  • Steady state along the screw. The barrel solution is recomputed whenever settings change; only the barrel zones have dynamics (first-order lag, 60 s). The product leaving the die reflects the conditions one mean residence time earlier (plug-flow delay).
  • Rheology. Power-law melt with Arrhenius and exponential moisture dependence (Eq. E3), parameters calibrated, not measured; no time–temperature history effects (cross-linking), no wall slip, no yield stress, no die-pressure calculation.
  • Heat transfer. One-dimensional energy balance with an assumed effective heat-transfer coefficient (800 W m⁻² K⁻¹); properties from the Choi–Okos correlations; no heat loss in the die adapter; ideal wall temperature in the cooling die; plug flow in the die.
  • Process window and texture. Semi-quantitative smooth-limit model (Eqs. E9–E10) synthesised from the literature cited below; the anisotropy index, fibre thickness, browning and all product renderings are illustrative, not predictions for a specific ingredient. The pea protein window is an illustrative variant of the soy window.
  • Not modelled: residence-time distributions, pressure and die swell, oil or fibre additions, protein source variability (denaturation degree of the isolate changes SME considerably, Osen et al., 2014), vent ports, die pressure control.
  • The 3-D machine is a faithful but generic visualisation (no specific manufacturer). Screws turn at 1/10 of the set speed for visibility.
