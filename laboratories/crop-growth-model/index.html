Crop growth model

How much will a lettuce head weigh in three weeks, and what would an extra hour of light, 300 ppm more CO₂ or a wider spacing change? This laboratory runs the kind of model growers and researchers use to answer such questions: leaves intercept light, intercepted light becomes dry matter with a radiation-use efficiency that depends on light intensity, CO₂ and temperature, and dry matter becomes fresh weight. Watch a raft of butterhead lettuce grow day by day, compare two scenarios side by side, and calibrate the model against your own measurements.

What to look at first

  1. Play the cycle. Press ▶ below the tray. The default reproduces the Cornell hydroponic lettuce system: seedlings are transplanted at 97 plants m⁻², re-spaced to 38 plants m⁻² after 10 days and harvested 24 days after transplanting at a daily light integral of 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹. Watch the raft (the white foam) disappear under the leaves: the uncovered raft is light the crop does not use. Turn on Show light reaching the raft to see it in amber.
  2. Read the growth curve. Fresh weight rises almost exponentially at first and then more linearly. The RGR chart shows why: early growth is limited by leaf area (every new leaf intercepts more light), later growth by the light itself.
  3. Re-spacing. On day 10, LAI and interception drop abruptly when the plants are moved apart — the heads now have room, but some light is wasted again until the canopy closes. Switch re-spacing off and compare the yield per m² and the head weight.
  4. Compare two scenarios. Press Store as scenario B, then change one factor — for example CO₂ from 700 to 400 ppm, or the photoperiod from 16 to 20 h at the same DLI. The tray splits into A and B, and every chart shows B dashed.
  5. Calibrate. In Compare with my data, press Load example (simulated data from a hydroponic experiment) and Calibrate RUE. The model finds the radiation-use efficiency that best explains the data. Then do the same with your own grow log.
Key idea

A crop grows in proportion to the light it intercepts, not the light it receives. Interception depends on leaf area (and so on plant size and spacing); the conversion of intercepted light to dry matter — the radiation-use efficiency — depends on light intensity, CO₂ and temperature. Nearly every decision in controlled-environment agriculture acts on one of these two terms.

Growth as light interception times efficiency

In the 1970s John Monteith noticed that, across many crops and seasons in Britain, the dry matter produced by a healthy crop was closely proportional to the solar radiation its leaves had intercepted (Monteith, 1977). The slope of this relationship — grams of dry matter per unit of intercepted radiation — is the radiation-use efficiency (RUE). For C3 crops in the field it is about 1.4 g per MJ of intercepted solar radiation; on a PAR-photon basis roughly 0.6 g per mole of photons (Sinclair & Muchow, 1999). The idea separates growth into two questions that are much easier than “how does a plant grow?”: how much light does the crop intercept? and how efficiently does it use it?

How much light does the crop intercept?

Light passing down through a canopy is absorbed layer by layer, just like light passing through a solution. Monsi and Saeki (1953; English translation 2005) showed that the light below a canopy falls exponentially with the leaf area above it, so the fraction intercepted is f = 1 − exp(−k·LAI). The leaf area index LAI is the leaf area per unit ground area, and the extinction coefficient k describes the leaf angle: flat (planophile) leaves such as those of lettuce have k ≈ 0.9, upright grasses ≈ 0.3–0.5. With k = 0.9, a canopy intercepts 59 % of the light at LAI = 1, 84 % at LAI = 2 and 93 % at LAI = 3. In the model, leaf area is the shoot dry weight times the specific leaf area SLA, and LAI is the leaf area per plant times the plant density. Young transplants therefore waste most of the light — which is why high-density seedling stages and re-spacing are standard in commercial hydroponics.

How efficiently is intercepted light used?

RUE is not a constant. At low light, leaves use photons efficiently; as light increases they approach saturation and each extra photon adds less. Photorespiration lowers the efficiency, and more so at low CO₂ and high temperature; CO₂ enrichment suppresses it. The model takes these effects from the lettuce model of Van Henten (1994), whose canopy photosynthesis equation (after Acock et al., 1978, and Goudriaan et al., 1985) combines a light-use efficiency that depends on CO₂ and temperature with a CO₂ conductance that depends on temperature. Van Henten calibrated and validated it against lettuce grown in Dutch greenhouses. Expressed per mole of intercepted PAR, the equation gives an RUE of 0.90 g of dry matter per mole at 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, 400 ppm and 20 °C; the model reports three modifiers relative to that reference: light intensity fI, CO₂ fC and temperature fT.

Common misconception

“Only the daily light integral matters.” The DLI sets how many photons arrive, but the photoperiod sets how intense they are. Spreading the same DLI over 20 instead of 16 hours lowers the PPFD by a fifth, and because leaves use low light more efficiently, the same photons make more dry matter. The gain shrinks at high CO₂ and has limits: very long photoperiods can cause leaf injury in some crops, and electricity is often cheaper at night.

From dry weight to fresh weight

Growers sell fresh weight, and lettuce is about 95 % water. Van Henten (1994) measured a fresh-to-dry ratio of 22.5 (a dry-matter content of 4.4 %) for the heads; Cornell growers report 150 g heads with nearly 7 g of dry matter, i.e. about 4.7 % (Brechner & Both, 2013). The dry-matter content itself rises with light and falls with rapid growth in humid conditions, so a model that predicts dry weight well can still be 10–20 % off in fresh weight. The model also allocates 7 % of the dry matter to the roots, which are not sold.

Temperature

Temperature acts in two ways. Photosynthesis responds through the CO₂ compensation point (photorespiration rises with temperature) and through the carboxylation conductance, which in Van Henten's parameterisation is zero at 5 and 40 °C and highest near 22 °C. Maintenance respiration, which the plant pays on all its living dry matter day and night, doubles with every 10 °C (Q₁₀ = 2). Warm nights therefore cost dry matter without adding any — a reason to lower night temperatures. Cornell experiments in a floating system found the most dry mass at about 24 °C air temperature (Thompson et al., 1998).

Relative growth rate

The relative growth rate, RGR = (1/W)(dW/dt) = d ln W/dt, is the growth rate per gram of plant. It is the natural way to compare plants of different size (Hunt et al., 2002). An RGR of 0.2 d⁻¹ means that the plant gains 20 % of its weight per day and doubles in about 3.5 days. RGR declines as the canopy closes — the plant keeps building leaves, but they mostly shade each other.

Research frontier

Plant factories now reach light-use efficiencies far above those of greenhouses because every photon is expensive. Research in 2020–2026 has focused on dynamic lighting and CO₂ (matching intensity to electricity prices), far-red light to expand leaves early and speed up canopy closure, and machine-vision systems that measure projected leaf area daily and assimilate it into growth models like this one to predict harvest dates (Kozai, 2013, gives the resource-use-efficiency framework).

What the model leaves out

The model is a “big-leaf” description of the canopy: it has no individual leaves, no nitrogen or water limitation, no effect of spectrum or of root-zone temperature, and it treats the crop as uniform. It is deliberately simple enough to calibrate with a handful of fresh-weight measurements — which is exactly what the Compare with my data panel does.

The equations behind the simulation

State variable: the total dry weight W of one plant (g). The model is integrated with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta scheme in quarter-day steps. Parameters are from Van Henten (1994) unless stated otherwise; symbols follow his notation where possible.

The leaf area of a plant is its shoot dry weight times the specific leaf area; the leaf area index is the leaf area of all plants on one square metre of ground.

Assumptions and validity

SLA is constant. Van Henten calibrated it so that the canopy intercepts the right amount of light; for a heading lettuce much of this leaf area sits inside the head, so the computed LAI late in the cycle (10 or more) is larger than the area of exposed leaves. Because interception saturates, this hardly affects growth — try halving SLA and see how little the harvest weight changes.

\[ f = 1 - e^{-k\,\text{LAI}} \]

The fraction of light intercepted by the canopy rises exponentially towards one as leaf area index increases; k sets how quickly.

Follow the derivation
  1. Think of the canopy as thin horizontal layers, each with leaf area dL per m² of ground. Each layer intercepts the fraction k dL of the light reaching it: dI = −k I dL.
  2. Integrating from the top (L = 0, I = I₀) to the bottom (L = LAI): I = I₀ e−k LAI.
  3. The intercepted fraction is (I₀ − I)/I₀ = 1 − e−k LAI. For small LAI it is ≈ k·LAI: every new leaf counts fully.
Assumptions and validity

A horizontally uniform canopy. Young, widely spaced plants form clumps, which in reality intercept somewhat less than the formula says. The tray view uses f/ρ as the projected area of each plant.

Radiation-use efficiency from canopy photosynthesis(G4)
\[ \text{RUE} = c_\alpha c_\beta\,\frac{P(I,C,T)}{I}, \qquad P = \frac{\varepsilon I\;\sigma\,(C-\Gamma)}{\varepsilon I + \sigma\,(C-\Gamma)}, \qquad \varepsilon = c_\varepsilon\,\frac{C-\Gamma}{C+2\Gamma}, \qquad \frac{1}{\sigma} = \frac{1}{g_{bnd}} + \frac{1}{g_{stm}} + \frac{1}{\sigma_{car}(T)} \]

Canopy photosynthesis is a rectangular hyperbola: proportional to light at low light (slope ε, reduced by photorespiration), and limited by CO₂ supply through three conductances in series at high light. Dividing by the light gives the efficiency per photon; cα converts CO₂ to sugar and cβ sugar to dry matter.

Modifiers and calibration
  1. At 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, 400 ppm and 20 °C the equation gives RUEref = 0.90 g mol⁻¹. The laboratory writes RUE = RUEref·RUEVH(I, C, T)/RUEVH(ref), so the slider “RUE at the reference conditions” scales the whole response while keeping its shape.
  2. The readouts split the ratio into a light modifier fI (at 400 ppm, 20 °C), a CO₂ modifier fC (at the current light, 20 °C) and a temperature modifier fT.
  3. Examples: doubling PPFD from 250 to 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ lowers RUE by about 30 %; raising CO₂ from 400 to 1000 ppm raises it by about half at 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

Photosynthesis is evaluated at the mean PPFD of the photoperiod, which is exact for constant LED light and an approximation for daylight. The CO₂ response is that of Van Henten's greenhouse calibration; at 1000 ppm and above it may overestimate growth, and many crops acclimate to high CO₂.

Temperature responses and maintenance respiration(G5)
\[ \Gamma(T) = \Gamma_{20}\,2^{(T-20)/10}, \qquad R_m = c_\beta\left[c_{r,s}(1-\tau) + c_{r,r}\tau\right] \rho W \sum_{\text{day, night}} t_i\,2^{(T_i-25)/10} \]

The CO₂ compensation point doubles with every 10 °C. Maintenance respiration is proportional to the living dry matter and doubles with every 10 °C; it runs for the photoperiod at the day temperature and for the rest of the day at the night temperature.

\[ \text{RGR} = \frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln W}{\mathrm{d}t} \approx \frac{\ln W_2 - \ln W_1}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad t_{2\times} = \frac{\ln 2}{\text{RGR}} \]

Calibration by least squares(G8)
\[ \widehat{\text{RUE}} = \arg\min_{\text{RUE}} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\text{FW}_{\text{obs},i} - \text{FW}_{\text{model}}(t_i;\ \text{RUE})\right]^2, \qquad \text{SE} = \sqrt{s^2\,\big(J^{\mathsf T}J\big)^{-1}} \]

The calibrated RUE is the value that minimises the sum of squared differences between observed and modelled fresh weights; its standard error follows from the scatter of the residuals and the sensitivity of the model to RUE.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(t_i,\ \text{FW}_{\text{obs},i}\)day and fresh weight of observation id, g
\(s^2\)residual variance, SSE/(n − p)g²
\(J\)Jacobian: ∂FWmodel/∂RUE at each tig mol g⁻¹
How it is done
  1. The model is non-linear in RUE (because more growth makes more leaf area), so the minimum is found iteratively with the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, starting from the current slider value.
  2. The Jacobian is computed numerically by running the model twice with slightly different RUE.
  3. Optionally the transplant fresh weight is fitted as a second parameter, which is useful if you did not weigh the transplants.
  4. Individual plants are used as observations (not means), so the standard error reflects plant-to-plant variation. Repeated weighing of the same plants makes the observations dependent; the SE is then too small.
SSE in g²; SE of RUE in g mol⁻¹ ✓

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator and the charts. Tick each task when you are done; Save investigation copies your settings and results into your notebook.

  1. Same light, different photoperiod

    With the default settings, record the harvest fresh weight for photoperiods of 12, 16, 20 and 24 h at DLI 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹. Explain the trend with the RUE readout and Eq. G4. Repeat at 400 and 1000 ppm CO₂: when is a long photoperiod most worthwhile?

  2. Spacing, re-spacing and yield

    Switch re-spacing off. Find the constant density that maximises the yield per m² per cycle while still producing heads of at least 150 g in 24 days. Compare with the Cornell strategy (97 → 38 m⁻²). Use the intercepted-light chart to explain why re-spacing raises the productivity of the growing area.

  3. What is CO₂ worth?

    Store the default as B, then lower CO₂ to 400 ppm. By how many days does the time to a 150 g head change? Repeat at a DLI of 8 and 25 mol m⁻² d⁻¹. At which light level does CO₂ enrichment pay off most in relative terms, and why (look at fC)?

  4. Warm nights

    Keep the day temperature at 24 °C and vary the night temperature from 12 to 26 °C. How much fresh weight does each degree of night warming cost at harvest? Which equation is responsible? Then find the day temperature that maximises growth at night 18 °C and explain the optimum with fT.

  5. Calibrate the model with data

    Load the example data, calibrate RUE, and report the estimate with its standard error, R² and RMSE. Then calibrate with also fit the transplant fresh weight. Does the RUE estimate change? Finally load your own grow log, set the model to your conditions (DLI from your PPFD and photoperiod, temperature, density) and calibrate. Discuss three reasons why your RUE differs from Van Henten's 0.90 g mol⁻¹.

  6. Is RUE really constant?

    In the Monteith plot, compare the model line with the dashed straight line of constant RUE. When and why do they diverge? Set maintenance respiration off and repeat. What would you need to measure in your own experiment to draw this plot with real data?

Model assumptions

  • One state variable (total dry weight per plant), a uniform “big-leaf” canopy and daily time steps (RK4, four sub-steps per day). No nutrient, water or root-zone limitations; no effect of light spectrum; no development stages (bolting, tipburn).
  • Parameters from the one-state lettuce model of Van Henten (1994), calibrated and validated for greenhouse lettuce: cα = 0.68, cβ = 0.8, cε = 17 × 10⁻⁹ kg J⁻¹, Γ₂₀ = 40 ppm, Q₁₀ = 2, gbnd = 0.004 m s⁻¹, gstm = 0.007 m s⁻¹, the σcar(T) polynomial, k = 0.9, SLA = 62.5 m² kg⁻¹, root fraction 0.07, maintenance respiration 3.47 × 10⁻⁷ and 1.16 × 10⁻⁷ s⁻¹ at 25 °C, fresh/dry ratio 22.5.
  • RUE is Van Henten's canopy photosynthesis per mole of intercepted PAR, evaluated at the mean PPFD of the photoperiod (exact for constant electric light, approximate for sunlight), with 4.57 µmol J⁻¹ for PAR; the slider scales it for calibration. CO₂ is assumed to be held during the photoperiod.
  • Temperature: one day and one night temperature; photosynthesis uses the day temperature, respiration both.
  • The default scenario follows the Cornell hydroponic lettuce system (Both et al., 1997; Brechner & Both, 2013): DLI 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, 24 °C day and about 18.5 °C night, re-spacing from 97 to 38 plants m⁻² and harvest 24 days after transplanting, which produced heads of about 150 g. CO₂ was enriched up to 1000 ppm when the vents were closed; the model uses a constant 700 ppm as an approximation, and a transplant fresh weight of 1 g is assumed. With these settings the model gives 150 g at harvest.
  • The tray shows plants on a square grid; each head's projected area is f/ρ (the canopy interception shared among the plants). Head drawings are procedural illustrations; leaf number increases with fresh weight.
  • The example data set in the Compare with my data panel is simulated (generated with a lower RUE and random plant-to-plant variation) for practice; it is not a measured data set.
