Aquaponic nutrient mass balance

Every gram of nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium in an aquaponic system arrives with the fish feed or the base you add — and must leave again: in fish, in plants, in sludge, as gas, or with discharged water. Anything else accumulates. Follow the flows in a live Sankey diagram, size the plant area and the biofilter the way designers do, discover why fish feed never matches a lettuce recipe, and compare a classic coupled system with one that recovers the nutrients locked in the sludge.

Accumulation in the water

What to look at first

  1. Read the nitrogen Sankey. One kilogram of 32 % protein feed brings 51 g of nitrogen. About 30 % stays in the fish, half is excreted as ammonia and urea, a fifth leaves in faeces and uneaten feed. Of the dissolved nitrogen, a quarter is lost as N₂ and N₂O; the lettuce takes up only a few grams; the rest is flushed out with exchange water or accumulates.
  2. Switch to phosphorus. The picture turns around: most feed phosphorus is bound in faeces and leaves with the sludge. Now raise Sludge mineralisation returned to 85 %: the grey sludge flow shrinks and the plant-available pool grows almost four-fold.
  3. Switch to potassium. The base you add for pH control (KOH) can supply several times more potassium than the feed. Move the KOH share slider and watch the potassium and calcium supply readouts: at the default design both run to thousands of per cent of the crop demand.
  4. Look at the ratios. In the first chart the grey “feed only” bars for P : N, K : N and Fe : N are far below 100 %: relative to nitrogen, fish feed delivers much less of these nutrients than a lettuce recipe contains. The blue bars — measured in lettuce aquaponic systems by Yang and Kim (2020) — are low for calcium too. Now lower Ca dissolved and watch the calcium bar follow.
  5. Design the plant area. The last chart shows that the “right” area depends on the rule: a feed-rate ratio of 60–100 g m⁻² d⁻¹ (Rakocy et al., 2006), FAO's 40–50 g m⁻² d⁻¹ for leafy greens, or an area that exactly consumes the nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium supplied by the feed. No single area balances every element.
  6. Stop exchanging water. Choose Zero exchange. The concentration curves keep rising for the whole year instead of levelling off: without exchange, denitrification or a larger crop, nitrate-N climbs past the 150–300 mg L⁻¹ that fish are reported to tolerate (Eck et al., 2019).
Key idea

For every element: input = output + accumulation. Fish feed brings nutrients in the proportions fish need, not plants. Nitrogen is in surplus, while phosphorus is trapped in sludge and potassium, calcium and iron often have to come from elsewhere — the pH-control base and chelated iron in coupled systems, or recovered sludge and fertiliser in decoupled ones.

From feed to fish, sludge and plants

A classical fish feed contains 6–8 % organic nitrogen, about 1.2 % phosphorus and 40–45 % organic carbon (Timmons & Ebeling, 2013, in Eck et al., 2019). The commercial pellet used by Yang and Kim (2020) is typical: 41 % protein (> 6.88 % total N on the label), > 1.10 % P, 0.99 % K, 2.25–2.75 % Ca, 0.23 % Mg and 40 mg Fe kg⁻¹. Once eaten, each element is partitioned three ways: retained in fish growth, excreted in dissolved form, or egested in faeces (Figure 1). The partition differs strongly between elements: nitrogen is mostly excreted in dissolved form, phosphorus mostly in faeces.

What the plants take: transpiration × solution

Plant uptake is complicated, but a robust first estimate is that a crop removes nutrients at roughly the concentrations of a good nutrient solution in the water it transpires (Goddek et al., 2019, Eqs. 8.12–8.15). For lettuce transpiring 1.3 L m⁻² d⁻¹ (the annual average in Example 8.2 of Goddek et al., 2019) and the Cornell lettuce solution (125 mg N, 31 mg P, 215 mg K, 84 mg Ca and 0.95 mg Fe per litre; Brechner & Both, 2013), the crop removes 0.16 g N, 0.04 g P and 0.28 g K per m² per day. A harvest-based check gives the same order of magnitude: 25 heads per m² (FAO: 20–25; Somerville et al., 2014) of 152 g shoot fresh mass harvested every 28 days yield ≈ 136 g m⁻² d⁻¹, and with the tissue contents measured by Yang and Kim (2020) — 1.63 g N and 2.54 g K per kg fresh shoot — they remove 0.22 g N and 0.35 g K m⁻² d⁻¹.

Common misconception

“Aquaponic water lacks potassium, so there cannot be too much potassium in the fish feed.” Whether an element is deficient depends on which element you size the system on. At a feed-rate ratio of 70 g m⁻² d⁻¹ (the default) the feed alone supplies about twice the potassium that lettuce removes, while nitrogen is in large surplus. Enlarge the crop until it uses all the nitrogen — the “Balance N” area of about 120 m² — and potassium runs out at about 31 m², phosphorus at 46 m², iron almost at once. Seawright et al. (1998) concluded that no fish-to-plant ratio matches plant needs when fish feed is the only input, and Delaide et al. (2016) found K, P and Fe lacking while N and Ca accumulated in a coupled system (both in Eck et al., 2019).

Sizing rules

Rakocy et al. (2006) balanced the UVI raft system with a feed-rate ratio of 60–100 g of feed per m² of plant area per day for tilapia on a 32 % protein diet; this system is over-supplied with nitrogen and relies on passive denitrification (Lennard, 2017, in Lennard & Goddek, 2019). FAO recommends 40–50 g m⁻² d⁻¹ for leafy greens and 50–80 g m⁻² d⁻¹ for fruiting crops (Somerville et al., 2014). Designs that remineralise all fish solids and add only the nutrients missing from the fish waste can go below 11 g m⁻² d⁻¹ for some leafy greens (Lennard, 2017, in Lennard & Goddek, 2019). Goddek et al. (2019) size the plant area on one limiting element, usually phosphorus, a finite resource: \(A = \text{supply}/(ET_c\,\rho_{\text{target}})\) (Eq. B6).

Accumulation, exchange and the time scale

Whatever is not taken up or lost accumulates in the water until the water exchange removes it as fast as it arrives. The steady-state concentration is surplus ÷ exchange flow, and it is approached with the time constant V/Q: at 1.5 % exchange per day, 67 days. A system run with very little exchange therefore takes months to show its final nitrate — and may pass the 150–300 mg NO₃-N L⁻¹ that fish are reported to tolerate (Eck et al., 2019) long after a design review. Measured systems recover only 34–51 % of the feed nitrogen in fish and plants (Hu et al., 2015; Zou et al., 2016); the readout “N recovered in fish + plants” shows the model's value.

Supplementing the missing elements

“Fish simply do not need the same amounts of iron, potassium and calcium that plants require” (Somerville et al., 2014). FAO therefore recommends adding chelated iron to about 2 mg Fe L⁻¹, and adding calcium and potassium with the base that neutralises the acid produced by nitrification — as calcium or potassium hydroxide or carbonate, with more calcium for leafy crops and more potassium for fruiting crops. In the model, the base demand follows the nitrified nitrogen (7.07 g CaCO₃ per g N, less 3.57 g per g N returned by denitrification and nitrate uptake; Eq. B8), and the KOH share decides how it is split between K and Ca. Because the base demand scales with the feed while plant demand scales with the area, the base can supply far more K or Ca than the crop uses. Yang and Kim (2020) kept the pH of their lettuce aquaponic systems near 7 with KOH, Ca(OH)₂ and NaOH; the water averaged 114 mg K L⁻¹ — a third of their hydroponic solution — but only 20 mg Ca L⁻¹, and 70 mg Na L⁻¹ from the NaOH.

Coupled or decoupled?

In a coupled (one-loop) system the sludge is usually discarded, and with it most of the phosphorus. Decoupled systems pass the sludge through mineralisation reactors — around 90 % of the nutrients in sludge can be mobilised at pH 4 (Jung & Lovitt, 2011, in Goddek et al., 2019) — and send the nutrient-rich effluent to a separate plant loop, where the pH can be kept at the plants' optimum and nutrients topped up with fertiliser (Goddek et al., 2019). The chart “What the sludge holds back” compares discarding the sludge with returning 85 % of it: the gain is largest for the elements bound in the solids — phosphorus and, in the model, precipitated iron.

Frontier — closing the phosphorus loop

Phosphorus is mined from finite rock deposits, and fish sludge holds more than half of the feed phosphorus. Upflow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) reactors that first remove carbon as biogas and then mobilise the nutrients at low pH are being developed for aquaponics (Goddek et al., 2018, in Goddek et al., 2019). Mass balances like the one in this lab are the tool for deciding whether such reactors pay off.

Investigation tasks

  1. Size a system three ways

    For 2 kg of feed per day, find the plant area by (a) the UVI feed-rate ratio 60–100 g m⁻² d⁻¹, (b) FAO's 45 g m⁻² d⁻¹, (c) balancing N and (d) balancing P. Explain in two sentences why the answers differ by an order of magnitude and which you would build for lettuce.

  2. Where does phosphorus go?

    With sludge discarded, what percentage of the feed P ends in fish, plants and sludge? Raise the mineralisation to 85 %: how much more P reaches the plants, and how much could the plant area grow before P becomes limiting? Relate to the frontier box.

  3. Nitrate, exchange and time

    Record nitrate-N after 30, 120 and 365 days for exchange rates of 0, 1, 3 and 5 % d⁻¹. Which combinations stay below 150 mg NO₃-N L⁻¹, the lower end of what fish are reported to tolerate? Estimate the water used per kg of feed for the lowest acceptable exchange rate.

  4. Calibrate the calcium partition

    Lower Ca dissolved until the “Feed + base supplements” Ca : N bar matches the bar measured by Yang and Kim (2020). What dissolved share do you find, and what does that suggest about where feed calcium goes? What does it mean for the KOH share you would choose?

  5. Split the base

    Find the KOH share that brings potassium and calcium supply both closest to 100 % of crop demand. Then enlarge the plant area to the N-balanced value and repeat. What happens to the base need, and could any split satisfy both elements?

  6. A trout system in Sweden

    Use the Trout feed preset (45 % protein). How do the N surplus, the base demand and the biofilter volume change compared with tilapia feed at 32 %? What does a cooler greenhouse with lower transpiration (1.0 L m⁻² d⁻¹) mean for the plant area?

Model assumptions

  • A steady feed input and long-term average partition fractions (Table 1); fish biomass, growth stage and ration effects are folded into those fractions.
  • All dissolved and mineralised nitrogen is nitrified; denitrification removes a fixed fraction of it. Phosphorus precipitation with calcium is included implicitly in the solids fraction.
  • Plant uptake equals transpiration × reference-solution concentration (Goddek et al., 2019), but never more than the plant-available supply; luxury uptake and growth stages are ignored.
  • Only 10 % of the iron that is not bound in sludge is assumed to stay plant-available at pH ≈ 7 (illustrative).
  • Accumulation follows a single well-mixed volume with constant exchange; evaporation is replaced by clean water that adds no nutrients (tap water with Ca, Mg or Na is ignored).
  • Base demand uses the design value of 7.07 g CaCO₃ per g N nitrified (Lesson 6.1, Eq. 6.1.7) and returns 3.57 g per g N denitrified or taken up as nitrate. The alkalinity that fish add when they excrete ammonia as NH₃, which then takes up a proton (up to 1 eq per mol N), and the alkalinity of make-up water are neglected, as in the usual design rule — so the base demand, and the K and Ca it supplies, are upper estimates.
  • The calcium partition is uncertain (see Table 1); the potassium and iron partitions are fixed.
