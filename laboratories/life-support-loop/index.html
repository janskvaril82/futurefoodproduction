Bioregenerative life-support loop

Four astronauts on Mars breathe out about 4 kg of CO₂ and eat about 50 MJ of food every day. Plants can turn one into the other — and purify water on the way — but only if you give them enough area and light. Choose a crew, a crop mix and a lighting regime, and a NASA crop model tells you what fraction of the oxygen, food energy, protein and water the plants close, how much still has to come from Earth, and how many kilowatts it costs. The loop diagram shows every flow as a band whose width is proportional to its mass.

What to look at first

  1. Start with the salad machine. Click the preset Salad machine (Veggie-like): less than a square metre of lettuce and tomato under 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, much like the plant chambers flown on the International Space Station. In the loop diagram the plant chamber is almost disconnected: the thick orange dashed bands show oxygen, food and water arriving from Earth. The readouts give its real value — well under 1 % of the crew's oxygen and food.
  2. Close the loop. Click Full closure (180 m² for four people). Now the O₂ and CO₂ bands between crew, cabin and plants balance, food flows from the plant chamber to the crew, and the resupply bands disappear. Look at the power readout: about 56 kW — roughly 14 kW per person, day and night, for light alone.
  3. Watch the water. Switch the diagram to Water. The transpiration band is by far the thickest in the whole loop: more than a tonne of water a day passes through the plants and is condensed from the cabin air as clean water. The crew uses only a few per cent of it; the rest goes back to the plants as nutrient solution.
  4. Follow the carbon. Set Inedible biomass oxidised to 0 %. The O₂ closure jumps, because the O₂ that the plants released while making straw, leaves and roots is no longer consumed by burning them — but the CO₂ removal changes and the plants now lock carbon away in a growing pile of stored biomass. In a truly closed loop the inedible biomass must be recycled, and then only the carbon the crew eats pays for the crew's oxygen.
  5. Change the light. Switch the light regime to Same for all crops and vary the PPFD. Wheat was grown by NASA at very high light (115 mol m⁻² d⁻¹); at 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 16 h it needs less power per square metre but yields far less. Watch Electricity per unit of food energy and the last column of the crop table.
  6. Run the mission. Press Run the mission to count the days of a 500-day surface stay and the tonnes of resupply the plants save compared with a crew that brings everything with it.
Key idea

A bioregenerative life-support system is a set of coupled mass balances. Plants close the oxygen and food loops together — because both come from the same carbon — and they close the water loop almost for free. The price is area and light: tens of square metres and more than ten kilowatts of electricity per person.

Why grow plants in space?

On the International Space Station oxygen is made by electrolysing water, CO₂ is removed by absorbent beds, water is recycled physico-chemically and food comes from Earth. Such physico-chemical systems are compact and reliable, and water recovery has reached 98 % (NASA, 2023) — but they cannot make food, and every kilogram of food must be launched. For a crew on the Moon or Mars for years, growing food locally becomes attractive, and plants bring more than calories: they remove CO₂, release O₂ and purify water through transpiration. A system in which living organisms regenerate the atmosphere, water and food is a bioregenerative life-support system (BLSS).

Oxygen and food come from the same carbon

Photosynthesis releases roughly one molecule of O₂ for every atom of carbon fixed into biomass (the oxygen production fraction is 1.02–1.16 mol O₂ per mol C in the crop model). Human metabolism does the reverse, consuming 0.816 kg of O₂ and releasing 1.04 kg of CO₂ per day (a respiratory quotient of about 0.93). If the inedible straw, leaves and roots are oxidised back to CO₂ in a bioreactor — which a closed loop must do, or its carbon slowly disappears into storage — they consume exactly the O₂ that their growth released. What is left for the crew is the O₂ that came with the carbon they eat. Closing the food loop and closing the oxygen loop are therefore almost the same problem. Crops with a high harvest index, such as potato (70 %) and lettuce (90 %), keep a larger share of the oxygen they release than wheat (40 %) once their waste is recycled.

How much area?

NASA's Baseline Values and Assumptions Document (BVAD) tabulates the growing area needed for three example diets: 1.35 m² per crew member for a diet that only adds fresh salad crops to stored food, 19.5 m² when carbohydrate crops such as potato and wheat are added, and 65 m² when all candidate crops supply most of the diet (Anderson et al., 2018, Table 4-99). The simulator reproduces the same order of magnitude: 40–50 m² per person under NASA's high light levels closes food energy and oxygen, and much more at lower light.

The price: light

Every mole of photons at the canopy costs \(10^6/(\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}})\) joules of electricity. Wheat grown at the 115 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ used in NASA's high-productivity studies (Bugbee & Salisbury, 1988) needs over 500 W of electricity per square metre, around the clock. Of that electricity, about 60 % leaves the LEDs as light, much of which is absorbed by leaves; only 2–3 % ends up as chemical energy in biomass and about 1 % as food energy on the plate. The rest is heat that the habitat must reject to space — a radiator problem as large as the power problem. This is why the kWh per MJ of food readout is so large, and why space agencies study sunlight collection, more efficient LEDs and crops with a higher harvest index.

From test beds to missions

NASA's Biomass Production Chamber at Kennedy Space Center produced the crop data behind the BVAD tables (Wheeler et al., 2003; Wheeler, 2017). The EDEN ISS space-analogue greenhouse at the German Neumayer III station in Antarctica grew 26 crops on 12.5 m² in 2018 and harvested over 268 kg of edible biomass — about 27 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹, or 75 g m⁻² d⁻¹ (Zabel et al., 2020). On the ISS, the Veggie unit grows a few plants under about 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of red, blue and green LED light (Khodadad et al., 2020): valuable for diet variety and crew morale, but — as the salad-machine preset shows — not a life-support system.

Water: the easy loop

A crop transpires 2–12 kg of water per square metre per day (Anderson et al., 2018). Condensed from the cabin air, this water is effectively distilled. A BLSS can therefore irrigate its plants with biologically treated wastewater and harvest clean water from the air conditioning: plants replace much of a physico-chemical water processor. The limiting loops are food and oxygen, not water.

Frontier 2024–2026

ESA's MELiSSA pilot plant and China's Lunar Palace experiments are testing long-duration crewed closed loops on the ground, and plant-growth hardware such as Veggie and the Advanced Plant Habitat continues to fly on the ISS. The open questions are about reliability: can a biological loop be controlled well enough to be trusted with human lives, and how should it be combined with physico-chemical back-ups?

The equations behind the simulation

The crop model is the Modified Energy Cascade (MEC) of Cavazzoni (2004), with the coefficients published in BVAD Rev. 1 (Anderson et al., 2018, §4.14.1). Equation numbers S1–S12 refer to this laboratory.

Daily light integral and lighting power(S2)
\[ \text{DLI}=\frac{\text{PPFD}\cdot H\cdot 3600}{10^6},\qquad P_{\text{el}}=(1+f_{\text{aux}})\sum_i \frac{A_i\,\text{DLI}_i\,10^6}{86\,400\;\eta_{\text{ph}}\;\eta_{\text{cap}}} \]

the daily light integral is the photon flux density times the photoperiod in seconds; the average electrical power is the number of photons each crop needs per second, averaged over the day, divided by the photons each joule of electricity delivers to the canopy, plus an auxiliary fraction for fans and pumps.

Follow the derivation
  1. Photons needed per second, averaged over 24 h: \(\text{DLI}\times10^6/86\,400\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  2. Each joule of electricity gives \(\eta_{\text{ph}}\) µmol, of which \(\eta_{\text{cap}}\) reach the plants, so the power per m² is the photon rate divided by \(\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}}\) (W = J s⁻¹).
  3. The average power depends on the DLI only, not on how it is split between intensity and photoperiod; the installed (peak) power is \(24/H\) times larger.

Light absorbed by a developing canopy(S3)
\[ A(t)=A_{\max}\left(\frac{t}{t_A}\right)^{n}\quad (t < t_A),\qquad A = A_{\max}=0.93\quad(t\ge t_A),\qquad \text{PPF}_E=\text{PPF}\,\frac{H}{H_0} \]

the fraction of light absorbed by the canopy grows as a power of time until the canopy closes at tA, and then stays at 93 %. The time to canopy closure is a polynomial in the effective PPF (the PPF scaled by the photoperiod relative to its nominal value) and the CO₂ concentration.

Assumptions and validity

BVAD Table 4-129 gives lower maximum absorption for lettuce, potato, sweet potato and rice under low light (≈ 200–300 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), because the canopy never closes; those corrections are not applied here, so yields at low PPFD are optimistic.

Daily carbon gain(S4)
\[ \text{DCG}=0.0036\,H\,\text{CUE}_{24}\,A\,\text{CQY}\,\text{PPF},\qquad \text{CQY}_{\max}=\sum_{j,k} c_{jk}\,\text{PPF}^{j}\,[\text{CO}_2]^{k} \]

the carbon fixed per square metre per day is the photoperiod in seconds (the factor 0.0036 also converts µmol to mol) times the fraction of fixed carbon kept after respiration, times the fraction of light absorbed, times the canopy quantum yield (carbon fixed per photon absorbed), times the photon flux.

Follow the derivation
  1. Gross photosynthesis per second: photons absorbed × carbon fixed per photon = \(A\,\text{CQY}\,\text{PPF}\) µmol C m⁻² s⁻¹ (Eq. 4-25 of BVAD).
  2. Multiply by \(3600H\) seconds of light per day and by \(10^{-6}\) mol per µmol: \(0.0036H\).
  3. Respiration by day and night consumes a fraction \(1-\text{CUE}_{24}\) of the carbon fixed (Monje & Bugbee, 1998).
  4. CQY falls with PPF (light saturation) and rises with CO₂ (suppressed photorespiration) — try the calculator.

From a crop cycle to a steady food supply

With staggered plantings, each square metre is occupied by one crop cycle at a time, so the average productivity is TEB divided by the cycle length. The simulator uses the MEC integrals only as ratios (Eq. S8), so the cycle length cancels.

\(\dfrac{\text{g mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{d}^{-1}}{\text{g g}^{-1}}=\text{g m}^{-2}\text{d}^{-1}\) ✓

the plants release OPF moles of oxygen and take up one mole of CO₂ for every mole of carbon they add to their biomass.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue

Why is OPF larger than 1?

Building a sugar (CH₂O) releases exactly one O₂ per C. Biomass also contains proteins and lipids, which are more reduced than sugar, and plants reduce nitrate to amino groups; both release extra oxygen. Soybean, rich in oil and protein, has the highest OPF.

mol mol⁻¹ × mol m⁻² d⁻¹ = mol m⁻² d⁻¹ ✓ (× 32 g mol⁻¹ or 44 g mol⁻¹)
Canopy transpiration(S7)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{VPD}=0.611\,e^{17.4T/(T+239)}\,(1-\text{RH}),\qquad g_S=\left(1.717\,T-19.96-10.54\,\text{VPD}\right)\frac{P_{\text{net}}}{[\text{CO}_2]} \\ g_C=\frac{g_A\,g_S}{g_A+g_S},\qquad \text{DTR}=3600\,H\,\frac{M_w}{\rho_w}\,g_C\,\frac{\text{VPD}}{P_{\text{atm}}} \end{gathered} \]

the vapour-pressure deficit follows from temperature and humidity; the canopy's stomatal conductance is proportional to net photosynthesis divided by the CO₂ concentration (reduced at high deficit); it acts in series with the aerodynamic conductance; the daily transpiration is the conductance times the deficit as a mole fraction, over the light period, converted to litres.

The form of \(g_S\) is from Monje (1998) for broad-leaved (planophile) canopies; for wheat, BVAD uses \(g_S=0.1389+15.32\,\text{RH}\,P_{\text{net}}/[\text{CO}_2]\). Doubling CO₂ roughly halves \(g_S\) at the same photosynthesis — enriched atmospheres save water.

\[ X_i = X_{i,\text{BVAD}}\cdot\frac{\text{MEC}_X\!\left(\text{PPF},H,[\text{CO}_2],\text{RH}\right)}{\text{MEC}_X\!\left(\text{PPF}_{0},H_0,1200,0.75\right)} \]

Why anchor?

The MEC model was fitted to the same NASA data but reproduces the tabulated averages only within about ±40 % (for wheat, soybean and sweet potato, for example), and its transpiration is lower than the tabulated values. Anchoring keeps the nominal numbers identical to the BVAD tables that students can look up, while the MEC supplies physiologically sensible responses to light, photoperiod, CO₂ and humidity.

Worked number

The water loop(S11)
\[ \begin{gathered} I = T + W_{\text{bio}},\qquad W_{\to p}=\min(0.95\,W_{\text{crew}},\,I),\qquad W_{\text{rest}}=W_{\text{crew}}-\frac{W_{\to p}}{0.95} \\ \text{resupply}_W=\max\!\left[0,\; D_W+(I-W_{\to p})-T-\eta_{\text{WRS}}W_{\text{rest}}-f_{\text{ox}}W_{\text{ined}}\right] \end{gathered} \]

the plants take up their transpiration plus the water in the harvested biomass; biologically treated crew wastewater is used first as nutrient solution; what is left goes through the physico-chemical water recovery system; clean water comes from condensed transpiration, from the recovery system and from drying the inedible biomass; any shortfall must come from Earth.

A surprising result

\(T\) appears on both sides — as plant uptake and as condensate — and cancels. However large the transpiration, it is recycled almost perfectly; what costs water is what leaves the loop: brine from the recovery system, bioreactor losses and water locked in stored biomass.

Model assumptions

  • Steady state with staggered plantings: every square metre always carries a crop at some stage, so daily productivities are cycle averages. No storage dynamics, crop failures or harvest scheduling.
  • Nominal productivities, O₂, CO₂ and transpiration are BVAD Tables 4-96 to 4-98 (NASA ground studies with hydroponics, elevated CO₂ and high light). Responses to light, photoperiod, CO₂ (330–1300 µmol mol⁻¹) and humidity come from the MEC model; the nominal CO₂ (1200 µmol mol⁻¹) and relative humidity (75 %) behind the tables are assumed. Low-light canopy corrections (BVAD Table 4-129), temperature responses and tip-burn in lettuce are not modelled.
  • Food composition is the USDA FoodData Central (SR Legacy) value for the raw edible portion, applied on a dry-matter basis; processing losses, cooking and micronutrients are ignored. Protein quality is not considered.
  • Crew interface values are BVAD Table 3-33 (82 kg crew member, RQ 0.93); the crew's water outputs are increased by the water eaten in fresh produce. Hygiene water follows BVAD Table 4-21.
  • Oxidised inedible biomass is assumed to consume the O₂ its growth released and to return all its carbon as CO₂ and its water as condensate. Biologically treated wastewater is 95 % recoverable as nutrient solution (assumption); the physico-chemical system recovers the chosen fraction; transpired water is fully condensed.
  • Lighting: photon efficacy and capture efficiency are uniform for all crops; auxiliary power is 10 % of lighting; PAR radiant output assumes 4.9 µmol J⁻¹; dry matter stores 17.5 kJ g⁻¹. Heat rejection, habitat pressure control and crew time are not included.
  • The loop diagram draws band widths proportional to the daily mass flow within the selected view; very small flows are drawn with a minimum width. The nutrient band is qualitative.
