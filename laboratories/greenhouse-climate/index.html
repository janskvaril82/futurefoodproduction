Greenhouse climate simulator

Run a Venlo glasshouse full of tomatoes through a whole day, as its climate computer does. The sun climbs and sets, the roof vents open when the air gets too warm, the energy screen slides shut on a cold night, hot water flows through the heating pipes, water droplets form on the glass and liquid CO₂ is dosed into the crop. Behind every movement is a dynamic model of heat, water vapour and CO₂ built from the same equations that greenhouse engineers use, fed with real climatological days for Västerås and Almería. Change a set-point and the whole day is recomputed in a fraction of a second.

A guided tour through five days

Each preset loads a climatological day (the 15th of the month) and a set of climate-computer settings. Press ▶ Play the day (or the space bar with the scene selected) and watch the 3D greenhouse, the readouts and the moving cursor on the charts. Click any component in the scene to see what it does and its live value.

  1. Swedish winter night, no screen. Load the first preset. At 02:00 it is −3.9 °C outside and the pipes, at about 60 °C, deliver roughly 157 W m⁻² to hold 18 °C. Look up from inside (the view is set for you): the glass is fogged with droplets. Its temperature (about 6 °C, violet line in the temperature chart) is far below the dew point of the greenhouse air (about 10 °C), so the roof is condensing water all night. The relative humidity settles near 60 %: the cold glass is an excellent, free dehumidifier.
  2. Now close the energy screen. Load the second preset. The fabric slides shut across every trellis bay, the heating power at 02:00 falls to about 100 W m⁻² and the pipes cool to 47 °C — a third less heat. But the relative humidity climbs to almost 80 %, because the humid air below the screen can no longer reach the cold glass. Watch the screen open briefly around noon, when there is enough daylight to be worth letting in.
  3. A sunny spring day. By mid-morning the sun has warmed the air past the ventilation line (heating set-point + 2 K); the vents start to open, in proportion to how far the temperature is above that line. The crop transpires up to 0.3 L m⁻² h⁻¹ and its photosynthesis pulls CO₂ down. While the vents are nearly closed the doser keeps 800 ppm; once they open more than 10 % the computer stops enriching and only holds the outside level.
  4. Summer heat in Almería. The vents are wide open from mid-morning until well after sunset and yet at 13:00 the air is 2 K warmer than outside; the leaf-to-air VPD reaches about 2.7 kPa, far above the comfortable band. A Dutch-style Venlo greenhouse with 10 % roof vents simply cannot ventilate enough here. Try the whitewash slider: chalk on the roof is how Almería growers cope.
  5. CO₂ with the vents open. This preset chases 1000 ppm on a Swedish July day with the vent interlock switched off. The doser runs at full capacity for most of the day, yet the concentration struggles to reach 550–650 ppm. The readout CO₂ dosed today shows where the gas went: about four-fifths of it left through the vents.
  6. A winter crop under LEDs. Without lamps, the dark January day gives the crop a negative carbon balance: it respires more CO₂ than it fixes. With 70 W m⁻² of LED top-lighting (about 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) the crop fixes over 25 g of CO₂ per m² per day — and the lamps' heat cuts the boiler's heat by a third.
Key idea

A greenhouse climate is the running balance of three quantities — heat, water vapour and CO₂ — each with its own sources and sinks, all linked through the crop and the air exchange. Every control action trades one against the others: closing a screen saves heat but traps vapour; opening vents removes heat and vapour but also the CO₂ you paid for; heating dries the air but costs energy.

What the simulator computes

The steady-state balance of Lesson 8.1 (Eq. 8.1.6) tells you how much heat a greenhouse needs on average. A real greenhouse is never in steady state: the sun rises in minutes, the air holds only a few kilojoules per kelvin, the floor stores heat by day and releases it at night, and the climate computer reacts continuously. The simulator therefore solves the balances as differential equations in time: every minute of simulated time it computes all the fluxes of heat, water vapour and CO₂, and updates the state of the greenhouse. It uses the structure and the parameters of the greenhouse climate model of Vanthoor et al. (2011a), in the open-source form of Katzin et al. (2020), simplified to the pieces that matter most for one day.

Heat: where the watts go

By day the dominant input is the sun: about 72 % of the global radiation on the roof is transmitted (Eq. G5), and a dense tomato canopy absorbs most of it. The absorbed energy has three destinations. Part evaporates water in the leaves (transpiration, a latent-heat flux that does not warm the air); part warms the leaves, which pass it to the air as sensible heat; and a little warms the floor, which stores it and gives it back at night. The air loses heat through the cover (convection to the glass and long-wave radiation from the crop to the glass, which in turn loses heat to the wind and radiates to the cold sky) and by ventilation. At night only the heating pipes (and the floor's stored heat) replace these losses.

The cover is modelled physically (Eq. G2), not with a fixed U-value, so you can watch the effective U-value change: about 5.4 W m⁻² K⁻¹ on an overcast Swedish January night with a light wind, 6.3 with 8 m s⁻¹ of wind and 6.6 under a clear sky, when the glass radiates to a sky at about −28 °C. These are the values quoted as “6–7.5” for single glass in Lesson 8.1 — and they show why the U-value is not a property of the glass alone.

Water vapour: the crop as a humidifier, the glass as a dehumidifier

A mature tomato crop transpires 1–1.5 L m⁻² on a dark January day and 3–5 L m⁻² on a bright summer day. That vapour must leave the air, or the humidity would rise until transpiration stopped. It leaves in two ways: carried out by ventilation (Eq. G3, G7), or condensed on any surface colder than the dew point of the air — above all the glass on a cold night. Condensation is free dehumidification, but it has a price: its latent heat is released into the glass and lost to the outside, so a humid greenhouse needs more heating. When the screen is closed, the humid air can reach only the underside of the screen, which is much warmer than the glass, and the small air flux through the fabric (Eq. G8); humidity rises. That is why growers open a screen gap or run a minimum pipe temperature when the relative humidity climbs.

Humidity and grey mould

Botrytis cinerea infects mainly when plant surfaces are wet or the air is close to saturation; limiting the daily wetting period by ventilation and heating reduces epidemics (Yunis et al., 1994; Elad et al., 2016). Climate computers therefore work with a humidity limit — typically 85–90 % RH — and with the margin between leaf temperature and dew point. The simulator shades RH > 85 % amber and > 90 % red — a risk indicator, not a disease model.

CO₂: the crop as a sink, the vents as a leak

In daylight a full crop fixes 20–40 µmol of CO₂ per m² of floor per second. The air above a square metre of floor contains only about 230 mol of air, so behind closed vents an uptake of 25 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ would lower the concentration by almost 400 ppm in an hour — in practice photosynthesis slows down first — unless CO₂ is supplied. At night the crop respires and the concentration rises. Enrichment to 800–1000 ppm raises photosynthesis by a quarter to a third (Eq. G9), but only while the vents are nearly closed: with the vents open, most of the dosed gas simply blows away. The CO₂ balance (Eq. G10) makes the arithmetic explicit, and the vent interlock of the climate computer — stop enriching above a small vent opening — is the standard remedy (see Lesson 8.3).

The climate computer

Commercial climate computers work with set-points and proportional bands. The heating line is the temperature below which heat is supplied; the ventilation line lies a few kelvin higher; between them is a dead zone in which neither heating nor ventilation is active, so that the two never fight each other. Above the ventilation line the vents open in proportion to the temperature excess (Figure 2). Humidity, light and CO₂ rules are layered on top.

Common misconception

“Opening the vents lets the heat out, so vents are for temperature.” On a winter night the vents are often opened a crack for humidity, even though every cubic metre of cold air must then be heated: in the simulator, a humidity limit of 75 % instead of 95 % on the screened January day opens the screen gap and the vents and raises the daily heat use by about 18 %; under LED lighting, 80 % instead of 95 % raises it by about half. Ventilation is the greenhouse's only large-scale exchange of heat, water vapour and CO₂ with the outside — all three at once.

Research frontier

Climate computers are moving from fixed set-points to optimisation. Wageningen University & Research's Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge let teams of data scientists and growers control real greenhouse compartments remotely with artificial intelligence; several AI teams produced more cucumbers or cherry tomatoes per unit of resources than the reference growers (Hemming et al., 2019, 2020). Models such as the one in this laboratory are the “digital twins” those algorithms learn from and plan with.

The equations behind the simulation

All fluxes are expressed per square metre of floor (W m⁻², kg m⁻² s⁻¹, µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Temperatures appear in °C except inside fourth powers, where they are absolute (K). Parameter values are those of the Dutch reference Venlo greenhouse and tomato crop of Vanthoor et al. (2011a, 2011b) unless the symbol table says otherwise.

Energy balances of the air (with crop) and of the floor(G1)

Follow the derivation
  1. Draw a control volume around the greenhouse air and the leaves. The first law says: rate of change of stored energy = inflows − outflows. The stored energy per m² floor is \(C_a T\) (air plus leaves, both at nearly the same temperature).
  2. The canopy is treated as a zero-capacity surface: the radiation it absorbs, \(R_n\), is split instantly into latent heat \(\lambda E\) (transpiration) and sensible heat \(H_{\text{can}}\) given to the air (Eq. G6). Only \(H_{\text{can}}\) appears in the air balance; \(\lambda E\) appears in the vapour balance (Eq. G7).
  3. The floor is a second store, one layer thick, exchanging heat with the air and radiating to the cover; the slow seasonal heat flux to the deep soil is neglected.
  4. Adding the air and floor balances and the canopy balance gives the whole-greenhouse balance shown in the Energy balance right now chart: sun + heating + lamps = cover + ventilation + transpiration + storage. In steady state (no storage) it is exactly Eq. 8.1.6 of Lesson 8.1.
Assumptions and validity

One well-mixed air volume (no vertical temperature gradient, no separate compartment above the screen); canopy and pipe temperatures quasi-steady; the heat capacity of the glass (about 8 kJ K⁻¹ m⁻²) is neglected. Vanthoor's full model has 16 states including five soil layers, the top compartment and the pipe water; the lumped version reproduces the daily course and the energy totals but not, for example, the thermal stratification under a closed screen.

Follow the derivation
  1. Glass is thin (4 mm) and conductive: its two faces differ by only about 0.5 K, so one temperature \(T_c\) describes it. Its heat capacity is small, so at every moment inflow = outflow (quasi-steady state).
  2. Inflows per m² of floor: Newton's law of cooling for the air (\(h_{ci}\), Eq. 8.1.2 of Lesson 8.1, times the cover-to-floor ratio), the Stefan–Boltzmann law for the canopy and floor (Eq. 8.1.3, with the view factors \(F_c\) and \(F_f\)), and the latent heat of any condensate.
  3. Outflows: Newton's law for the wind-cooled outer surface and Stefan–Boltzmann radiation to a sky at \(T_{\text{sky}}\).
  4. The equation is non-linear in \(T_c\) (fourth powers, the 0.33 exponent, condensation), so the simulator solves it by Newton iteration every time step. Dividing the heat actually leaving the cover by \((A_c/A_f)(T - T_o)\) gives the effective U-value of Lesson 8.1 — now an output that depends on wind, sky and condensation.

  3. Wind effect: a wind of speed \(v\) creates pressure differences \(\Delta p = C_w \rho v^2/2\) across the openings.
  4. The two effects are combined in quadrature under the square root, and half the opening area is used for inflow (Boulard & Baille, 1995). The coefficients \(C_d\) and \(C_w\) are fitted to measurements on Venlo greenhouses and taken from Vanthoor et al. (2011a).
Assumptions and validity

Air exchange rates measured in Venlo greenhouses span 0.2–1 h⁻¹ when closed and up to 30–60 h⁻¹ with wide-open vents in strong wind.

Crop transpiration (Stanghellini's form of Penman–Monteith)(G6)
\[ \lambda E = \frac{\Delta\,R_n + \dfrac{2\,\text{LAI}\,\rho_a c_p}{r_b}\,D}{\Delta + \gamma\left(1+\dfrac{r_s}{r_b}\right)}, \qquad r_s = r_{s,\min}\,f_R\,f_{\text{CO}_2}\,f_D, \qquad T_{\text{can}} = T + \frac{(R_n-\lambda E)\,r_b}{2\,\text{LAI}\,\rho_a c_p} \]

the latent heat used by transpiration is a weighted combination of the net radiation absorbed by the crop and the drying power of the air (its vapour pressure deficit), divided by a term that grows with the stomatal resistance. The stomatal resistance is a minimum value multiplied by factors for low light, high CO₂ and dry air. The leaves are warmer than the air when they absorb more energy than they transpire.

Follow the derivation

  2. Sensible heat leaves through the boundary layer: \(H = \dfrac{2\,\text{LAI}\,\rho_a c_p}{r_b}(T_{\text{can}} - T)\).
  3. The canopy stores no energy: \(R_n = \lambda E + H\).
  4. Linearise \(e_s(T_{\text{can}}) \approx e_s(T) + \Delta(T_{\text{can}}-T)\), so \(e_s(T_{\text{can}}) - e = D + \Delta(T_{\text{can}}-T)\).
  5. Eliminate \(T_{\text{can}}\) between the three equations — Penman's trick — to obtain the boxed formula. The canopy temperature then follows from \(H = R_n - \lambda E\).
  6. Example: \(R_n\) = 180 W m⁻², \(D\) = 0.8 kPa, 22 °C, LAI 3, \(r_s\) = 318 s m⁻¹. Then \(2\,\text{LAI}\,\rho_a c_p/r_b\) = 26.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹ and \(\lambda E\) = (0.161 × 180 + 26.3 × 0.8)/(0.161 + 0.145) = 163 W m⁻², i.e. 0.24 L m⁻² h⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

The whole canopy is one “big leaf” with one temperature; the stomatal response functions were fitted for tomato by Stanghellini (1987) and are used as in Vanthoor et al. (2011a). The VPD factor makes stomata close strongly in dry air, which is why transpiration rises less than proportionally with VPD. Negative values (dew on the leaves) are allowed.

Water-vapour balance and condensation(G7)
\[ \rho_a\,\bar h\,\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = E - M_{\text{cond}} - \rho_a\,G\,(W-W_o), \qquad M = 6.4\times10^{-9}\,h\,\big(e - e_s(T_s)\big) \quad \text{when } e > e_s(T_s) \]

the mass of dry air above a square metre of floor times the rate of change of its humidity ratio equals transpiration minus condensation minus the vapour carried out by ventilation. Water condenses on a surface at temperature \(T_s\) at a rate proportional to its convective heat-transfer coefficient and to the excess of the air's vapour pressure over the saturation vapour pressure at the surface.

The energy screen: less heat loss, more humidity(G8)
\[ \varphi_s = 1 - s\,(1-f_s), \quad \frac{Q_{\text{cov}}(f_s)}{Q_{\text{cov}}(1)} = 1-\text{ES}, \quad T_{su} = T - f_s\,(T-T_c), \quad M_{\text{scr}} = 6.4\times10^{-9}\,h_{su}\big(e-e_s(T_{su})\big) + \rho_a K_s |T-T_c|^{0.66}\big(W-W_{\text{sat}}(T_c)\big) \]

a closed screen multiplies all heat transfer from the inside to the cover by a factor \(f_s\) chosen so that the cover heat loss falls by the screen's energy saving ES. The underside of the screen then sits between air and glass temperature; with the screen closed, vapour is removed only by condensation on that warmer underside and by the small air flux through the fabric.

Canopy photosynthesis and CO₂ uptake(G9)
\[ J = \frac{J_{\text{pot}} + \alpha I_a - \sqrt{(J_{\text{pot}} + \alpha I_a)^2 - 4\Theta J_{\text{pot}}\,\alpha I_a}}{2\Theta}, \qquad P = \frac{J\,(C_s-\Gamma)}{4\,(C_s+2\Gamma)}, \qquad U = P\left(1-\frac{\Gamma}{C_s}\right) - R_m - R_g \]

the electron transport rate of the canopy is a smooth minimum of the light-limited rate and its temperature-dependent capacity; gross photosynthesis is electron transport divided by four, reduced by photorespiration through the ratio of the CO₂ compensation point to the CO₂ concentration in the leaf. The crop's net CO₂ uptake is gross photosynthesis minus photorespiration, maintenance respiration and growth respiration.

The climate computer: heating, vents, screen and CO₂(G11)
\[ Q_{\text{heat}} = \operatorname{clip}_{[Q_{\min},\,Q_{\max}]}\!\left(K_p\left[e + \frac{1}{T_i}\int e\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\right),\ e = T_h - T; \quad u^{*} = \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(\frac{T - T_v}{P_b}\right); \quad \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{u^{*}-u}{\tau_u}; \quad \phi_{\text{CO}_2} = \phi_{\max}\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(\frac{C_{sp}-C}{50}\right) \]

heat is supplied by a proportional–integral controller acting on the error between the heating set-point and the air temperature, limited between the minimum-pipe heat and the pipe capacity; the vents open linearly over a proportional band above the ventilation line and follow the command with a motor delay; CO₂ is dosed at a rate proportional to how far the concentration is below its set-point, up to the dosing capacity.

Why P and PI?
  1. A purely proportional heating controller would leave a permanent offset below the set-point on a cold night; the integral term removes it by slowly accumulating the error.
  2. For the vents a proportional band is deliberately kept: the air temperature rises above the ventilation line by an amount that tells the grower how hard the vents are working, and small temperature fluctuations do not make the vents hunt.
  3. The integral is frozen while the output is saturated (anti-windup), so the heating does not overshoot when the vents close in the evening.
Assumptions and validity

Real climate computers add many rules (wind-dependent vent limits, lee/windward vents, light-dependent temperature, pre-night temperature drops, screen opening in steps, CO₂ from boiler flue gas). The simulated computer keeps only the rules that shape the daily balances.

Model assumptions

  • The model represents one square metre of a large multi-span Venlo glasshouse (1 ha or more); the 3D scene shows a 20 m × 20 m section. Side-wall effects of a small greenhouse are ignored.
  • One well-mixed air volume at one temperature, humidity and CO₂ concentration; no separate compartment above the screen and no vertical gradients.
  • Canopy, cover and pipe temperatures are quasi-steady (no heat capacity); the floor is a single layer and the seasonal heat flux into the deep soil is neglected.
  • Solar transmissivity is constant (0.72) regardless of sun angle, and sunlight is half PAR, half NIR; long-wave transmission of glass is zero.
  • The cover is a single surface with the convective and radiative coefficients of Vanthoor et al. (2011a); the sky temperature is a monthly mean depression below the air temperature (or the Brutsaert clear-sky value).
  • Ventilation through roof vents on one side only, no insect screens; leakage proportional to wind speed.
  • Transpiration uses Stanghellini's (1987) big-leaf Penman–Monteith form with Vanthoor's stomatal response functions; the canopy temperature follows from it.
  • Photosynthesis is electron-transport limited (Farquhar et al., 1980, as in Vanthoor et al., 2011b); respiration uses Vanthoor's coefficients with organ masses assumed from the LAI; growth respiration is instantaneous.
  • The screen is an extra resistance calibrated to its energy saving; vapour removal with the screen closed is limited to the screen underside and the air flux through the fabric.
  • The climate computer is simplified (Table 1 of the Explain tab); actuators are first-order lags.
  • Weather is a smooth climatological day, not a measured sequence; the same day is repeated three times and the third is shown, so the result is periodic.
  • Grey-mould “risk” is an indicator based on RH thresholds, not a disease model. The 3D plants, fruits and equipment are illustrative; the charts and readouts are the computed quantities.
