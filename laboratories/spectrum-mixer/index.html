LED spectrum mixer

Mix seven LED channels — UV-A, royal blue, green, deep red, far-red, warm and cool white — the way a fixture designer does, and add sunlight for comparison. Every change is propagated through the physics: from electrical watts to radiant watts, from watts to photons, from photons to PPFD, ePAR and yield photon flux, to the red:far-red ratio and the phytochrome photostationary state, and finally to how bright and what colour the light looks to your eye. The grow tent on the left is rendered from the spectrum itself: each surface's colour is the product of the light, the surface reflectance and the CIE colour-matching functions.

A guided tour of the mixer

  1. Press “Blurple” red + blue. The tent turns magenta and the lettuce-green leaves look almost black-purple: there are hardly any green photons for them to reflect. Read the Illuminance: only about 8 lux per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (sunlight gives about 55), so this light looks dim even though its PPFD is high. Now read the Photon efficacy — red-and-blue fixtures are the most efficient, because deep-red LEDs deliver many photons per joule.
  2. Press “Broad white”. The plants look green again, the illuminance per µmol rises almost nine-fold (to about 73 lux per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), and the photon efficacy falls: some energy is lost in the phosphor that converts blue photons into longer wavelengths (the Stokes shift). Look at the CIE diagram: the mix sits almost on the black-body curve, which is why it looks “white”.
  3. Press “White + far-red”. Compared with broad white, PPFD barely changes — the 60 W of far-red photons lie outside 400–700 nm — but the ePAR flux rises by almost a fifth, R:FR falls from above 20 to about 2.3 and the phytochrome PSS from 0.83 to 0.78. On the morphology map the point moves left, and the illustrated plants stretch and lift their leaves: the classic shade-avoidance syndrome.
  4. Press “Sunlight”. The model spectrum (Planck 5778 K filtered by the atmosphere) lies on top of the ASTM G173 reference spectrum in the chart. Sunlight contains roughly one far-red photon (700–750 nm) for every five PAR photons, R:FR ≈ 1.1 and PSS ≈ 0.70.
  5. Switch the spectrum to Energy units. The same light now looks bluer: a blue photon carries 47 % more energy than a 660 nm photon. Photosynthesis counts photons, electricity bills count joules — engineers must juggle both.
  6. Turn off the white channels and raise only green. Green LEDs are inefficient (the “green gap”), but green light penetrates deeper into leaves and canopies. The efficacy bar chart shows the cost of each colour at a glance.
Key idea

A spectrum is not one number. The same electrical power can give very different photon flux (growth), photon composition (morphology and quality through photoreceptors), illuminance (what workers see) and colour rendering (whether you can spot a disease). A good spectrum is a deliberate compromise between efficacy, plant response and human needs.

From electrons to photons

An LED is a semiconductor p–n junction. When a current flows, electrons and holes recombine in thin quantum wells and each recombination can emit a photon whose energy is close to the band gap of the well material. Indium-gallium-nitride (InGaN) wells emit UV-A, violet, blue and green; aluminium-gallium-indium-phosphide (AlGaInP) wells emit red; aluminium-gallium-arsenide emits far-red. The emission of a single-colour LED is a narrow band with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of about 10–35 nm.

The wall-plug efficiency \(\eta_{\text{wp}}\) is the radiant power out divided by the electrical power in. Kusuma, Pattison and Bugbee (2020) compiled values for the best packages at a moderate current density (100 mA mm⁻²) and 25 °C junction temperature: 93 % for royal blue (450 nm), 81 % for deep red (660 nm), 77 % for far-red (730 nm), 76 % and 69 % for cool (6500 K) and warm (2700 K) phosphor-converted white, but only 42 % for green (530 nm) — the notorious green gap. These are the defaults in this laboratory. For UV-A at 385 nm the 2020 UV-emitter roadmap reports an external quantum efficiency of about 50 % (Amano et al., 2020), which corresponds to a wall-plug efficiency of about 45 % once the forward voltage is taken into account (Derive, S1).

Packages do not work at 25 °C and optimal current in a real fixture. Kusuma et al. list four fixture losses: current droop, thermal droop (≈ 10 % from 25 to 85 °C), driver losses (drivers are 85–95 % efficient) and optical losses (covers transmit ≈ 92 %). Multiplying the typical factors (0.90 × 0.90 × 0.90 × 0.92) gives 0.67 of the package efficacy; near-optimal engineering reaches ≈ 0.93. The fixture efficiency slider (\(\eta_{\text{fix}}\), default 0.80) represents this product.

Counting photons: PPFD, ePAR and YPF

Photosynthesis is a photochemical process: every absorbed photon can drive one electron transfer, whatever its energy. Plant lighting is therefore specified in photons. The classic definition of photosynthetically active radiation counts photons between 400 and 700 nm (PPFD). Zhen and Bugbee (2020) showed that far-red photons up to 750 nm, when they arrive together with shorter wavelengths, drive canopy photosynthesis as efficiently as PAR photons; this gives extended PAR (ePAR, 400–750 nm). McCree's (1972) relative quantum efficiency curve weights photons by their measured effectiveness in single leaves, giving the yield photon flux (YPF). The mixer computes all three so you can compare how much each definition changes the ranking of spectra.

Phytochrome: a photoreversible switch

Plants sense the colour of light with photoreceptors. Phytochromes exist in two interconvertible forms: Pr absorbs mainly red light (peak ≈ 665 nm) and is converted to the active form Pfr; Pfr absorbs mainly far-red (peak ≈ 730 nm) and is converted back. Under any constant spectrum the two reactions reach a dynamic equilibrium, the photostationary state PSS = Pfr/(Pr + Pfr). Sager et al. (1988) showed how to compute it from a measured spectrum and the photochemical cross-sections of the two forms; here we use the cross-sections of purified oat phytochrome measured by Lagarias et al. (1987), as tabulated by Kusuma and Bugbee (2021).

Because leaves absorb red strongly but transmit and reflect far-red, light under a canopy is depleted in red: R:FR drops from about 1.1 in open sunlight to well below 1 in shade (Smith, 1982). Plants read a low PSS as “neighbours are shading me” and respond with the shade-avoidance syndrome: faster stem and petiole extension, more upright (hyponastic) leaves, larger but thinner leaves, reduced branching and earlier flowering in many species. Morgan and Smith (1976) found a linear relationship between PSS and the stem extension rate of plants grown under simulated natural radiation. Growers exploit this: adding far-red increases leaf expansion and light capture of young plants (Park & Runkle, 2017), whereas a high PSS keeps transplants compact.

Blue photons are sensed by cryptochromes and phototropins. Raising the blue share generally reduces stem elongation and leaf expansion, with diminishing effect above ≈ 20–30 % and a strong interaction with total photon flux (Cope & Bugbee, 2013; Snowden et al., 2016). Cope and Bugbee showed that the absolute blue photon flux, not only the relative fraction, controls several responses — a caveat for any index that uses fractions.

What the eye sees: lux and colour

The eye is a very different detector. Its daytime sensitivity \(V(\lambda)\) peaks at 555 nm and is only about 4 % of the peak at 450 nm and 6 % at 660 nm. Illuminance (lux) weights the spectral irradiance by \(V(\lambda)\) and the constant 683 lm W⁻¹. A red-and-blue spectrum that is excellent for photosynthesis therefore looks dim, while green-rich white light looks bright. Colour is computed with the three CIE 1931 colour-matching functions \(\bar x, \bar y, \bar z\) (\(\bar y = V\)), giving tristimulus values X, Y, Z and chromaticity coordinates (x, y). Mixtures of light sources lie inside the polygon spanned by their chromaticities — the CIE chart shows why only specific red–green–blue or blue–phosphor mixtures can look white. The laboratory uses the analytic fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley (2013), which reproduces the tabulated CIE functions closely, and converts XYZ to the sRGB colour space of your screen (IEC 61966-2-1).

What the rendering can and cannot show

The tent preview uses a fixed daylight (D65) white balance, like a camera: warm light looks orange and blurple light looks magenta. Your eyes adapt partly to the illuminant, so in a real tent warm white looks whiter than on screen. Saturated LED colours also lie outside the sRGB gamut and are shown desaturated. The leaf, soil and film reflectances are stylised spectral shapes, not measurements.

Why workers care

Under red–blue light, nutrient deficiencies, pests and diseases are hard to see and many people report discomfort. Commercial fixtures therefore usually combine white LEDs with deep red: a little efficacy is traded for good colour rendering and a more natural plant appearance.

The equations behind the simulation

The model works on a 1-nm wavelength grid from 300 to 800 nm. Every integral below is evaluated as a sum over that grid (\(\Delta\lambda = 1\) nm).

Radiant output of an LED channel(S1)
\[ \Phi_{e,i}(\lambda) = \eta_{\text{wp},i}\,\eta_{\text{fix}}\,P_i\;s_i(\lambda), \qquad s_i(\lambda)=\frac{\exp\!\left[-4\ln 2\,(\lambda-\lambda_{p,i})^2/w_i^2\right]}{\int \exp\!\left[-4\ln 2\,(\lambda-\lambda_{p,i})^2/w_i^2\right]\mathrm{d}\lambda} \]

the spectral radiant power of channel \(i\) equals its electrical power times its package wall-plug efficiency times the fixture efficiency, distributed over wavelength by a normalised emission shape — a Gaussian of peak \(\lambda_p\) and full width at half maximum \(w\) for single-colour LEDs.

Where the numbers come from
  1. Wall-plug efficiencies for blue, green, red, far-red, cool and warm white are the package values in Table 1 of Kusuma et al. (2020) (100 mA mm⁻², 25 °C).
  2. For UV-A, the roadmap of Amano et al. (2020) gives an external quantum efficiency EQE ≈ 0.50 at 385 nm. The wall-plug efficiency is EQE × (photon energy ÷ electrical energy per electron): \(\eta_{\text{wp}} = \text{EQE}\cdot\frac{hc/\lambda}{qV_f} = 0.50\times\frac{3.22\ \text{eV}}{3.5\ \text{eV}} \approx 0.46\), with an assumed forward voltage \(V_f\approx 3.5\) V. We use 0.45.
  3. The Gaussian's area is \(w\sqrt{\pi/(4\ln 2)} = 1.0645\,w\), so the peak value of \(s_i\) is \(1/(1.0645\,w)\).
  4. Phosphor-converted whites are modelled as a split-Gaussian blue pump (450 nm) plus one or two broad split-Gaussian phosphor bands. Their parameters were tuned with the laboratory's own colorimetry (S9–S10) so that the cool white has CCT ≈ 6600 K with Duv ≈ 0 and ≈ 29 % blue photons, and the warm white CCT ≈ 2700 K and ≈ 9 % blue photons — consistent with the ≈ 30 % and ≈ 10 % blue quoted by Kusuma et al. (2020) for 6500 K and warm whites.
Assumptions and validity

Real LED spectra are slightly asymmetric and shift to longer wavelengths (and broaden) as the junction warms; efficiencies fall with drive current and temperature. \(\eta_{\text{fix}}\) lumps all these effects into one factor, applied equally to all channels.

From watts to photons(S2)
\[ q(\lambda) = \Phi_e(\lambda)\,\frac{\lambda}{h\,c\,N_A}\times10^{6} \approx 0.008359\,\lambda\;\Phi_e(\lambda), \qquad \eta_{\text{ph}} \approx \eta_{\text{wp}}\,\eta_{\text{fix}}\;0.008359\,\bar\lambda \]

the spectral photon flux in micromoles per second per nanometre equals the spectral radiant power times the wavelength divided by Planck's constant, the speed of light and Avogadro's number; for a narrow channel the photon efficacy is simply the radiant efficiency times 0.008359 times its (mean) wavelength in nanometres.

Follow the derivation
  1. One photon of wavelength \(\lambda\) carries \(E = hc/\lambda\). A mole carries \(N_A hc/\lambda\).
  2. Radiant power \(\Phi_e\) (J s⁻¹) therefore delivers \(\Phi_e\lambda/(N_A hc)\) mol s⁻¹; multiply by 10⁶ for µmol.
  3. With λ in nm: \(10^{6}\times10^{-9}/(6.626\times10^{-34}\times2.998\times10^{8}\times6.022\times10^{23}) = 0.008359\) µmol J⁻¹ nm⁻¹.
  4. Example: deep red, \(\eta_{\text{wp}}=0.81\), \(\eta_{\text{fix}}=0.80\): \(0.81\times0.80\times0.008359\times660 = 3.58\) µmol J⁻¹. Royal blue at 0.93: \(0.93\times0.80\times0.008359\times450 = 2.80\) µmol J⁻¹ — more efficient in watts, less in photons.

Assumptions and validity

McCree's RQE was measured on single leaves under monochromatic light at low flux; in canopies and under broad spectra the differences between wavelengths shrink. ePAR assumes far-red is always mixed with PAR photons (it is ineffective alone). Consequences

Far-red and UV-A photons count zero in PPF, so adding them always lowers the PPF efficacy, although far-red raises ePAR efficacy. This is why lamp labels should state the waveband (Both et al., 2017). The DesignLights Consortium's Horticultural Technical Requirements V4.0 (effective April 2025) require at least 2.5 µmol J⁻¹ (400–700 nm) for listed LED products.

Phytochrome photostationary state(S7)
\[ \text{PSS}=\frac{P_{fr}}{P_{tot}}=\frac{\sum_\lambda q(\lambda)\,\sigma_R(\lambda)}{\sum_\lambda q(\lambda)\,\sigma_R(\lambda)+\sum_\lambda q(\lambda)\,\sigma_{FR}(\lambda)} \]

the fraction of phytochrome in the active far-red-absorbing form equals the red-form conversion rate divided by the sum of the conversion rates of both forms, each rate being the photon spectrum weighted by that form's photochemical cross-section.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\sigma_R(\lambda),\ \sigma_{FR}(\lambda)\)photoconversion cross-sections (extinction coefficient × quantum yield) of Pr and Pfr; Lagarias et al. (1987)m² mol⁻¹
\(k_1, k_2\)first-order rate constants Pr→Pfr and Pfr→Prs⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. A pigment molecule with cross-section σ exposed to photon flux density \(q\) (mol m⁻² s⁻¹) is converted at rate \(k=\sigma q\) (m² mol⁻¹ × mol m⁻² s⁻¹ = s⁻¹). For a spectrum, sum over wavelengths: \(k_1=\sum q\,\sigma_R\), \(k_2=\sum q\,\sigma_{FR}\).
  2. The two-state balance: \(\mathrm{d}P_{fr}/\mathrm{d}t = k_1 P_r - k_2 P_{fr}\), with \(P_r + P_{fr} = P_{tot}\).
  3. At steady state \(k_1 (P_{tot}-P_{fr}) = k_2 P_{fr}\), so \(P_{fr}/P_{tot} = k_1/(k_1+k_2)\).
  4. The equilibrium is independent of intensity — only the spectral shape matters — and it is reached within seconds to minutes at horticultural fluxes (the time constant is \(1/(k_1+k_2)\)).
  5. Example with the cross-sections at the peaks: pure 660 nm light gives \(4209/(4209+553) = 0.88\); pure 730 nm gives \(36/(36+1209)=0.03\).
Assumptions and validity

The cross-sections were measured on purified oat phytochrome in solution. Inside a leaf, chlorophyll absorbs red strongly, so deeper tissue sees a lower R:FR and a lower PSS than the incident spectrum suggests (Kusuma & Bugbee, 2021). Different published cross-section sets (e.g. those tabulated by Sager et al., 1988) give PSS values that differ by a few hundredths.

How the parameters were obtained

\(a\) and \(d_j\) were fitted by least squares to the ASTM G173-03 global-tilt reference spectrum (Gueymard et al., 2002) between 350 and 800 nm, with \(m\) fixed at 1.5 (the standard's air mass). The fitted \(a\) is of the same order as the molecular (Rayleigh) scattering coefficient (Bodhaine et al., 1999), but it is an effective value: it also absorbs aerosol extinction and the diffuse sky light that global irradiance adds back. The model reproduces the reference well for plant-relevant metrics: R:FR 1.10 (G173: 1.10), PSS 0.697 (0.695), 56 (55.5) lx per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

the relative stem-extension index rises linearly as the photostationary state falls below that of a typical white LED (0.83) and falls as the blue share rises above 15 %; the leaf-size index follows the same directions more weakly.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(I_{\text{stem}}, I_{\text{leaf}}\)stem-extension and leaf-size index relative to a broad-white reference (= 1)–
\(f_B\)blue share of PPFD (capped at 0.40)–
What is — and is not — behind this equation

The form follows the literature: a linear decline of extension growth with increasing PSS (Morgan & Smith, 1976), suppression of elongation and leaf expansion by blue photons that saturates at higher fractions (Cope & Bugbee, 2013; Snowden et al., 2016), and increased leaf expansion with far-red (Park & Runkle, 2017). The coefficients (2.0, 1.5, 1.2, 0.6) were chosen by the course authors to produce visible, plausibly sized changes; they are not fitted to any data set. Real responses depend on species, cultivar, photon flux, photoperiod, temperature and timing (e.g. end-of-day far-red). Use the index to reason about directions, never to predict plant height.

Investigation tasks

Use the simulator. Record settings and results with Save investigation, and download spectra as CSV for your report.

  1. The price of colour

    Keep the total electrical power at 350 W. Compare the photon efficacy, PPFD, YPF, illuminance and CCT/colour of (a) red + blue (10 % blue photons), (b) broad white, (c) white + deep red. Put the results in a table. How much PPFD (in %) does the grower give up to work under white light? Which metric (PPF, YPF, ePAR) changes the ranking?

  2. Far-red: more photons or a morphological signal?

    Start from “White + deep red”. Add far-red in steps of 20 W up to 100 W while reducing deep red by the same power. Plot PPFD, ePAR photon flux, R:FR and PSS against far-red power. At what R:FR does PSS start to fall steeply? Explain with the shape of the magenta curve in the PSS chart and the cross-sections in the spectrum chart.

  3. Design a “sun-like” LED spectrum

    Using only LED channels, try to match sunlight's PSS (≈ 0.70), blue share (≈ 25 % of PPFD) and R:FR (≈ 1.1) as closely as possible. Report your recipe (W per channel), its photon efficacy and how close you got. Why is a sun-like spectrum not automatically the best one for a vertical farm?

  4. How good is the lux meter?

    For five presets, record lux and PPFD and compute lux per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. A grower owns only a lux meter calibrated to sunlight (55 lx per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Calculate the error (%) of the PPFD he would infer for each spectrum. Which spectra give the largest errors, and why (use Eq. S8)?

  5. The green gap in numbers

    Set 100 W on the green channel only, then 100 W on cool white. Compare photon efficacy, the CIE chromaticity and the leaf colour in the tent. Now raise the green wall-plug efficiency to 0.70 (a plausible future value). How much would a mixed RGB “white” then gain over a phosphor white? Discuss with Kusuma et al. (2020).

  6. Connect to your project

    Obtain the spectrum (datasheet or measured) of the lamp above your soilless system. Recreate it approximately with the channels, record the predicted PPFD, PSS and colour, and compare with a quantum-sensor measurement. List three reasons why the prediction and measurement might differ.

Model assumptions

  • Single-colour channels have Gaussian emission with fixed peak and FWHM (UV-A 385/12, blue 450/20, green 530/32, red 660/20, far-red 730/28 nm; typical datasheet widths). Temperature-dependent peak shifts and broadening are ignored.
  • Phosphor-converted whites are modelled as a blue pump plus broad phosphor bands, tuned so that the computed CCT, Duv and blue share match the stated class (cool ≈ 6600 K, Duv ≈ 0, 29 % blue; warm ≈ 2700 K, Duv ≈ −0.002, 9 % blue).
  • Package wall-plug efficiencies are the best-in-class values of Kusuma et al. (2020, Table 1) at 100 mA mm⁻² and 25 °C; UV-A is derived from the EQE reported by Amano et al. (2020). One fixture efficiency factor (default 0.80) represents driver, optical, thermal and current-droop losses for all channels.
  • PPFD is an area average: PPF × capture fraction / area. The spatial distribution is not modelled.
  • PSS uses the two-state model (no dark reversion, synthesis or destruction) and the cross-sections of purified oat phytochrome (Lagarias et al., 1987, interpolated from 5-nm samples of the 1-nm table in Kusuma & Bugbee, 2021). In-leaf spectral distortion is ignored.
  • Colour uses the Wyman et al. (2013) analytic CIE 1931 functions and the sRGB primaries with a D65 white; the preview has a fixed daylight white balance and stylised surface reflectances.
  • Sunlight is a Planck 5778 K spectrum with a fitted λ⁻⁴ attenuation and four absorption bands (Eq. S11); it represents clear-sky global light at air mass 1.5, not cloudy or canopy-shade light.
  • The morphology index (Eq. S12) is a qualitative teaching device with author-chosen coefficients.
