About the course

Sources & model assumptions

Every laboratory in this course is a model — a deliberate simplification of a greenhouse, a fermenter, a field or a market. This page explains how the models are built and how you should judge them, which units, constants, colours and coordinates they share, how equations and sources are presented, where the data and images come from, and where to find the list of assumptions behind each laboratory.

Why every model here is simplified — and how to judge one

A model is useful precisely because it leaves things out. The lighting designer ignores the reflection of light between leaves; the bioreactor assumes perfect mixing; the LCA comparator uses average emission factors. The statistician George Box argued that since all models are wrong, the scientist must be alert to what is importantly wrong (Box, 1976). The question is never “is the model true?” but “is it good enough for this purpose, in this range of conditions?”

That question can never be answered once and for all. Oreskes et al. (1994) showed that models of open natural systems cannot be verified — proven true — because such systems are never closed and model results are never unique. Agreement between a model and observations confirms it only partially and only for the conditions tested; the main value of a model is heuristic, as a tool for understanding and for asking better questions. The laboratories are built in that spirit: they teach mechanisms and orders of magnitude, not precise predictions for your particular greenhouse.

Before you trust a number from any simulation — in this course or in a company brochure — ask five questions:

  1. Purpose. What question was the model built to answer? A teaching model of a greenhouse is not a design tool for a real one.
  2. Boundary and state. What is inside the model, what is outside, and which variables does it track over time?
  3. Parameters. Where do the numbers come from — measurements, the literature, a fit, or an assumption? Each laboratory cites its sources.
  4. Evaluation. Has the model been compared with data, and in which range of conditions? Outside that range it is an extrapolation.
  5. Sensitivity. Would the conclusion change if an uncertain parameter were 20 % higher or lower? Move the sliders and find out.

Two kinds of model appear in the course. Mechanistic models are built from physical, chemical and biological principles — energy and mass balances, the Farquhar photosynthesis model, Penman–Monteith evapotranspiration, Monod kinetics — and can be extrapolated with some confidence. Empirical models — regressions, growth curves, machine learning — are fitted to data and are reliable only within the data’s range (Lesson 10.1). Many laboratories combine the two.

Every laboratory lists its assumptions

The last tab of each laboratory, Assumptions & sources, lists what the model simplifies and the references its equations and parameters come from. Section 5 below links to every one of them. “Where would this model fail?” is a question you should be able to answer for any laboratory you use in your report.

Angles, time and coordinates

  • Sun position. Solar elevation is measured from the horizon; solar azimuth clockwise from north (north 0°, east 90°, south 180°). Time in solar-geometry calculations is local solar time, with 12:00 at solar noon; the day of the year runs from 1 to 365. Declination, day length and extraterrestrial radiation follow the equations of FAO-56 (Allen et al., 1998).
  • Latitude and longitude are in decimal degrees, positive north and east. The course’s reference location is Västerås, Sweden, at 59.6° N.
  • 3D scenes use metres, with the y axis pointing up; in outdoor scenes north is −z and east is +x, so that shadows fall where they would in reality.
  • Angles in code are in radians; controls and readouts show degrees.
  • Charts put the independent variable (time, wavelength, a treatment) on the horizontal axis; logarithmic axes are labelled as such; error bars state whether they show a standard deviation, a standard error or a confidence interval (Lesson 3.2).

Colour maps

Colour carries data in heat maps, NDVI maps and 3D scenes. Badly chosen colour maps — above all the rainbow — distort data and exclude readers with colour-vision deficiencies (Crameri et al., 2020). The course therefore prefers perceptually uniform maps, whose lightness increases steadily and which remain readable in greyscale and with the common forms of colour blindness (Nuñez et al., 2018). Data, licences and image credits

  • Sources in the text. Every non-trivial number in a lesson is traceable to a reference in that lesson’s reference list, in APA style with a DOI link where one exists. Regulatory and market facts are dated “as of 2026” and are checked first when the course is updated; the frontiers radar is reviewed each term.
  • Data in the laboratories. Each laboratory’s Assumptions & sources tab names its data sources — for example FAOSTAT land-use and crop statistics and UN population prospects (via Our World in Data) in the Earth laboratory, SMHI climate normals and NASA POWER radiation data in the greenhouse and daylight laboratories, and published parameter values in the process models. Copernicus Sentinel data are available free of charge under the Copernicus data policy.
  • Images. Photographs and drawings are taken only from Wikimedia Commons or from public-domain collections of NASA, ESA and the USDA. Each image carries a credit line naming its author and licence where it is shown; the licence of each image is the one stated on its Commons page. If an image cannot be loaded, a link to the original is shown instead. Most figures are drawn specifically for the course as diagrams that follow the light or dark theme.
  • Software and fonts. 3D scenes use three.js and equations are typeset with KaTeX, both open-source libraries released under the MIT licence and loaded from the jsDelivr content-delivery network. The typefaces Inter (text) and JetBrains Mono (numbers and code) are loaded from Google Fonts and are released under the SIL Open Font License; mathematics uses the fonts that come with KaTeX. Everything else — the page code, the models and the diagrams — is written for the course without a build step.
  • Course material. Unless stated otherwise, the text, figures, models and code of the course are © the course author; third-party material keeps its own licence, stated where it is used.
  • Your data. Notes, progress, quiz results and project data are stored only in your own browser and are never sent to a server by the course. As with any website, the providers of the fonts and libraries (Google Fonts and jsDelivr) receive the technical information of an ordinary web request, such as your IP address.

Course aim and intended learning outcomes

The aim of the course, as stated on the course page:

Producing food for a growing population within the planetary boundaries is among the most pressing sustainability challenges of our time, and a new generation of technologies is changing what is possible. The aim of the course is to provide students with knowledge of these innovations and, through laboratory and project-based teaching in which they grow, measure and evaluate their own systems, to strengthen their ability to analyse, design and assess emerging food production systems in terms of sustainability, resilience and societal acceptance. The course also provides insight into the factors that enable or hinder the path of new food technologies to the market, knowledge that is central to the professional practice of an environmental engineer.
