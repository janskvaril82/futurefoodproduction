How to read the radar

A technology radar is a map for navigating uncertainty, borrowed from software engineering and technology foresight. Every numbered blip stands for a family of technologies rather than a single product or company. Its sector (the wedge) says what kind of change it brings; its ring (the distance from the centre) says how mature it is; a small triangle says whether the evidence of 2024–2026 pushed it forwards or backwards. The centre is not a target: it simply collects what is already in routine use.

Table 1. The four rings. Rings combine technical readiness with market and regulatory maturity; the TRL view of the radar separates the two.
RingMeaningTypical TRLExamples on the radar
EstablishedMature and widely deployed; innovation is incremental (efficiency, cost, control).9LED lighting, multispectral satellites, plant-based analogues, mycoprotein
ScalingProven in commercial operation but still growing, consolidating after failures, or waiting for costs and rules to fall into place.8–9Vertical farms, precision fermentation, insect protein, agrivoltaics, gene editing
EmergingDemonstrated in a relevant or operational environment; first commercial niches or large pilots.5–7Cultivated meat, gas fermentation, autonomous greenhouses, hyperspectral missions
HorizonResearch, prototypes and long-range bets whose feasibility or value is still open.1–5Biological nitrification inhibition, electro-agriculture, closed-loop life support

The five sectors follow the structure of the course. Controlled environments covers greenhouses, plant factories and aquaculture in tanks (Modules 4–8). Precision & digital covers sensing, robotics and artificial intelligence (Modules 9–11). Biotech & breeding covers changes to the crop itself and its microbial partners. Alternative proteins covers new routes to protein (Module 12). Circular & systems collects technologies that redesign how land, energy, waste and information flow through the food system (Modules 1 and 15).

Readiness is expressed with the nine technology readiness levels (TRLs) that NASA developed and the European Commission adopted for its research programmes (Mankins, 1995; European Commission, 2014), exactly as in Lesson 1.4. Each card gives a range and a best estimate, and justifies it in one or two sentences.

Table 2. EU definitions of technology readiness levels (Horizon 2020 General Annexes, continued in Horizon Europe).

Key idea — readiness is not desirability

A TRL tells you whether something works, not whether it is affordable, accepted, legal or good for the planet. Vertical farms reached TRL 9 years ago and are still in the Scaling ring, because the business model is consolidating after a wave of closures; cultivated meat is authorised in several countries yet remains at TRL 6–7 in volume terms. Switch the radar to Readiness (TRL) to see where technical and market maturity diverge.

The small triangle on a blip shows momentum over 2024–2026, judged from the dated milestones on its card: pointing inwards means advancing (approvals, record performance, scale-up), pointing outwards means setbacks (closures, bans, negative safety opinions), and no triangle means the signals are mixed or quiet. Momentum is an editorial judgement; the milestones and their sources let you check it.

Common misconception

“A blip on the Horizon ring is science fiction, and one in the centre is the answer.” Horizon technologies such as nitrification-inhibiting wheat may deliver large benefits within a decade; established ones such as LED lighting still need better design and cleaner electricity to deliver theirs. Position describes maturity, not merit.

Promise and risk against the planetary boundaries

The course asks you to relate every technology to the planetary boundaries (LO1). In the 2025 Planetary Health Check, seven of the nine boundaries were assessed as transgressed, and food production is the largest single driver of several of them (Richardson et al., 2023; Sakschewski et al., 2025; Lesson 1.2). Each card therefore shows the direction in which the technology would move the pressure on the six boundaries most affected by food production — if it were deployed at scale in place of today’s dominant way of producing the same food or service:

  • ↓ reduces pressure — the literature cited on the card supports a benefit;
  • ↑ adds pressure — a likely net burden;
  • ↕ depends — the sign depends on design, scale or, most often, the electricity mix;
  • – little direct effect at the scale of the food system.

The six columns are climate change; biosphere integrity; land-system change; freshwater change; the biogeochemical flows of nitrogen and phosphorus; and novel entities — synthetic chemicals, plastics and other new substances or modified life forms (Persson et al., 2022). These are qualitative judgements that summarise the cited studies; they are not the results of a life-cycle assessment. Use the LCA comparator, the diet footprint composer and the planetary boundaries dashboard to quantify them.

Burden shifting and rebound

Notice how many ↕ symbols sit in the climate column. Technologies that replace land with electricity — plant factories, gas fermentation, precision fermentation, cultivated meat — shift the burden from the land boundary to the energy system. Whether that is a good trade depends on how clean and how additional the electricity is, and on rebound effects: when a technology makes a service cheaper, total use can rise (Sorrell, 2009; Lesson 1.4).

How the radar is maintained

The syllabus states that the selection of technologies is continuously renewed. The radar is where that renewal happens first: lessons change when a development is important enough to teach; the radar changes whenever the evidence changes. Every edition follows the same five steps (Figure 1).

1. Evidence rules

Not all sources are equal. The radar uses the hierarchy in Table 3 and labels claims that rest on the lower levels. This is the same critical reading you practise in Lesson 3.4 and in the study guide.

Table 3. Evidence hierarchy used for milestones.
LevelSourceUsed forExamples
1Legal texts and official registersAuthorisations, bans, deadlinesEUR-Lex, EFSA Journal, FDA inventories, SFA and FSANZ lists, national gazettes
2Peer-reviewed studies and systematic reviewsPerformance, footprints, TRL justificationsField trials, LCAs, techno-economic analyses
3Official statistics and intergovernmental reportsProduction volumes, waste, trendsFAO, UNEP, Eurostat
4Company releases, datasheets and trade pressCompany events only (openings, closures, product specifications)Press releases, business news — marked “company-reported” when a number cannot be checked

2. Placement rules

  • TRL is assigned from the EU definitions (Table 2) for the family as a whole; where members differ, the card gives the range and says which member sets the best estimate.
  • Ring starts from the TRL and is adjusted for market and regulatory maturity. Every adjustment is explained in the card’s justification — for example why vertical farming (TRL 9) sits in Scaling.
  • Momentum is judged only from dated milestones of the last three years. Older events stay on the cards as grey context.
  • Boundary effects summarise the cited literature relative to the incumbent the technology would replace; when the sign depends on the electricity mix, the card says so.

3. Adding, moving and retiring

A technology is added when it reaches at least TRL 2 with a credible route to a food-system effect, or when a regulator, a major funder or the peer-reviewed literature gives it sustained attention. It moves when a milestone changes its readiness or maturity. It is retired when it becomes ordinary infrastructure (one day, LED lighting may simply be “lighting”), or when three years pass without a meaningful milestone — the blip then moves outwards first and is removed at the following review, with a note in the log. Removed blips are not forgotten: the review log keeps the reason.

4. Limits of this radar

The radar is written from a European and Nordic perspective for a first-cycle engineering course. It over-represents technologies with an English-language paper trail, company news is easier to find than quiet adoption by farmers, and a single editor’s judgement shapes ring and momentum. Treat it as an argued starting point: the milestones and sources are there so that you can disagree with evidence.

Use the radar in your studies

The radar is built for learning outcomes LO1 (compare technologies against the planetary boundaries), LO6 (the societal and regulatory factors behind adoption) and LO7 (sustainability and resilience). These tasks work as seminar preparation or as a short written assignment.

  1. Challenge a placement

    Choose a blip whose ring you disagree with. Using the TRL definitions in Table 2 and at least two sources, write a 150-word argument for moving it — and state which future evidence would change your mind.

  2. Trace a milestone to its primary record

    Open the source of one 2024–2026 milestone and follow it to the primary record: the regulation, the register entry or the peer-reviewed paper. What does the primary record say that the summary leaves out?

  3. Compare three proteins

    Mycoprotein, gas fermentation and insects compete for similar markets. Use their cards, the protein quality calculator and the bioreactor laboratory to compare land, energy and amino-acid quality per 100 g of protein. Which boundary decides your ranking?

  4. Run a hype check

    Find a company press release from the last twelve months about one of these technologies. Classify each claim as measured, modelled or aspirational, and look for independent evidence for the most important one.

  5. Write a new entry

    Propose a technology that is not on the radar, using the card format: what it is, how it works, a TRL with justification, dated milestones with sources, promise and risks against the boundaries, and links to the course. Save it in your notebook.
