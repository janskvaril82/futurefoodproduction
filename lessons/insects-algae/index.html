Insects, microalgae and seaweed

About 1,900 insect species are eaten around the world; spirulina was harvested from alkaline lakes long before it became a health-food tablet; and seaweed has fed coastal peoples for millennia. What is new is the attempt to produce them industrially — mealworms in climate-controlled towers, algae in glass tubes, kelp on ropes in the Skagerrak. Each turns out to be governed by a different piece of physics: an insect farm is a heat-removal problem, an algae culture is a light-attenuation problem, and a kelp farm is a nutrient — and iodine — problem. This lesson quantifies all three, and asks honestly where they fit in a future food system.

Old foods, new industries

Insects form part of the traditional diets of at least two billion people, mostly in the tropics, and about 1,900 species are eaten worldwide (van Huis, 2013; van Huis et al., 2013). In Europe they are "novel": until recently almost nobody ate them, and they were not on the market before May 1997. Microalgae and seaweed have a similar double identity — traditional in some cultures, novel in industrial form. Table 1 sets out what each of the three offers and what limits it.

Big idea

Insects convert feed more efficiently than livestock because they are cold-blooded: they do not burn feed to keep warm — but that means the farm must keep them warm, and remove the heat they do produce. Microalgae are limited not by nutrients but by light, which a dense culture absorbs within centimetres. Seaweed needs neither feed nor fertiliser, but concentrates iodine to levels that can make it unsafe.

Insect biology and nutrition

The insects farmed in Europe belong to two groups. The yellow mealworm (Tenebrio molitor) and the lesser mealworm (Alphitobius diaperinus) are beetles, and the black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) — the main feed insect — is a fly. All three undergo complete metamorphosis (holometabolous development: egg, larva, pupa, adult) and are harvested as larvae (Figure 1). The house cricket (Acheta domesticus) and the migratory locust (Locusta migratoria) develop by incomplete metamorphosis: the nymphs look like small adults. As of 2026, all of these except the black soldier fly are authorised as food in the EU (see the timeline below). A T. molitor female produces about 160 eggs in her three-month life, and the species develops to adulthood in about ten weeks (Oonincx & de Boer, 2012); the black soldier fly, reared at 27 °C, needs 40–43 days from egg to adult, of which 22–24 days as a larva (Tomberlin et al., 2002).

Insects are rich in protein and fat (Table 2), and their composition depends on species, stage, diet and temperature: in one study, T. molitor larvae reared at 31 °C contained 38 % protein and 47 % lipid on a dry basis (Bjørge et al., 2018). Black soldier fly prepupae reared on four different organic substrates varied little in protein (40–43 % DM) but greatly in fat (22–39 %) (Spranghers et al., 2017). A compilation of 236 nutrient compositions concluded that many edible insects provide sufficient energy and protein, meet human amino-acid requirements, are high in mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids, and are rich in micronutrients such as copper, iron, magnesium, manganese, phosphorus, selenium and zinc (Rumpold & Schlüter, 2013).

There is a catch in the protein numbers. Protein is normally measured as total nitrogen multiplied by 6.25, a factor that assumes protein is 16 % nitrogen and that all nitrogen is in protein. Insects contain non-protein nitrogen, notably in the chitin of their exoskeleton.

Nitrogen-to-protein conversion(12.5.1)
\[ P = k_p\,N,\qquad k_p = \frac{\sum_j m_j}{\sum_j m_j\,w_{N,j}},\qquad \frac{P_{6.25}}{P_{\text{true}}} = \frac{6.25}{k_p} \]

the protein content equals the nitrogen content times a conversion factor; the true factor is the mass of amino acids recovered divided by the nitrogen they contain. Using the default 6.25 instead of the true factor overestimates protein by the ratio of the two.

Follow the derivation
  1. If protein contains a fraction \(f_N\) of nitrogen and all N is protein N, then \(P = N/f_N\). With \(f_N = 0.16\), \(k_p = 1/0.16 = 6.25\).
  2. Amino-acid analysis gives the mass of each amino acid and, from its formula, its nitrogen content; the ratio of total amino-acid mass to amino-acid nitrogen is the true \(k_p\).
  3. Janssen et al. (2017) did this for larvae of T. molitor, A. diaperinus and H. illucens and found \(k_p = 4.76 \pm 0.09\) for whole larvae and 5.60 ± 0.39 for extracted, purified protein, because chitin and other non-protein N inflate the nitrogen.
  4. Overestimation with 6.25: \(6.25/4.76 = 1.31\), i.e. about 31 %.
Assumptions and validity

The factor depends on species, life stage and processing; the same principle explains why the default 6.25 overestimates the protein of many plant foods, for which 5.4–5.7 is often more accurate (Lesson 12.2).

Allergy

EFSA's opinions on edible insects consistently conclude that they may trigger allergic reactions in people allergic to crustaceans and dust mites, because of shared proteins such as tropomyosin, and that the substrate can introduce further allergens (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2015; EFSA NDA Panel, 2021). Insect foods in the EU must therefore carry specific labelling.

Rearing insects: temperature, density and metabolic heat

Insects are ectotherms: their body temperature follows their surroundings, and so do their growth and metabolic rates. Maximum daily growth rates of 16.6 % (T. molitor) and 18.3 % (A. diaperinus) were measured at 31 °C, and the efficiency with which assimilated energy was used for growth was highest between about 23 and 31 °C (Bjørge et al., 2018). Industrial farms therefore hold rearing rooms at roughly 25–30 °C and high humidity, in stacked crates on racks (Figure 2). In Oonincx and de Boer's (2012) life-cycle assessment of a Dutch mealworm farm, gas for heating caused 26 % of the carbon footprint and 35 % of the energy use — yet the same study noted that larger larvae produce a surplus of metabolic heat. Heat is the central design problem of an insect farm.

How much heat? Oonincx et al. (2010) measured the CO₂ production of five insect species in respiration chambers. Because nearly all aerobic metabolism releases about 460 kJ per mole of O₂ consumed (Eq. 12.3.9) and the respiratory quotient RQ = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed is about 0.7–1.0, CO₂ production can be converted into heat.

Metabolic heat of an insect population(12.5.2)
\[ \dot Q = \frac{m\,r_{\mathrm{CO_2}}}{M_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\;\mathrm{RQ}}\;\Delta h_{\mathrm{O_2}} \]

the heat released equals the live mass of insects times their specific CO₂ production, converted to moles of CO₂, divided by the respiratory quotient to obtain moles of oxygen, times the heat released per mole of oxygen.

Follow the derivation

  2. Oxygen consumed: divide by RQ (for carbohydrate oxidation RQ = 1; for fat about 0.7).
  3. Heat: multiply by \(\Delta h_{\mathrm{O_2}}\) — the principle of indirect calorimetry used for humans and animals. Divide by 86,400 s d⁻¹ to obtain watts.
  4. Numbers for 1 kg of T. molitor at 25 °C: \(61/44.0 = 1.39\) mol CO₂ d⁻¹; / 0.9 = 1.54 mol O₂ d⁻¹; × 460 kJ = 708 kJ d⁻¹ = 8.2 W kg⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

The CO₂ values were measured on feeding insects at one temperature per species; metabolic rates increase with temperature and activity. Part of the heat leaves as latent heat of evaporated water. The RQ is uncertain; 0.9 is a middle value.

The ventilation must carry away both the heat and the CO₂. Two balances give two required air flows; the larger one governs.

Ventilation for heat and for CO₂(12.5.3)
\[ \dot V_{\mathrm{heat}} = \frac{\dot Q}{\rho_a\,c_{p}\,\Delta T},\qquad \dot V_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = \frac{\dot m_{\mathrm{CO_2}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\,\Delta x_{\mathrm{CO_2}}},\qquad \dot V = \max\!\left(\dot V_{\mathrm{heat}},\dot V_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\right) \]

the air flow needed to remove the heat is the heat divided by the air's density, its specific heat and the permitted warming of the air; the air flow needed to keep CO₂ below a limit is the CO₂ release divided by the density of CO₂ times the permitted rise in its volume fraction. The room needs whichever is larger.

Feed conversion and the environmental case

The classic argument for insects is feed efficiency. In a widely cited comparison, crickets needed about 1.7 kg of feed per kg of live weight, against 2.5 for broiler chickens, 5 for pigs and 10 for cattle; and because about 80 % of a cricket is edible, compared with 55 % of a chicken or pig and 40 % of a steer, the difference per kilogram of edible product is even larger (van Huis, 2013; van Huis et al., 2013). Controlled experiments are more nuanced: on diets made from food by-products, black soldier fly larvae and Argentinean cockroaches converted feed more efficiently than conventional livestock, whereas yellow mealworms and house crickets were about as efficient as pigs on three of four diets — and as efficient as poultry on the best diet when corrected for edible portion (Oonincx et al., 2015).

Feed per kilogram of edible product and protein-conversion efficiency(12.5.4)
\[ F_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{\mathrm{FCR}}{y_{\mathrm{e}}},\qquad \mathrm{PCE} = \frac{y_{\mathrm{e}}\,x_{\mathrm{e}}}{\mathrm{FCR}\;x_{\mathrm{f}}},\qquad x_{\mathrm{e}} = \frac{k_p}{6.25}\,x_{\mathrm{e},6.25} \]

the feed needed per kilogram of edible product is the feed-conversion ratio divided by the edible fraction; the protein-conversion efficiency is the edible fraction times the protein content of the edible product, divided by the feed-conversion ratio times the protein content of the feed — with the insect protein corrected from the default factor to the true factor.

Life-cycle results. The first LCA of an insect farm (Oonincx & de Boer, 2012) found for fresh mealworms a global warming potential of 2.7 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹, an energy use of 34 MJ kg⁻¹ and a land use of 3.6 m² kg⁻¹; per kg of edible protein, 14 kg CO₂e, 173 MJ and 18 m². Mealworms needed only 43 % of the land of milk and 10 % of the land of beef per kg of edible protein, but their energy use was higher than that of milk or chicken, similar to pork and lower than beef; natural gas for heating alone accounted for 35 % of it. A later review listed the main environmental advantages of insect farming over livestock as lower land and water use, lower greenhouse gas emissions, high feed-conversion efficiency and the ability to turn low-value organic by-products into food or feed (van Huis & Oonincx, 2017); insect LCAs have been reviewed by Halloran et al. (2016).

Black soldier fly and waste. The black soldier fly is the workhorse of insect feed: its larvae grow on a wide range of moist organic materials, reducing them to a residue while turning part of them into protein and fat. Research at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences found that the waste-to-biomass conversion ratio and development time depend strongly on the volatile-solids and protein content of the substrate; abattoir waste, food waste and even human faeces supported good larval development (Lalander et al., 2019; Gold et al., 2018). European law is much stricter. Farmed insects count as farmed animals, so their substrates must be permitted feed materials: manure, catering waste and former foodstuffs containing meat or fish may not be used. Processed insect protein has been permitted in aquaculture feed since 2017 and in pig and poultry feed since 2021. The residue — frass, a mixture of excrement, exuviae and uneaten feed — may be marketed as a fertiliser or soil improver after a heat treatment (70 °C for 60 minutes), under rules adopted in 2021.

2025

Why has scaling up been so hard? The biology is fast, but the business is capital-intensive and competes on price with soybean meal and fishmeal, which are cheap commodities. Automated vertical farms need large investments; energy for heating and cooling, labour, substrate logistics, disease control in dense colonies and the drying of larvae all cost money; and demand for insects as human food has remained small in Europe, where consumer acceptance is limited by disgust and unfamiliarity (Lessons 13.1 and 13.2). Ÿnsect's giant factory near Amiens became a symbol of the gap between investor expectations and the economics of feed protein. Insects may yet find durable niches — pet food, aquaculture feed, waste treatment, specialty foods — rather than replacing meat.

Common misconception

"The EU is secretly putting insects into bread." Every authorised insect ingredient must be named in the list of ingredients, and the specific conditions of each authorisation include allergy labelling (Lesson 14.1). Authorisation allows a product on the market; it does not oblige anyone to use it.

Microalgae: growth limited by light

Spirulina (Arthrospira platensis and A. maxima) is a filamentous cyanobacterium that thrives in warm, alkaline water; chlorella (Chlorella spp.) is a single-celled green alga with a tough cell wall. Both are rich in protein — 46–63 % of dry matter for A. platensis, 60–71 % for A. maxima and 42–55 % for C. vulgaris in the studies compiled by Ritala et al. (2017) — and both have been sold as food supplements for decades, so they are not novel foods in the EU. The engineering question is how much biomass a square metre of culture can produce, and the answer is set by light.

A microalgal culture is dense enough to absorb nearly all the light that falls on it within a few centimetres. Light intensity therefore falls exponentially with depth, exactly as in a leaf canopy (Lesson 2.4) or an NDIR gas sensor (Lesson 9.1).

Beer–Lambert attenuation in an algal culture(12.5.5)
\[ I(z) = I_0\,e^{-k_a X z},\qquad z_c = \frac{\ln\left(I_0/I_c\right)}{k_a X} \]

the photon flux at depth z equals the incident flux times the exponential of minus the biomass-specific absorption coefficient times the biomass concentration times the depth. The compensation depth — below which the cells respire more than they photosynthesise — is where the light has fallen to the compensation irradiance.

\(I_0,\ I(z)\)PPFD at the surface and at depth \(z\)µmol m⁻² s⁻¹0–2,000

Follow the derivation
  1. In a thin layer \(\mathrm{d}z\) the cells absorb a fraction of the light proportional to the number of absorbing cells in the layer: \(\mathrm{d}I = -k_aXI\,\mathrm{d}z\) (scattering neglected).
  2. Separate and integrate from the surface: \(\ln(I/I_0) = -k_aXz\), i.e. \(I = I_0e^{-k_aXz}\).
  3. Set \(I(z_c) = I_c\) and solve: \(z_c = \ln(I_0/I_c)/(k_aX)\). Doubling the biomass halves the photic depth.
Assumptions and validity

Monochromatic-equivalent light and no scattering; in reality blue and red light are absorbed more strongly than green, and cells scatter light forwards. \(k_a\) is not constant: cells grown in dim light make more pigment (photoacclimation). The value 0.15 m² g⁻¹ used in this lesson is an illustrative value for a green alga.

(m² g⁻¹ × g m⁻³ × m) = dimensionless exponent ✓ · \(z_c\): 1/(m² g⁻¹ × g m⁻³) = m ✓

Cells in a well-mixed culture circulate between the bright surface and the dark interior, so what matters for their average growth is the light averaged over the depth.

Depth-averaged light in a mixed culture(12.5.6)
\[ \bar I = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^{L} I_0\,e^{-k_aXz}\,\mathrm{d}z = \frac{I_0}{k_aXL}\left(1 - e^{-k_aXL}\right) \]

the average photon flux over a culture of depth L equals the incident flux divided by the optical depth kaXL, times one minus the fraction of light transmitted through the whole culture.

Follow the derivation
  1. Average of \(I(z)\) over the depth: \(\bar I = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^LI_0e^{-kz}\mathrm{d}z\) with \(k = k_aX\).
  2. The integral is \(\frac{I_0}{k}(1 - e^{-kL})\); divide by \(L\).
  3. For an optically thick culture (\(kL \gg 1\)), \(\bar I \approx I_0/(k_aXL)\): the average light is inversely proportional to the amount of biomass per unit area, \(XL\). The absorbed photons per square metre are then fixed — about all of \(I_0\) — whatever the reactor.
Assumptions and validity

Using \(\bar I\) in a growth law assumes that cells respond to the average light, which holds for fast light–dark cycles; for slow mixing, cells respond to the local light (the "integration" question studied in photobioreactor research).

Worked example 3Raceway versus flat panel

Both systems receive \(I_0 = 1500\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹; \(k_a = 0.15\) m² g⁻¹ and \(I_c = 10\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. A raceway pond is 25 cm deep with 0.5 g L⁻¹ of biomass; a flat-panel photobioreactor has a 2 cm light path and 4 g L⁻¹. For each, calculate the compensation depth, the fraction of the culture volume that is photosynthetically active, and the depth-averaged PPFD. What do they have in common?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Raceway. \(k_aX = 0.15\times500 = 75\) m⁻¹. \(z_c = \ln(1500/10)/75 = 5.01/75 = 0.067\) m = 6.7 cm: only 27 % of the 25 cm depth is photic. \(\bar I = 1500(1 - e^{-18.8})/18.8 = 80\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Raceway ponds and photobioreactors

Two families of systems dominate outdoor microalgae production (Figure 6). Raceway ponds are shallow, oval channels, typically 0.2–0.3 m deep, in which a paddle wheel circulates the culture; they are cheap to build and operate but lose water by evaporation and CO₂ to the air, are open to contamination and control temperature poorly. Photobioreactors (PBRs) — arrays of transparent tubes, usually 0.1 m or less in diameter, or flat panels — enclose the culture, allow much higher biomass concentrations and better control, but cost far more per square metre and must be cooled and degassed: photosynthesis produces oxygen, which inhibits growth if it accumulates in long tubes (Chisti, 2007).

Because a productive culture absorbs essentially all incident photons, the upper bound on areal productivity is set by the photon supply — the daily light integral of Lesson 4.2 — times the biomass yield on light.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Photosynthesis needs at least eight photons per O₂ evolved (Lesson 2.1); with the energy needed to build biomass this gives a theoretical yield of about 1.5 g of biomass per mole of photons on nitrate (Zijffers et al., 2010).
  2. Zijffers et al. Assumptions and validity

Assumes that light is the only limitation. Outdoor yields are typically well below laboratory maxima because the midday sun saturates photosynthesis; realistic annual productivities have been estimated at 40–80 t of dry matter per hectare per year depending on technology and location (as cited by Zijffers et al., 2010).

Harvesting is the next bottleneck. A raceway culture at 0.5 g L⁻¹ is 99.95 % water; separating two kilograms of algae means processing four cubic metres of broth. Filamentous spirulina can be collected on screens, but single-celled chlorella must be flocculated, filtered or centrifuged, and the energy cost scales with the volume processed.

Harvesting energy per kilogram of biomass(12.5.8)
\[ e_{\mathrm{h}} = \frac{e_V}{X} \]

the energy needed to harvest one kilogram of biomass equals the energy needed to process one cubic metre of culture divided by the biomass concentration in kilograms per cubic metre.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(e_V\)specific energy of the separation (per volume processed)kWh m⁻³order of 1 for continuous centrifuges; much lower for screens and flotation

Follow the reasoning
  1. A centrifuge or pump spends energy per cubic metre of liquid it moves, largely independent of how much biomass that liquid contains.
  2. One kilogram of biomass is contained in \(1/X\) m³ of culture, so the energy per kilogram is \(e_V/X\).
  3. Consequence: a tenfold more dilute culture costs ten times more to harvest — the hidden advantage of dense photobioreactor cultures and of pre-concentration by flocculation.
Assumptions and validity

Single-step separation; real processes pre-concentrate (flocculation, flotation, filtration) and then dewater. Reported energy requirements vary widely between methods and machines (Molina Grima et al., 2003; Milledge & Heaven, 2013). Drying the resulting paste needs even more energy.

Microalgae as food: protein, vitamin B12 and omega-3

Microalgal proteins are of reasonable quality, but the cells come with caveats. Chlorella's rigid cell wall limits digestibility unless the cells are broken; spirulina's cells are easier to digest. Pigments give strong colours and "green" flavours that limit how much can be added to foods, which is why most microalgae are still sold as supplements rather than as protein staples (Becker, 2007; Caporgno & Mathys, 2018).

Vitamin B12. Spirulina is often promoted as a vegan source of vitamin B12. Watanabe et al. (1999) showed why this is misleading: a microbiological assay gave B12 values six to nine times higher than an assay based on intrinsic factor, the protein humans need to absorb B12; of the intrinsic-factor-active compounds, 83 % was pseudovitamin B12, which is inactive in humans, and only 17 % was true B12. Spirulina is therefore not a reliable B12 source — a serious point for people who replace animal foods (Lesson 12.1).

Omega-3 fatty acids. The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA that make fish nutritionally valuable are synthesised by microalgae at the base of the marine food web. Algal oils rich in DHA are produced commercially by growing heterotrophic microalgae on sugar in fermenters — the same technology as Lesson 12.3 — and offer a fish-free source for vegetarians and for fish feed (Caporgno & Mathys, 2018).

Algae without light?

Some microalgae, including certain Chlorella strains, can grow heterotrophically on sugar in the dark, at much higher cell densities and in the same steel fermenters used for yeast. That escapes the light limit of Eq. 12.5.7 — but it also gives up the main attraction of algae, photosynthesis, and returns the land footprint to the sugar crop.

Seaweed: kelp farming in Nordic waters

Seaweed aquaculture has been called the fastest-growing component of global food production (Duarte et al., 2017). Most of it takes place in East and Southeast Asia, but interest in Europe has grown since the 2010s. On the Swedish west coast, research at the University of Gothenburg's Tjärnö laboratory in the Koster archipelago and at KTH has made sugar kelp (Saccharina latissima) a case study. Kelp has a two-stage life cycle (Figure 8): the large blade is the diploid sporophyte, which releases spores; these grow into microscopic male and female gametophytes whose fertilisation produces a new sporophyte. Farmers exploit this in a hatchery, where young sporophytes are grown on twine that is wound onto longlines at sea in autumn. The kelp grows through winter and spring and is harvested before the summer, when biofouling organisms colonise the blades.

Seaweed farming is extractive aquaculture: it needs no feed or fertiliser and removes nitrogen and phosphorus from the water. A before–after study of a Swedish kelp farm found limited environmental effects — no changes in dissolved nutrients or benthic oxygen flux, a positive effect on seafloor fauna, and about 40 % light attenuation at 5 m depth at peak biomass (Visch et al., 2020b). A qualitative assessment of ecosystem services on the Swedish west coast found mainly positive or neutral effects on supporting, regulating and provisioning services, while some cultural services are likely to be negatively affected (Hasselström et al., 2018).

Iodine. Kelps accumulate iodine from sea water to extraordinary concentrations. Raw S. latissima contained on average 4,605 mg of iodine per kg of dry weight; blanching in water at 80 °C for 120 s lowered this to 293 mg kg⁻¹ (Nielsen et al., 2020). For comparison, the tolerable upper intake level (UL) for adults in the EU is 600 µg per day (EFSA, 2006) and the adequate intake for adults 150 µg per day (EFSA NDA Panel, 2014). Both too little and too much iodine harm the thyroid.

A critical view of "blue carbon" claims

Seaweed farming has been promoted as a climate fix that could be traded in carbon markets. Krause-Jensen and Duarte (2016) estimated that wild macroalgae export a substantial amount of carbon to sediments and the deep sea, and Duarte et al. (2017) discussed farming for climate mitigation and adaptation. But verifying that CO₂ fixed by seaweed leads to long-term storage is extremely difficult: biomass turns over rapidly, dissolved and particulate carbon pathways are poorly known, and air–sea CO₂ exchange must be accounted for (Hurd et al., 2022). Troell et al. (2023) conclude that presenting seaweed farming as a "quick fix" for the climate risks misdirected investment; its real contributions are food, feed, nutrient removal and the substitution of higher-emission products.

Frontier 2024–2026

Nordic research explores seaweed as a source of protein concentrates, alginate and bioactive compounds in "biorefineries"; closing nitrogen and phosphorus loops between land and sea with low-trophic mariculture such as kelp and mussels (Thomas et al., 2022b); and processing — blanching, fermentation — that makes kelp safe to eat in larger portions.

Key terms

Edible insect (novel food)
Insect species or form authorised under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, e.g. dried Tenebrio molitor larva (2021).
Holometabolous development
Complete metamorphosis with egg, larva, pupa and adult stages, as in beetles and flies.
Nitrogen-to-protein factor
Factor kp that converts total nitrogen to protein; 6.25 by default, about 4.76 for insect larvae because of non-protein nitrogen in chitin.
Chitin
Nitrogen-containing polysaccharide of the insect exoskeleton; indigestible fibre that inflates nitrogen-based protein estimates.
Respiratory quotient (RQ)
Ratio of CO₂ produced to O₂ consumed in respiration; used to convert CO₂ production into oxygen uptake and heat.
Frass
Residue of insect rearing — excrement, exuviae and uneaten substrate — marketable in the EU as a fertiliser after heat treatment.
Processed animal protein (PAP)
Protein meal from animals (including permitted insects) used in feed under EU animal by-product rules.
Raceway pond
Shallow, open, paddle-wheel-driven channel for outdoor microalgae cultivation.
Photobioreactor
Closed, transparent vessel (tubes or panels) with a short light path for cultivating microalgae.
Biomass-specific absorption coefficient
ka (m² g⁻¹): light absorbed per unit biomass in the Beer–Lambert law I = I₀e−kaXz.
Compensation irradiance
Light level at which photosynthesis just balances respiration; it defines the edge of the photic zone.
Pseudovitamin B12
A cobamide found in spirulina that resembles vitamin B12 but is inactive in humans.
Extractive aquaculture
Farming of organisms such as seaweed and mussels that take nutrients from the water instead of being fed.
Carbon sequestration
Secure storage of carbon removed from the atmosphere for more than 100 years; distinct from temporary uptake in biomass.
