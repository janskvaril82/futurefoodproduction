Precision irrigation and nutrient management

In the summer of 2018 the rain simply stopped over much of Sweden. Farmers with irrigation — mostly potato and vegetable growers in the south — watered day and night; everyone else watched their cereals ripen early and light. Water and nitrogen are the two inputs that most often limit yield, and the two whose excess does most harm: water pumped onto a field that cannot hold it drains away, and nitrogen that the crop does not take up ends its journey in the Baltic Sea. This lesson turns the internationally standard FAO-56 method into a working daily water balance, shows how sensors and models decide when and how much to irrigate, and then applies the same accounting logic to nitrogen — so that you can put numbers on the promise of "the right amount, at the right time".

  • Calculate total and readily available water, run the daily root-zone depletion balance with the water-stress coefficient, and schedule irrigation from it (LO3, LO5).
  • Compare calendar, sensor-based and model-based scheduling, soil-moisture sensors, irrigation methods and variable-rate irrigation, and quantify yield loss under deficit irrigation with the yield-response factor Ky (LO3, LO5, LO7).
  • Calculate a field nitrogen balance, nitrogen-use efficiency indicators and the economic optimum nitrogen rate, and explain split application and crop nitrogen sensing (LO3, LO5).
  • Relate nitrogen surplus to nitrate leaching, Baltic Sea eutrophication and the Swedish rules for nitrate-vulnerable zones (LO1, LO6, LO7).

Water and nitrogen: the inputs that matter most

Globally, agriculture accounts for about 69 % of all freshwater withdrawals; in Western Europe the share is only about 5 %, because most European crops are rain-fed (FAO AQUASTAT). Sweden fits the European pattern: irrigation is used mainly for high-value crops such as potatoes, vegetables and some sugar beet, most of it in the southern and eastern parts of the country. Yet dry summers such as 2018 show that even at 60° N the difference between rainfall and crop water use can be large enough to halve yields. Precision irrigation is about applying water only where and when the crop needs it — and not so much that it drains below the roots, taking nutrients with it.

Nitrogen is the other half of the story. Synthetic fertiliser made by the Haber–Bosch process, together with manure and biological fixation, feeds the world's crops (Zhang et al., 2015), but crops use it inefficiently: worldwide, only about a third of the nitrogen applied to cereals ends up in the harvested grain (Raun & Johnson, 1999), and more than half of all nitrogen added to cropland is lost to the environment (Lassaletta et al., 2014). In the Baltic Sea, nutrient inputs from land are the main driver of a roughly ten-fold expansion of oxygen-depleted bottom waters over the last century (Carstensen et al., 2014). Water and nitrogen are coupled: nitrate dissolved in the soil water moves wherever the water goes, so every millimetre of excess drainage carries nitrogen with it.

Key idea

Both halves of this lesson are mass balances: what comes in, what goes out, what is stored. The FAO-56 method (Allen et al., 1998) keeps the books for water in millimetres per day; a nitrogen balance keeps them in kilograms per hectare per year. Precision agriculture adds a spatial dimension — the books are kept for every zone of the field — and sensors that check the books against reality.

Reference evapotranspiration: FAO-56 Penman–Monteith

How much water does a crop use? It depends on the weather (the evaporative demand of the atmosphere), on the crop (its height, leaf area, stomatal behaviour and reflectance) and on the soil (whether water is available). FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 separates these influences in two steps (Allen et al., 1998). First it defines a reference evapotranspiration ET₀: the evapotranspiration of a hypothetical, extensive, well-watered grass surface of height 0.12 m, with a fixed surface resistance of 70 s m⁻¹ and an albedo of 0.23. ET₀ depends only on the weather. Then it multiplies ET₀ by a crop coefficient that captures how a given crop differs from the reference grass (next section).

ET₀ is calculated with the Penman–Monteith equation, which you met for a single leaf and a crop tier in Lesson 2.2 (Eq. 2.2.9). In resistance form, with \(r_a = 1/g_a\) and \(r_s = 1/g_s\):

\[ \lambda\,ET = \frac{\Delta\,(R_n-G) + \rho_a\,c_p\,(e_s-e_a)/r_a}{\Delta + \gamma\left(1+r_s/r_a\right)} \]

The FAO-56 equation is this same equation with the reference grass inserted and the constants evaluated. The derivation below shows where every number comes from.

FAO-56 Penman–Monteith reference evapotranspiration(11.3.1)
\[ ET_0=\frac{0.408\,\Delta\,(R_n-G)+\gamma\,\dfrac{900}{T+273}\,u_2\,(e_s-e_a)}{\Delta+\gamma\,(1+0.34\,u_2)} \]

reference evapotranspiration equals 0.408 times the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve times the available energy, plus the psychrometric constant times 900 over the air temperature in kelvin times the wind speed at 2 m times the vapour pressure deficit — all divided by the slope plus the psychrometric constant times one plus 0.34 times the wind speed.

Follow the derivation (6 steps, from Eq. 2.2.9 to Eq. 11.3.1)
  1. Aerodynamic resistance of the reference grass. For neutral stability, momentum and heat are transferred by turbulence with \(r_a=\dfrac{\ln\left[(z_m-d)/z_{om}\right]\,\ln\left[(z_h-d)/z_{oh}\right]}{k^2\,u_z}\), where \(k=0.41\) is von Kármán's constant. For grass of height \(h=0.12\) m: zero-plane displacement \(d=\tfrac23 h=0.08\) m, roughness length for momentum \(z_{om}=0.123h=0.01476\) m and for heat and vapour \(z_{oh}=0.1z_{om}\). With wind, temperature and humidity measured at \(z_m=z_h=2\) m: \(r_a = \dfrac{\ln(1.92/0.01476)\,\ln(1.92/0.001476)}{0.41^2\,u_2}=\dfrac{4.868\times7.171}{0.1681\,u_2}=\dfrac{208}{u_2}\) s m⁻¹.
  2. Surface resistance. A well-watered grass leaf has a stomatal resistance of about \(r_l=100\) s m⁻¹; roughly half of the leaf area (the upper, sunlit half) is active, and for clipped grass \(\text{LAI}=24h=2.88\). So \(r_s=r_l/(0.5\,\text{LAI})=100/1.44=69.4\approx70\) s m⁻¹.
  3. The denominator. \(1+r_s/r_a = 1+70\,u_2/208 = 1+0.337\,u_2\approx 1+0.34\,u_2\). ✓
  4. Converting energy to water. Dividing λET (MJ m⁻² d⁻¹) by the latent heat of vaporisation λ = 2.45 MJ kg⁻¹ gives kg m⁻² d⁻¹ = mm d⁻¹, because 1 kg of water spread over 1 m² is 1 mm deep. So the radiation term becomes \(\Delta(R_n-G)/\lambda = 0.408\,\Delta(R_n-G)\), since \(1/2.45=0.408\). ✓
  5. The aerodynamic term. Air density follows from the gas law, \(\rho_a = P/(1.01\,(T+273)\,R)\) with \(R=0.287\) kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, and the psychrometric constant is \(\gamma = c_pP/(\varepsilon\lambda)\) with \(\varepsilon=0.622\). Eliminating \(P\): \(\rho_ac_p=\gamma\varepsilon\lambda/[1.01(T+273)R]\). Multiplying by 86 400 s d⁻¹ (resistances are in s m⁻¹), dividing by \(r_a=208/u_2\) and by λ gives \(\gamma\,\dfrac{0.622\times86\,400}{1.01\times0.287\times208}\,\dfrac{u_2}{T+273}(e_s-e_a)=\gamma\,\dfrac{891}{T+273}\,u_2\,(e_s-e_a)\). FAO rounded 891 to 900. ✓
  6. Assemble. Dividing numerator and denominator of the Penman–Monteith equation by λ and inserting steps 1–5 gives Eq. 11.3.1. The same equation is implemented as et0PenmanMonteith in the course physics library, used by the irrigation laboratory.
Net radiation from sunshine and temperature (what weather stations give you)

Stations measure global (shortwave) radiation \(R_s\), not \(R_n\). FAO-56 estimates net shortwave radiation as \(R_{ns}=(1-0.23)R_s\) and net long-wave loss as \(R_{nl}=\sigma\,\tfrac12\left(T_{\max,K}^4+T_{\min,K}^4\right)(0.34-0.14\sqrt{e_a})\left(1.35\,R_s/R_{so}-0.35\right)\), with \(\sigma = 4.903\times10^{-9}\) MJ K⁻⁴ m⁻² d⁻¹ and the clear-sky radiation \(R_{so}=(0.75+2\times10^{-5}z)R_a\) from the extraterrestrial radiation \(R_a\) (Lesson 4.2). Then \(R_n=R_{ns}-R_{nl}\). On 15 July at Västerås (59.6° N) \(R_a = 39.2\) and \(R_{so} = 29.4\) MJ m⁻² d⁻¹; with \(R_s = 20\), \(T_{\max}=23\) °C, \(T_{\min}=13\) °C and \(e_a = 1.44\) kPa, \(R_{nl}=3.45\) and \(R_n=15.4-3.45\approx12.0\) MJ m⁻² d⁻¹.

Assumptions and validity

ET₀ is defined for a large, uniform, well-watered grass reference, not for your actual field. Weather should be measured over grass, away from buildings; data from dry sites (such as airport tarmac) overestimate ET₀. Daily calculations take \(G\approx 0\). For hourly steps FAO-56 uses different constants (37 instead of 900, and a surface resistance that differs between day and night; Allen et al., 2006). When only temperature data are available, the Hargreaves equation gives a rougher estimate.

Radiation term: (kPa °C⁻¹)(MJ m⁻² d⁻¹)(kg MJ⁻¹) / (kPa °C⁻¹) = kg m⁻² d⁻¹ = mm d⁻¹ ✓. Aerodynamic term: (kPa °C⁻¹)(K⁻¹ … the constant 900 carries kg K kJ⁻¹ d⁻¹)(m s⁻¹)(kPa) / (kPa °C⁻¹) = mm d⁻¹ ✓.

A weather station at 20 m above sea level near Västerås records a mean temperature of 18 °C, a mean relative humidity of 70 %, a wind speed of 2.5 m s⁻¹ at 2 m and a net radiation of 13 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹. Calculate ET₀ and the share of it driven by radiation.

Reveal the reasoning

From reference to crop: crop coefficients

A potato field does not evaporate like reference grass. Early in the season most of the ground is bare and only the wet soil surface evaporates; at full cover the crop is taller, rougher and has more leaf area than clipped grass; near maturity leaves senesce and transpiration falls. FAO-56 lumps all these differences into a crop coefficient \(K_c\) that changes through the season (Allen et al., 1998):

Crop evapotranspiration and the single crop-coefficient curve(11.3.2)
\[ ET_c = K_c\,ET_0,\qquad K_{c,i}=K_{c,\text{prev}}+\frac{i-\sum L_{\text{prev}}}{L_{\text{stage}}}\left(K_{c,\text{next}}-K_{c,\text{prev}}\right) \]

crop evapotranspiration under standard conditions is the crop coefficient times the reference evapotranspiration; during the development and late-season stages the crop coefficient on day i is found by linear interpolation between the values at the start and end of the stage.

Follow the reasoning: the four stages
  1. Initial (sowing to ≈ 10 % cover): ET is mostly soil evaporation, so \(K_{c\,\text{ini}}\) is low (0.3–0.5) and depends strongly on how often the soil surface is wetted by rain or irrigation.
  2. Development (10 % to effective full cover): \(K_c\) rises linearly as the canopy closes.
  3. Mid-season (full cover to start of maturity): \(K_{c\,\text{mid}}\) is constant, typically 1.05–1.20 for field crops, because a closed, tall canopy is rougher and has more leaf area than the grass reference.
  4. Late season (to harvest): \(K_c\) falls towards \(K_{c\,\text{end}}\) as leaves senesce; for cereals harvested dry it falls to 0.25–0.4.
  5. Climate adjustment. The tabulated \(K_{c\,\text{mid}}\) refers to a sub-humid climate (minimum relative humidity 45 %, \(u_2=2\) m s⁻¹). For other climates FAO-56 adds \([0.04(u_2-2)-0.004(RH_{\min}-45)](h/3)^{0.3}\), where \(h\) is crop height in m. In humid Swedish summers (RHmin ≈ 55–60 %) this lowers \(K_{c\,\text{mid}}\) by a few hundredths.
Assumptions and validity

The single coefficient combines crop transpiration and soil evaporation; FAO-56 also offers a "dual" approach (\(K_{cb}+K_e\)) for daily calculations after wetting events. Tabulated values assume a healthy, well-fertilised, disease-free crop under standard conditions; a thin or diseased crop uses less. A review of two decades of measurements confirmed the reliability of the tabulated FAO-56 values (Pereira et al., 2021). Stage lengths vary with variety, planting date and latitude; the remote-sensing methods of Lesson 11.2 can replace fixed stages by observed canopy development.

How much water can the crop use? TAW and RAW

The soil in the root zone is the crop's water tank. After a thorough wetting, water drains away by gravity for a day or two until the soil reaches field capacity (θFC, a matric potential of about −10 to −33 kPa); this is the most water the tank can hold against gravity. As roots extract water, the remaining water is held ever more tightly, until at the permanent wilting point (θWP, conventionally −1500 kPa) plants can extract no more and wilt permanently. The water between the two is available to the crop — but not all of it equally easily. The physics of water potential behind these definitions is in Lesson 2.2.

Total and readily available water(11.3.3)
\[ \text{TAW}=1000\,(\theta_{FC}-\theta_{WP})\,Z_r,\qquad \text{RAW}=p\cdot\text{TAW},\qquad p = p_{\text{table}}+0.04\,(5-ET_c) \]

the total available water in the root zone, in millimetres, is one thousand times the difference between the volumetric water contents at field capacity and wilting point, times the rooting depth in metres. The readily available water is the fraction p of it, and p increases when the evaporative demand is low.

Follow the derivation
  1. Volumetric water content θ is the volume of water per volume of soil (m³ m⁻³). A soil column of 1 m² surface and depth \(Z_r\) holds \(\theta Z_r\) m³ of water.
  2. The extractable volume is \((\theta_{FC}-\theta_{WP})Z_r\) m³ per m² of surface — a depth of water in metres. Multiply by 1000 to express it in mm: Eq. 11.3.3. ✓
  3. Roots extract water easily only while it is loosely held. FAO-56 approximates this with a fixed fraction \(p\) of TAW, tabulated for \(ET_c\approx5\) mm d⁻¹ (Table 1). Assumptions and validity

The bucket is uniform with depth, and field capacity is treated as a sharp limit. Real soils are layered; stones, compacted plough pans and shallow groundwater change the effective TAW. Root depth grows during the season, so TAW grows too. Stress really depends on matric potential, not water content: the same \(p\) corresponds to a lower potential in a clay than in a sand — FAO-56 notes that \(p\) should be reduced by 5–10 % for fine soils and increased for coarse ones.

The daily root-zone water balance

With the tank defined, irrigation scheduling becomes bookkeeping. FAO-56 tracks the root-zone depletion \(D_r\) — how far the root zone is below field capacity — day by day: rain and irrigation reduce it, evapotranspiration increases it, and any water that would push the soil above field capacity drains away as deep percolation (Allen et al., 1998, Eq. 85).

Daily root-zone depletion and the water-stress coefficient(11.3.4)
\[ D_{r,i}=D_{r,i-1}-(P-RO)_i-I_i-CR_i+ET_{c,\text{adj},i}+DP_i,\qquad ET_{c,\text{adj}}=K_s\,K_c\,ET_0,\qquad K_s=\begin{cases}1, & D_r\le \text{RAW}\\[2pt] \dfrac{\text{TAW}-D_r}{(1-p)\,\text{TAW}}, & D_r>\text{RAW}\end{cases} \]

today's depletion is yesterday's depletion minus the rain that infiltrates, minus irrigation and capillary rise, plus the actual crop evapotranspiration, plus any deep percolation. Actual evapotranspiration is the water-stress coefficient times the crop coefficient times reference evapotranspiration, and the stress coefficient is one until the depletion exceeds the readily available water, then falls linearly to zero at the wilting point.

  5. Scheduling rule: to avoid stress, irrigate before \(D_r\) exceeds RAW, with a net depth no larger than \(D_r\) — anything more becomes deep percolation.

A single, well-mixed soil layer; rain and irrigation infiltrate on the day they fall; runoff must be estimated separately (e.g. heavy rain on a crusted or sloping soil). \(K_s\) is evaluated with the depletion at the start of the day. The model ignores the time it takes for water to redistribute and for roots to grow into newly wetted soil — reasons why real fields drift from the model and sensors are needed to correct it.

Scheduling strategies, sensors and application

Three families of scheduling strategy are used in practice (Jones, 2004):

  • Calendar scheduling — a fixed amount at fixed intervals, perhaps adjusted by experience. Simple and predictable, but blind to the weather: in a wet spell it causes drainage and leaching, in a heatwave it lets the crop run dry.
  • Soil-based (sensor) scheduling — irrigate when a soil-moisture sensor shows that the water content or matric potential has reached a threshold corresponding to RAW. It measures the real soil, but only at the sensor's location and depth.
  • Model-based scheduling — the water balance of Eq. 11.3.4, driven by weather data and forecasts. It covers the whole field and can look ahead, but errors in \(K_c\), rain and runoff accumulate; it drifts unless it is corrected with measurements. The best systems combine models with sensors (data assimilation) and, increasingly, plant-based signals such as canopy temperature (the CWSI of Lesson 11.2).

Variable-rate irrigation

A uniform irrigation system applies the same depth everywhere, although — as Worked example 3 showed — a sand ridge and a silty hollow in the same field may need different amounts at different times. Variable-rate irrigation (VRI) makes the application depth site-specific. On centre pivots and linear-move machines this is done either by speed control (the machine moves faster over zones that need less water) or by zone control (individual nozzles or banks of nozzles are pulsed on and off according to a prescription map) (Evans et al., 2013). Prescriptions come from the same layers as in Lesson 11.1 — soil electrical conductivity, yield maps, elevation — plus soil-moisture sensors and, increasingly, thermal and multispectral images. As with variable-rate fertilisation, the hardware is now available; the harder problems are creating good prescriptions and proving that the water, energy and yield gains pay for the investment.

Deficit irrigation and the yield-response factor

Full irrigation — keeping \(D_r\) below RAW all season — maximises yield per hectare. Where water is scarce, expensive or restricted by a permit, it can pay to maximise yield per cubic metre instead. Deficit irrigation deliberately supplies less than the full crop water requirement. Fereres and Soriano (2007) reviewed its use and concluded that, for many field crops, the supply should still be relatively high — enough to achieve 60–100 % of full evapotranspiration — while regulated deficit irrigation of fruit trees and vines, which stresses the crop only in stages when yield is insensitive, can raise both water productivity and profit. How much yield is lost for a given shortfall of water is captured by the FAO-33 yield-response factor (Doorenbos & Kassam, 1979), which FAO-56 uses to link the water balance to yield:

Yield response to water(11.3.6)
\[ 1-\frac{Y_a}{Y_m} = K_y\left(1-\frac{ET_a}{ET_m}\right) \]

the relative yield loss equals the yield-response factor times the relative evapotranspiration deficit.

  2. To first order, the relative loss of yield is therefore proportional to the relative loss of evapotranspiration. The proportionality constant is \(K_y\).
  3. \(K_y>1\): yield falls proportionally more than water use (sensitive crops such as maize, potato); \(K_y<1\): less (tolerant crops such as soybean, cotton).
  4. Water productivity \(Y_a/ET_a\) increases under mild deficit only if \(K_y<1\) over the stages concerned — which is why deficit irrigation is targeted at tolerant crops and stages.
Assumptions and validity

A linear, seasonal relation fitted to experiments, valid for deficits up to about 50 %. Sensitivity differs strongly between growth stages (flowering and tuber initiation are critical), so stage-specific \(K_y\) values exist. Quality effects (tuber size, protein) are not captured.

Nitrogen: balances, efficiency and precision

Nitrogen management follows the same bookkeeping logic as water. The field nitrogen balance compares inputs — mineral fertiliser, manure, atmospheric deposition, biological fixation by legumes, seed — with the output in harvested products. The difference, the nitrogen surplus, is either stored in soil organic matter or lost: leached as nitrate, emitted as ammonia, nitrous oxide or dinitrogen gas. Nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE) indicators express how well inputs are converted into product (Cassman et al., 2002):

Nitrogen balance and use-efficiency indicators(11.3.7)
\[ S_N=\underbrace{(F+M+D+B+S_d)}_{\text{inputs}}-\underbrace{H}_{\text{output}},\quad \text{NUE}=\frac{H}{F+M+D+B+S_d},\quad \text{AE}_N=\frac{Y-Y_0}{F},\quad \text{RE}_N=\frac{U-U_0}{F} \]

the nitrogen surplus is total inputs minus the nitrogen removed in the harvest; the nitrogen-use efficiency is harvested nitrogen divided by inputs; the agronomic efficiency is the extra yield per kilogram of fertiliser nitrogen; the recovery efficiency is the extra crop nitrogen uptake per kilogram of fertiliser nitrogen, both relative to an unfertilised control.

How much nitrogen? The economic optimum

Yield rises with nitrogen, but with diminishing returns. If the response is described by a quadratic curve, the most profitable rate is where the last kilogram of N just pays for itself:

Economic optimum nitrogen rate for a quadratic response(11.3.8)
\[ Y(N)=a+bN+cN^{2}\;(c<0),\qquad \frac{dY}{dN}=b+2cN^{*}=\frac{p_N}{p_Y}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; N^{*}=\frac{p_N/p_Y-b}{2c} \]

with yield a quadratic function of the nitrogen rate, profit is maximised where the marginal yield response, b plus 2cN, equals the price ratio of nitrogen to grain; solving gives the economic optimum rate N star.

Split application and crop nitrogen sensing

Because the optimum depends on the season, Swedish winter wheat growers typically split nitrogen into two or three doses between tillering and booting (Söderström et al., 2017), adjusting the later doses to the crop's development and the weather. The question for each dose is: how much nitrogen has the crop already taken up, and how much does it still need? Plant physiology gives a benchmark. As a crop grows, the nitrogen concentration of its biomass falls — structural tissue dilutes the nitrogen-rich photosynthetic machinery. The critical nitrogen concentration is the minimum concentration that still allows maximum growth; comparing the actual concentration with it gives the nitrogen nutrition index (NNI):

\[ N_c = 5.35\,W^{-0.442}\quad(1.55\le W\le 12\ \text{t ha}^{-1}),\qquad \text{NNI}=\frac{N_{\text{act}}}{N_c} \]

the critical shoot nitrogen concentration, in per cent of dry matter, is 5.35 times the shoot biomass in tonnes per hectare raised to the power minus 0.442; the nitrogen nutrition index is the actual concentration divided by the critical one.

Common misconception

"If I apply the recommended rate, nothing leaches." Leaching happens even from unfertilised fields, because soil organic matter mineralises nitrate after harvest when the crop no longer takes it up. The goal is not zero leaching but to keep the extra leaching caused by management small — by not exceeding the economic optimum, by keeping the soil green in autumn with catch crops, and by avoiding drainage from excess irrigation.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Irrigation and nitrogen decisions are converging into digital advisory systems that combine satellite canopy maps, soil-moisture sensor networks, weather forecasts and crop models, and send prescriptions directly to variable-rate machines. Research focuses on estimating soil nitrogen mineralisation in-season, on data assimilation that keeps the water balance honest, and on quantifying the environmental benefit — the kilograms of nitrate that did not reach the Baltic — so that it can be rewarded.

Application efficiency
The fraction of applied irrigation water that is stored in the root zone.
Deficit irrigation
Deliberately supplying less water than the full crop requirement to raise water productivity.
Yield-response factor (Ky)
The ratio of relative yield loss to relative evapotranspiration deficit (FAO-33).
Variable-rate irrigation (VRI)
Irrigation systems that vary the applied depth within a field according to a prescription map, by speed or nozzle control.
Nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE)
Nitrogen in harvested products divided by nitrogen inputs; judged together with N output and N surplus.
Nitrate-vulnerable zone
An area designated under the EU Nitrates Directive in which stricter rules on manure and fertiliser apply; in Sweden, nitratkänsligt område.
