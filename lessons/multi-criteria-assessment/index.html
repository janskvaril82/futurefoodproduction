Integrated multi-criteria assessment

Which is better: lettuce from a Spanish field, a Swedish greenhouse or a Stockholm vertical farm? After fourteen modules you know that the honest answer is "it depends" — on the grid, the season, the price of capital, the weather, the workers and the weights you give to climate, land, water, cost and resilience. Multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) is the discipline of making that dependence explicit and testable. This final lesson teaches you to build a performance matrix without double counting, to normalise and weight criteria defensibly — including the eigenvector method of the analytic hierarchy process — to aggregate them with the weighted sum, the weighted product and TOPSIS, and to check how robust a ranking is with break-even weights and Monte Carlo. It ends where the course began: with a checklist for assessing any emerging food technology.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the steps of an MCDA and structure a technology choice as options, criteria and a performance matrix, checking for dominance (LO7).
  • Select criteria that are complete, operational, preferentially independent and non-redundant, and detect double counting (LO7).
  • Normalise indicators with min–max, target and distance-to-target methods and explain how normalisation can reverse rankings (LO3).
  • Derive weights by direct rating, swing weighting and the analytic hierarchy process, including the principal eigenvector and the consistency ratio (LO3).
  • Aggregate with the weighted sum, weighted product and TOPSIS, explain compensation and outranking, and test robustness with break-even weights and Monte Carlo rank acceptability (LO3, LO7).
  • Apply an integrated checklist — planetary boundaries, resource efficiency, resilience, accessibility, acceptance, regulation — to any emerging food technology (LO1, LO7).

Why one number is never enough

Each lesson of this module produced numbers that point in different directions. A vertical farm uses about a thirtieth of the land and less than a hundredth of the water of field lettuce (Table 1, from Zhuang et al., 2022, and Kozai, 2013), but on an average European grid it emits several times more CO₂e per kilogram (Lesson 15.1); it survives droughts but not insolvency (Lesson 15.2); and at about US$7.5 per kilogram its lettuce is a food for the affluent (Lesson 15.3). A decision-maker — a municipality choosing where to site a food hub, a retailer choosing suppliers, a ministry designing subsidies — must nevertheless decide. Collapsing everything into money (cost–benefit analysis) requires prices for carbon, water, biodiversity and resilience that often do not exist; reporting a dashboard of indicators leaves the trade-offs to intuition. Multi-criteria decision analysis sits in between: it keeps each criterion in its own unit, makes the value judgements — which criteria matter and how much — explicit, and shows how the conclusion depends on them (Belton & Stewart, 2002).

Key idea

MCDA does not make value judgements disappear; it makes them visible. Two honest analysts with the same data can reach different rankings because they weight climate against cost differently. The analysis is successful when everyone can see which judgement produces which ranking — and which conclusions hold whatever the weights.

Criteria and the performance matrix

The running example of this lesson is a question you can now answer quantitatively: how should a Stockholm retailer source lettuce in January? Four options: (A) field lettuce from Almería, trucked 3,500 km; (B) a Swedish greenhouse heated with natural gas; (C) a Swedish greenhouse with a heat pump on the Swedish grid; (D) a Stockholm vertical farm on the Swedish grid. Criteria are organised in a value tree that breaks the overall goal into measurable attributes (Figure 2).

Good criteria have five properties (Keeney & Raiffa, 1993; Belton & Stewart, 2002): they are complete (together they capture everything that matters), operational (measurable, with a clear direction of preference), preferentially independent (how much you value an improvement in one does not depend on the level of another), non-redundant (no aspect is counted twice) and minimal (as few as possible). Redundancy is the most common error in food-technology assessments. Energy use and greenhouse-gas emissions of a vertical farm are almost the same information (Lesson 15.1: \(G_{\text{el}} = e_{\text{el}}\,\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}\)); land occupation and biodiversity loss often are too. Including both halves of such a pair silently doubles its weight. A simple check is to compute the correlation of each pair of criteria across the options, and to ask of every highly correlated pair whether the two measure different values or merely the same physical cause.

Normalisation: putting apples and kilowatt-hours on one scale

Criteria come in different units and directions. Normalisation converts each column of the performance matrix into a dimensionless score, usually between 0 (worst) and 1 (best). The choice of method is itself a modelling decision with consequences (OECD & JRC, 2008).

Min–max normalisation(15.4.1)
\[ \text{(a) benefit: } v_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}-\min_i x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij}-\min_i x_{ij}} \qquad \text{(b) cost: } v_{ij} = \frac{\max_i x_{ij}-x_{ij}}{\max_i x_{ij}-\min_i x_{ij}} \]

the normalised score of option i on criterion j is its distance from the worst value in the set divided by the range between the worst and the best value; for benefit criteria the worst is the minimum, for cost criteria the maximum.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x_{ij}\)performance of option \(i\) on criterion \(j\)unit of criterion \(j\)
\(\min_i x_{ij}\), \(\max_i x_{ij}\)smallest and largest value of criterion \(j\) among the optionsunit of criterion \(j\)
\(v_{ij}\)normalised score, 1 = best in the set, 0 = worst–
Follow the derivation
  1. We want a linear map \(v=\alpha x+\beta\) that sends the best value to 1 and the worst to 0. For a benefit criterion: \(\alpha\max+\beta=1\), \(\alpha\min+\beta=0\), hence \(\alpha=1/(\max-\min)\), \(\beta=-\min/(\max-\min)\): form (a).
  2. For a cost criterion swap the roles of max and min: form (b), equivalent to \(1-\)(a).
  3. Linearity preserves ratios of differences: an improvement from 0.62 to 0.55 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ is worth the same number of points wherever it occurs — an interval scale, which is what the weighted sum requires.
  4. Example (climate, cost-type): range 0.55–2.52. Option A: \((2.52-0.62)/1.97=0.964\); option D: \((2.52-0.84)/1.97=0.853\).
Assumptions and validity

The scale is defined by the options in the set. Add or remove an option that sets a maximum or minimum and every score in that column changes — and rankings can reverse (Worked example 1). The best option always gets 1 and the worst 0, however small the real difference: a range of 0.55–0.62 kg CO₂e would be stretched to 0–1 just like a range of 0.5–15. Linearity also assumes that each unit of improvement is equally valuable, which fails near thresholds.

\(\dfrac{\text{unit}}{\text{unit}}\) = dimensionless ✓

Two alternatives avoid the dependence on the option set. Target normalisation divides by an external reference — a policy target, a planetary-boundary share, a best-available-technology value — so that the scale means the same thing whatever options are compared. Distance-to-target expresses how far an option is from that reference.

Target and distance-to-target normalisation(15.4.2)
\[ d_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}-x_j^{\text{target}}}{x_j^{\text{target}}}, \qquad v_{ij} = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{x_j^{\text{target}}}{x_{ij}}\right)\ \ \text{(cost criteria)} \]

the relative distance to target is the difference between the value and the target, divided by the target; a bounded target-based score for a cost criterion is the target divided by the value, capped at one when the target is met.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x_j^{\text{target}}\)externally defined target or reference for criterion \(j\)unit of criterion \(j\)
\(d_{ij}\)relative distance to target: > 0 target exceeded (for a cost criterion: missed), < 0 headroom–
\(v_{ij}\)target-based score–
Follow the derivation
  1. Divide the gap to the target by the target itself to obtain a unit-free relative distance, as in the transgression ratio of Lesson 1.2.
  2. For scoring, the ratio target/value rewards improvement in proportion (halving emissions doubles the score) and stops rewarding once the target is met — a deliberate non-linearity that expresses "good enough".
  3. Example: with a target of 0.50 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹, option D (0.84) has \(d=(0.84-0.50)/0.50=0.68\) (68 % above target) and \(v=0.50/0.84=0.60\).
Assumptions and validity

The result is only as defensible as the target. Science-based targets (a per-capita share of a planetary boundary, a national climate budget) are preferable to arbitrary round numbers. The EU Environmental Footprint method normalises impact categories by global per-person impacts before weighting — a form of external normalisation.

dimensionless ✓

Other methods you will meet: vector normalisation, \(x_{ij}/\sqrt{\sum_i x_{ij}^2}\), used by TOPSIS; ratio (max) normalisation, \(x_{ij}/\max_i x_{ij}\) for benefits and \(\min_i x_{ij}/x_{ij}\) for costs, used with the weighted product; and z-scores in composite indicators (OECD & JRC, 2008).

Weighting: direct rating, swing weights and the AHP

Weights express how much a stakeholder is willing to give up on one criterion to gain on another. The simplest method, direct rating or point allocation ("distribute 100 points over the criteria"), is quick but has a fatal flaw: people answer "how important is climate?" without thinking about how much climate performance is at stake between the options. If all options emitted between 0.55 and 0.62 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹, climate should get almost no weight in this decision, however important climate change is in general. This is the range-sensitivity principle, and swing weighting builds it in (von Winterfeldt & Edwards, 1986):

  1. Imagine a hypothetical option that is worst on every criterion.
  2. Ask: if you could improve just one criterion from its worst to its best value in this option set, which swing would you choose first? Give it 100 points.
  3. Rate every other swing relative to it (a swing half as valuable gets 50).
  4. Normalise: \(w_j = s_j/\sum_k s_k\).

When criteria are many, or judgements are easier in pairs, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) of Saaty (1977, 1980) asks only pairwise questions — "how much more important is climate than cost?" — on a 1–9 scale (1 equal, 3 moderately, 5 strongly, 7 very strongly, 9 extremely more important; the reciprocal for the reverse). The answers fill a reciprocal matrix \(A\) with \(a_{ij}\) = importance of \(i\) relative to \(j\) and \(a_{ji}=1/a_{ij}\).

AHP weights: the principal eigenvector(15.4.3)
\[ A\,\mathbf{w} = \lambda_{\max}\,\mathbf{w}, \qquad \sum_j w_j = 1, \qquad w_i \approx \frac{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}\right)^{1/n}}{\sum_k\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n} a_{kj}\right)^{1/n}} \]

the weight vector is the principal eigenvector of the pairwise comparison matrix — the vector that the matrix only stretches, by its largest eigenvalue λmax — scaled to sum to one; a good approximation is the normalised geometric mean of each row.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(A=[a_{ij}]\)pairwise comparison matrix, \(a_{ij}\) = importance of criterion \(i\) over \(j\) (1/9 … 9), \(a_{ji}=1/a_{ij}\)–
\(\mathbf{w}\)weight vector–
\(\lambda_{\max}\)largest (principal) eigenvalue of \(A\); \(\lambda_{\max}\ge n\)–
\(n\)number of criteria–
Follow the derivation
  1. Suppose the true weights \(w\) exist and the judgements are perfectly consistent: \(a_{ij}=w_i/w_j\). Then row \(i\) of \(A\) times \(w\) is \(\sum_j (w_i/w_j)w_j = n\,w_i\): \(A\mathbf w=n\mathbf w\). The weights are an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(n\).
  2. A consistent matrix has rank 1 (every row is a multiple of the first), so \(n\) is its only non-zero eigenvalue. Real judgements contain small errors; Saaty showed that the principal eigenvector of the perturbed matrix is still the best estimate of the weights, and by the Perron–Frobenius theorem a positive matrix has a unique positive principal eigenvector.
  3. Computing it by power iteration: start with \(w=(1/n,\dots)\), repeat \(w\leftarrow Aw/\sum(Aw)\) until it stops changing. The figure below does exactly this.
  4. Geometric-mean shortcut: for consistent \(A\), \(\big(\prod_j a_{ij}\big)^{1/n}=\big(\prod_j w_i/w_j\big)^{1/n} = w_i/(\prod_j w_j)^{1/n}\) — proportional to \(w_i\). For nearly consistent matrices it differs from the eigenvector only in the third decimal.
  5. Example: \(a_{12}=3\), \(a_{13}=5\), \(a_{23}=2\). Row geometric means: \((1\cdot3\cdot5)^{1/3}=2.466\), \((\tfrac13\cdot1\cdot2)^{1/3}=0.874\), \((\tfrac15\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1)^{1/3}=0.464\); normalised: \(w=(0.648, 0.230, 0.122)\).
Assumptions and validity

AHP weights answer "how important?" without reference to ranges unless the analyst insists on range-aware questions — the same trap as direct rating. Adding a new option to AHP scoring of alternatives can reverse the ranking of the old ones (Belton & Gear, 1983). Use AHP for criterion weights with range-aware wording, and check consistency.

Consistency index and consistency ratio(15.4.4)
\[ \text{CI} = \frac{\lambda_{\max}-n}{n-1}, \qquad \text{CR} = \frac{\text{CI}}{\text{RI}_n}, \qquad \lambda_{\max} \approx \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(A\mathbf w)_i}{w_i} \]

the consistency index is the excess of the largest eigenvalue over the number of criteria, divided by n − 1; the consistency ratio divides it by the average index of random matrices of the same size; λmax can be estimated as the average ratio of A w to w.

Aggregation: weighted sum, TOPSIS and outranking

With normalised scores and weights, the options can be ranked. The most transparent method is the weighted sum, also called simple additive weighting or the additive value model.

Weighted-sum model(15.4.5)
\[ V_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j\,v_{ij}, \qquad \sum_j w_j = 1 \]

the overall value of option i is the sum over all criteria of the weight times the normalised score.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(V_i\)overall value of option \(i\), 0–1–
\(w_j\)weight of criterion \(j\)–
\(v_{ij}\)normalised score (Eq. 15.4.1 or 15.4.2)–
\(m\)number of criteria–
Follow the derivation
  1. Multi-attribute value theory (Keeney & Raiffa, 1993) shows that if criteria are mutually preferentially independent, preferences can be represented by an additive value function \(V=\sum_j w_j\,v_j(x_j)\).
  2. With linear single-criterion value functions (min–max) and swing weights, \(w_j\) is the value of moving criterion \(j\) across its whole range.
  3. Running example with the swing weights: \(V_C = 0.333(1)+0.100(0.927)+0.133(0.927)+0.267(0.763)+0.167(1) = 0.920\).
Assumptions and validity

Additivity means full compensation: a poor score on one criterion can always be offset by good scores elsewhere (Section 15.4.6). Scores must be on interval scales and weights must match the normalisation (swing weights with min–max).

dimensionless ✓

TOPSIS — the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (Hwang & Yoon, 1981) — ranks options by their geometric position relative to an ideal and an anti-ideal option.

TOPSIS closeness coefficient(15.4.6)
\[ u_{ij} = w_j\frac{x_{ij}}{\sqrt{\sum_k x_{kj}^{2}}},\quad D_i^{\pm} = \sqrt{\sum_j\big(u_{ij}-u_j^{\pm}\big)^{2}},\quad C_i = \frac{D_i^{-}}{D_i^{+}+D_i^{-}} \]

each value is vector-normalised and weighted; the ideal point takes the best weighted value of every criterion and the anti-ideal the worst; D⁺ and D⁻ are the Euclidean distances of an option from them; the closeness coefficient is the distance from the anti-ideal divided by the sum of both distances.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(u_{ij}\)weighted, vector-normalised value–
\(u_j^{+}\), \(u_j^{-}\)ideal and anti-ideal values: best and worst \(u_{ij}\) of criterion \(j\) (min for cost, max for benefit criteria, and vice versa)–
\(D_i^{+}\), \(D_i^{-}\)Euclidean distances to the ideal and anti-ideal points–
\(C_i\)closeness coefficient: 1 = at the ideal, 0 = at the anti-ideal–
Follow the derivation
  1. Treat each option as a point in an \(m\)-dimensional space of weighted, normalised criteria. Vector normalisation makes each criterion column a unit vector before weighting, so no criterion dominates through its unit.
  2. Construct two reference points from the columns: the ideal \(A^{+}\) (best of each) and the anti-ideal \(A^{-}\) (worst of each). Neither need exist as a real option.
  3. The best option should be close to \(A^{+}\) and far from \(A^{-}\). These can conflict; the ratio \(C_i=D_i^-/(D_i^++D_i^-)\) balances them and lies between 0 and 1.
  4. Running example (swing weights): \(C=\) 0.878 (C), 0.626 (A), 0.586 (D), 0.445 (B) — D and A swap places compared with the weighted sum, because vector normalisation treats D's cost disadvantage and A's land and water disadvantages differently from min–max.
Assumptions and validity

Euclidean distance lets large deviations on one criterion count more than several small ones — partly non-compensatory. Like min–max, the ideal points depend on the option set, so rank reversal is possible (Opricovic & Tzeng, 2004).

dimensionless ✓

A third family, outranking, drops the idea of a single value score. ELECTRE (Roy, 1991) asks for each pair of options whether there is enough evidence (concordance: the weight of criteria on which A is at least as good as B) and no strong counter-evidence (discordance, including vetoes) to say "A is at least as good as B". PROMETHEE (Brans & Vincke, 1985) turns each pairwise difference into a preference degree between 0 and 1 via a preference function with indifference and preference thresholds, and ranks options by their net flow — how much they outrank others minus how much they are outranked. Outranking methods accept that some options are incomparable and that a very poor score can block an option, which suits sustainability questions with non-negotiable limits (Cinelli et al., 2014).

Compensation, strong sustainability and vetoes

The weighted sum is fully compensatory: with equal weights, an option scoring 0.95 on cost, 0.9 on resilience and 0.05 on biodiversity gets \(V=0.63\), a respectable score, although it nearly destroys biodiversity. Whether that is acceptable is a question of sustainability philosophy. Weak sustainability assumes that natural capital can be substituted by other forms of capital; strong sustainability holds that critical natural capital — a stable climate, functioning biosphere — cannot be. The planetary-boundaries framework of Lesson 1.2 is a strong-sustainability idea: a boundary is a limit, not a price. MCDA offers three ways to limit compensation.

Weighted-product model(15.4.7)
\[ P_i = \prod_{j=1}^{m} v_{ij}^{\,w_j} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \ln P_i = \sum_j w_j\ln v_{ij} \]

the overall score is the product of the normalised scores, each raised to the power of its weight — equivalently, the weighted sum of their logarithms.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(P_i\)weighted-product score (a weighted geometric mean)–
\(v_{ij}\)strictly positive normalised score, usually ratio-normalised: \(x/\max\) for benefits, \(\min/x\) for costs–
Follow the derivation
  1. Take logarithms: the product becomes a weighted sum of \(\ln v_{ij}\). Because \(\ln v\to-\infty\) as \(v\to0\), a near-zero score on any criterion drags the whole score towards zero: compensation is limited.
  2. Marginal rate of substitution between two criteria: \(\frac{\partial P/\partial v_1}{\partial P/\partial v_2}=\frac{w_1/v_1}{w_2/v_2}\). Gaining on a criterion where you are already good buys less than repairing one where you are poor — unlike the constant trade-off \(w_1/w_2\) of the weighted sum.
  3. The WPM compares ratios, so it needs ratio-scale scores with a natural zero; min–max scores of 0 would make the product 0. With ratio normalisation, the WPM ranking is independent of the units of each criterion.
  4. The example above: \((0.95\times0.9\times0.05)^{1/3}=0.35\) instead of 0.63.
Assumptions and validity

Ratio normalisation rewards orders-of-magnitude differences strongly: a vertical farm's water use of 2 L kg⁻¹ versus 250 gives field lettuce a water score of 0.008, which the product punishes severely. That may be exactly what a water-scarce region wants — or an exaggeration. Always look at the normalised matrix, not only at the final scores.

dimensionless ✓

The strongest limit on compensation is a threshold or veto: options that violate a minimum requirement — exceeding a per-capita share of a planetary boundary, failing a food-safety rule, lacking a legal route to market — are excluded before aggregation. Screening then ranking is common in practice and transparent to stakeholders.

Sensitivity and robustness

A ranking is only as credible as its robustness. Two questions should accompany every MCDA result: how much would a weight have to change to change the answer? and across all plausible weights, how often does each option come first?

Break-even weight between two options(15.4.8)
\[ w^{*} = \frac{b_2-a_2}{(a_1-b_1)+(b_2-a_2)} \qquad\text{(two criteria, weights } w \text{ and } 1-w\text{)} \]

with two criteria, option A with scores a₁ and a₂ and option B with scores b₁ and b₂ are equally good when the weight of criterion 1 equals B's advantage on criterion 2 divided by the sum of A's advantage on criterion 1 and B's advantage on criterion 2; above w* option A is preferred.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a_1, a_2\), \(b_1, b_2\)normalised scores of options A and B on criteria 1 and 2 (A better on 1, B better on 2)–
\(w\), \(1-w\)weights of criteria 1 and 2–
\(w^{*}\)break-even (rank-reversal) weight–
Follow the derivation
  1. Weighted sums: \(V_A = w a_1+(1-w)a_2\), \(V_B = w b_1+(1-w)b_2\).
  2. Set \(V_A=V_B\): \(w(a_1-b_1) = (1-w)(b_2-a_2)\). Collect terms in \(w\): \(w\,[(a_1-b_1)+(b_2-a_2)] = b_2-a_2\). Divide.
  3. More criteria: vary one weight \(w_j\) and rescale the others in proportion, \(w_k' = w_k(1-w_j)/(1-w_j^{0})\). Each \(V_i\) is then linear in \(w_j\): \(V_i = w_j v_{ij} + (1-w_j)R_i\) with \(R_i=\sum_{k\ne j}w_k^{0}v_{ik}/(1-w_j^{0})\), and two options swap where the lines cross — the same algebra.
  4. Running example (weighted sum, swing weights): C stays first unless the cost weight rises above 0.695 (then A wins), the land weight above 0.83 or the water weight above 0.84 (then D wins). The original weights were 0.267, 0.100 and 0.133: far from any break-even point — C's lead is robust to one-at-a-time changes.
dimensionless ✓

One-at-a-time analysis misses combinations of weight changes. Stochastic multicriteria acceptability analysis (SMAA; Lahdelma et al., 1998) explores all of them: sample thousands of weight vectors — uniformly from all possible weights if nothing is known, or from ranges elicited from stakeholders — rank the options for each, and count how often each option attains each rank.

Rank acceptability index (Monte Carlo)(15.4.9)
\[ b_i^{r} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\!\left[\operatorname{rank}_i\!\big(\mathbf w^{(k)}\big)=r\right], \qquad w_j^{(k)} = \frac{E_j^{(k)}}{\sum_l E_l^{(k)}},\;\; E_j^{(k)} = -\ln U_j^{(k)} \]

the acceptability of rank r for option i is the fraction of N sampled weight vectors under which option i attains rank r; uniformly distributed weight vectors are obtained by drawing independent exponential random numbers — minus the logarithm of uniform random numbers — and dividing by their sum.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(b_i^{r}\)rank acceptability index; \(b_i^{1}\) = share of weight space in which \(i\) is best–
\(\mathbf w^{(k)}\)\(k\)-th sampled weight vector–
\(U_j^{(k)}\)uniform random number on (0, 1)–
\(N\)number of Monte Carlo draws–
Follow the derivation
  1. The set of valid weight vectors (non-negative, summing to 1) is a simplex. "Uniform over the simplex" is the Dirichlet distribution with all parameters equal to 1.
  2. A Dirichlet(1,…,1) vector is obtained by normalising independent Gamma(1) — that is, exponential — random numbers, and an exponential number is \(-\ln U\) for uniform \(U\). Normalising independent uniforms directly would not be uniform on the simplex.
  3. Each draw gives a ranking; \(b_i^r\) is a binomial proportion, so its Monte Carlo standard error is \(\sqrt{b(1-b)/N}\) — the formula of Lesson 15.1 (Eq. 15.1.9). With \(N=10{,}000\) the error is below 0.5 percentage points.
  4. Uncertain performance values can be sampled in the same loop, giving acceptabilities that combine preference and data uncertainty.
Assumptions and validity

"Uniform weights" means maximal ignorance about preferences — rarely true. Constrain the sampling with what stakeholders do agree on (e.g. "climate at least as important as land") and report the results for each stakeholder group separately rather than averaging away disagreement.

Frameworks for integrated sustainability assessment

MCDA is a method; frameworks tell you what to assess. Four are widely used in food and agriculture.

  • Triple bottom line. Elkington (1994) urged companies to account for environmental and social performance alongside profit — "people, planet, profit". It is a reminder of scope, not a method: it says nothing about how to trade the three off.
  • Life cycle sustainability assessment (LCSA). Kloepffer (2008) proposed LCSA = environmental LCA + life cycle costing + social LCA, all with the same functional unit and system boundary, so that the three pillars are assessed consistently along the life cycle.
  • FAO SAFA. The Sustainability Assessment of Food and Agriculture systems guidelines (FAO, 2014) define four dimensions — good governance, environmental integrity, economic resilience and social well-being — divided into 21 themes, 58 sub-themes and 116 indicators, assessed in four stages: mapping, contextualisation, selection of tools and indicators, and reporting. Results are shown as sustainability polygons rather than a single score; a simplified smallholder version uses 44 indicators (Pérez-Lombardini et al., 2021).
  • Absolute sustainability. Planetary boundaries (Richardson et al., 2023; Lesson 1.2) turn part of the assessment into thresholds: a technology is judged by whether its footprint fits within an allocated share of each boundary, not only by whether it beats an alternative.

Reviews of MCDA in environmental science show a steady growth of applications and recommend combining methods with stakeholder deliberation and uncertainty analysis (Huang et al., 2011; Cinelli et al., 2014; Diaz-Balteiro et al., 2017). For vertical farming specifically, Dziumla et al. Synthesis: how to assess any emerging food technology

This is the last lesson of the course, so let us step back. You have met vertical farms and aquaponics, gene-edited crops and robots, precision fermentation and insects, sensors and neural networks. New ones will appear every year of your career, each with a confident pitch. The course has given you the tools to answer the only question that matters: is this technology actually better — and for whom? The checklist below condenses the modules into eight lenses. Use it as a structure for the course project, for reviewing a scientific paper or a business plan, and for your own MCDA.

The last lens deserves emphasis. Collingridge (1980) described the dilemma at the heart of technology assessment: early on, a technology is easy to change but its impacts are hard to foresee; once its impacts are clear, it is entrenched and hard to change. Responsible innovation answers with four habits (Stilgoe et al., 2013): anticipation (ask "what if?" early), inclusion (involve those affected), reflexivity (question your own assumptions and interests) and responsiveness (be willing to change direction). An MCDA done well is an instrument for all four.

The whole course in one sentence

A new food technology is better only if it delivers more nutrition within planetary boundaries, uses resources efficiently, makes the food system more resilient, is accessible and fair, is accepted and legal — and if you can show, with numbers and their uncertainty, which of these holds, for whom, and under which assumptions.

Check your understanding

Key terms

Multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA)
A family of methods that evaluate options on several criteria in their own units, make value judgements explicit and show how conclusions depend on them.
Performance matrix
A table of options × criteria containing the measured or estimated performance of each option on each criterion.
Dominance
Option A dominates B if it is at least as good on every criterion and strictly better on at least one; dominated options can be discarded whatever the weights.
Double counting
Including two criteria that measure the same underlying effect (e.g. electricity use and emissions of an electric system), which silently doubles its weight.
Min–max normalisation
Linear rescaling of a criterion so that the best option in the set scores 1 and the worst 0; sensitive to the option set (rank reversal).
Distance to target
The relative gap between a value and an external target, \((x-x_{\text{target}})/x_{\text{target}}\).
Swing weighting
Eliciting weights by valuing the improvement from the worst to the best value of each criterion in the option set.
Analytic hierarchy process (AHP)
Deriving weights from pairwise comparisons on a 1–9 scale as the principal eigenvector of the comparison matrix.
Consistency ratio
\(\text{CR} = [(\lambda_{\max}-n)/(n-1)]/\text{RI}\); judgements with CR ≤ 0.10 are considered acceptably consistent.
Weighted-sum model
Overall score \(\sum w_j v_j\); transparent and fully compensatory.
Weighted-product model
Overall score \(\prod v_j^{w_j}\); penalises weak criteria and limits compensation.
TOPSIS
Ranking by relative closeness to an ideal point and distance from an anti-ideal point in weighted, vector-normalised criterion space.
Outranking
Methods such as ELECTRE and PROMETHEE that compare options pairwise with concordance, discordance and preference functions instead of a single value score.
Compensation
The extent to which poor performance on one criterion can be offset by good performance on others; limited compensation reflects strong sustainability.
Rank acceptability index
The share of sampled weight vectors (and data scenarios) under which an option attains a given rank (SMAA).

Check your reasoning

  • I can structure a technology choice as options, criteria and a performance matrix, check for dominance and avoid double counting.
  • I can normalise criteria with min–max and distance-to-target methods and explain why min–max can cause rank reversal.
  • I can elicit swing weights and derive AHP weights with the eigenvector or geometric-mean method, and check the consistency ratio.
  • I can rank options with the weighted sum, the weighted product and TOPSIS, and explain why the rankings differ.
  • I can compute a break-even weight and interpret Monte Carlo rank acceptabilities with their standard errors.
  • I can use the eight-lens checklist to assess any emerging food technology and name its weakest spoke.
