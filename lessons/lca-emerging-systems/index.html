Life-cycle assessment of emerging food systems

In January, a head of lettuce on a shelf in Stockholm may have grown in an open field in Almería, in a heated greenhouse in Skåne or on the eighth tier of a vertical farm a few kilometres away. Its carbon footprint can differ by a factor of twenty between these routes — and, for the vertical farm, by a factor of thirty depending only on which power station happens to feed the grid. This lesson teaches you to compare such systems honestly: to find what really drives their footprints, to derive the grid intensity at which a new technology breaks even, and to state how confident you are in the answer.

What you will be able to do

  • Set up a comparative life-cycle assessment of field, greenhouse and vertical-farm lettuce with a defensible functional unit and system boundary (LO3, LO7).
  • Calculate the contributions of electricity, heat, transport, packaging and food waste to the carbon footprint of a vegetable, with unit checks (LO3).
  • Derive and apply the break-even grid intensity and explain why the energy source dominates the footprint of controlled-environment agriculture (LO1, LO3).
  • Distinguish attributional, consequential and prospective LCA and choose the right one for a given decision (LO1, LO7).
  • Quantify uncertainty with Monte Carlo analysis and interpret the probability that one option is better than another (LO3, LO7).
  • Critically read published LCAs of emerging food systems, including land sparing and rebound effects (LO1, LO7).

Life-cycle thinking, revisited for new technologies

You met life-cycle assessment (LCA) in Lesson 1.3. It is standardised in ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 (2006) and proceeds in four iterative phases: goal and scope definition (what question, which functional unit, which system boundary), life-cycle inventory (LCI: every input and emission per functional unit), life-cycle impact assessment (LCIA: converting emissions into impact indicators such as global warming potential, GWP) and interpretation (contribution, sensitivity and uncertainty analysis, and conclusions that respect the limitations). Nothing in that recipe changes when the product comes from a vertical farm. What changes is how hard each step becomes.

Emerging food systems — vertical farms, cultivated meat, precision fermentation, insect farms — share four features that make their LCAs difficult and their results fragile:

  • They replace free natural flows with purchased energy. Sunlight, rain and soil warmth are replaced by LEDs, HVAC and pumps. The footprint therefore depends on a background system — the electricity grid — that the farmer does not control and that changes from hour to hour and from year to year.
  • They are small and young. Today's pilot plants are less efficient than tomorrow's full-scale plants. An LCA of a 2019 farm may say little about a 2030 farm (the problem of prospective LCA, §15.1.9).
  • They change other systems. A vertical farm might spare farmland, displace imports, add demand to a grid at night, or create new consumption. Whether these consequences belong in the assessment depends on the question (the problem of consequential LCA).
  • Their data are uncertain and often proprietary. Companies rarely publish their meter readings, so studies rely on a handful of case farms (the problem of uncertainty, §15.1.10).
Key idea

A comparative LCA answers a specific question: which way of delivering one kilogram of lettuce to a Stockholm consumer in January causes the least climate impact, under stated assumptions? Change the month, the city, the grid, the functional unit (kg? kg eaten? kg of dry matter? a nutrient?) or the time horizon and you are asking a different question — and may get a different answer.

From inventory to footprint: the bookkeeping equation

Every carbon footprint, however sophisticated the software that produced it, is a sum of products: the amount of each activity needed per functional unit multiplied by the emissions per unit of that activity. Writing this down explicitly is the single most useful habit you can develop, because it lets you check any published number on the back of an envelope.

Carbon footprint as a sum of activities(15.1.1)
\[ G = \sum_{i} a_i\,\text{EF}_i \]

the carbon footprint per functional unit equals the sum, over all activities, of the amount of each activity needed for one functional unit multiplied by that activity's emission factor.

SymbolMeaningUnitExample
\(G\)carbon footprint (GWP₁₀₀) per functional unitkg CO₂e FU⁻¹0.6 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹
\(a_i\)activity amount per functional unit (the inventory)kWh, t·km, kg … per FU12 kWh kg⁻¹
\(\text{EF}_i\)emission factor of activity \(i\): life-cycle GHG emissions per unit of activitykg CO₂e per kWh, per t·km, per kg …0.211 kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. An LCA model is a network of unit processes (farm, fertiliser factory, power station, truck…). Each process emits greenhouse gases \(k\) (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O…) in proportion to its output.
  2. The inventory step scales every process to the functional unit: process \(i\) must run at level \(a_i\) to deliver one FU.
  3. Impact assessment converts each gas into CO₂-equivalents with its global warming potential, \(\text{GWP}_k\) (e.g., 27 for non-fossil and 30 for fossil CH₄ and 273 for N₂O over 100 years in IPCC AR6), so each process has a single emission factor \(\text{EF}_i = \sum_k e_{ik}\,\text{GWP}_k\).
  4. Because LCA models are linear, total impact is the sum of scaled contributions: \(G=\sum_i a_i\,\text{EF}_i\). Background databases such as ecoinvent pre-compute the emission factors of whole upstream supply chains, so the foreground model only needs a handful of \(a_i\).
Assumptions and validity

Linearity: doubling production doubles every input (no economies of scale inside the model). Emission factors are averages over their own supply chains and years; choosing between an average and a marginal factor is a methodological decision (§15.1.9). The equation also hides allocation: when a process has several products (combined heat and power, a greenhouse selling both tomatoes and heat), its emissions must be split, and the split changes \(\text{EF}_i\).

\(\sum \dfrac{\text{activity unit}}{\text{FU}}\cdot\dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{activity unit}} = \dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{FU}}\) ✓ — every term must cancel to the same unit, which is a powerful error check.

The defaults above describe a Swedish greenhouse heated with natural gas in winter: 8 kWh of heat per kilogram at 0.262 kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ gives 2.1 kg CO₂e, and that single line dwarfs the electricity and transport lines. Try replacing the gas with wood chips (≈ 0.02 kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ of heat) and watch the ranking of contributions change completely. This is contribution analysis, the first thing to do with any LCA result: find the one or two terms that carry most of the total, and spend your effort on getting those right.

Electricity: the dominant term for indoor farms

For a plant factory with artificial lighting, electricity is almost the whole story. Graamans et al. (2018) modelled lettuce production and found that a plant factory needs about 247 kWh of electricity per kilogram of dry matter — roughly 12 kWh per kilogram of fresh lettuce at 5 % dry matter — against 70 kWh kg⁻¹ DW for a Dutch greenhouse and 182–211 kWh kg⁻¹ DW for Swedish greenhouses, where heating and supplementary light are needed. A 2025 review of vertical-farm studies reports energy-use efficiencies of about 0.08–0.13 kg fresh weight per kWh, i.e. 8–12.5 kWh per kilogram (Pennisi et al., 2025), and Casey et al. (2022) used about 15 kWh kg⁻¹ for lighting, cooling, ventilation and pumping. In a cradle-to-grave study of a Dutch vertical farm, electricity caused 85 % of the carbon footprint (Blom et al., 2022).

Electricity-related footprint(15.1.2)
\[ G_{\text{el}} = e_{\text{el}}\cdot\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}, \qquad e_{\text{el}} = \frac{\text{DLI}\cdot 365}{\eta_{\text{ph}}\,\eta_{\text{cap}}\,Y\cdot 3.6}\cdot\frac{1}{f_{\text{light}}} \]

the electricity footprint per kilogram is the electricity used per kilogram times the grid emission factor; the electricity per kilogram follows from the photons the crop needs, the efficiency with which the LEDs make and deliver them, the yield, and the fraction of the farm's electricity that is used for lighting.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical
\(e_{\text{el}}\)electricity per kg of fresh producekWh kg⁻¹8–15
\(\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}\)emission factor of the electricity usedkg CO₂e kWh⁻¹0.02–0.7
DLIdaily light integral delivered to the cropmol m⁻² d⁻¹15–17
\(\eta_{\text{ph}}\)photon efficacy of the LEDsµmol J⁻¹2.7–3.5
\(\eta_{\text{cap}}\)fraction of photons captured by the canopy–0.7–0.9
\(Y\)yield per growing areakg m⁻² yr⁻¹60–105
\(f_{\text{light}}\)share of total electricity used by lighting–0.5–0.7
Follow the derivation of \(e_{\text{el}}\)
  1. Photons needed per year per m²: \(\text{DLI}\times365\) mol, i.e. \(\text{DLI}\times365\times10^{6}\) µmol.
  2. Electrical energy for lighting: divide by photon efficacy (µmol J⁻¹) and capture efficiency: \(\text{DLI}\cdot365\cdot10^{6}/(\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}})\) J. Divide by \(3.6\times10^{6}\) J kWh⁻¹: \(\text{DLI}\cdot365/(3.6\,\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}})\) kWh m⁻² yr⁻¹ — the same step as Eq. L4 in the lighting lab.
  3. Lighting is only a fraction \(f_{\text{light}}\) of the farm's electricity (the rest is mainly cooling and dehumidification, which remove the lamps' heat); divide by \(f_{\text{light}}\) for the total.
  4. Divide by the yield \(Y\) to express it per kilogram. Example: DLI 17, \(\eta_{\text{ph}}=3.0\), \(\eta_{\text{cap}}=0.8\), \(f_{\text{light}}=0.6\), \(Y=80\): \(17\cdot365/(3.6\cdot3.0\cdot0.8)=718\) kWh m⁻² yr⁻¹ for light, 1197 in total, and \(1197/80 = 15\) kWh kg⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

The grid factor must match the time and place of consumption and the accounting method (production mix, consumption mix with trade, residual mix after green certificates, or marginal mix — see the text below). The expression for \(e_{\text{el}}\) is a steady-state, whole-year average; it ignores the extra electricity that very hot or humid outdoor weather causes for cooling.

\(\dfrac{\text{kWh}}{\text{kg}}\cdot\dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{kWh}} = \dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{kg}}\) ✓ ; \(\dfrac{\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{d}^{-1}\cdot\text{d yr}^{-1}}{\mu\text{mol J}^{-1}\cdot\text{kg m}^{-2}\text{yr}^{-1}} = \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\mu\text{mol J}^{-1}\,\text{kg}}\): the factor \(10^{6}/3.6\times10^{6} = 1/3.6\) converts mol/µmol J⁻¹ into kWh ✓

How clean is "the grid"?

Table 1 lists life-cycle emission factors of electricity generation. Sweden's production mix, dominated by hydropower and nuclear with a growing wind share, emitted about 35 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹ in 2024 on a life-cycle basis (Ember, via Our World in Data — the value Lesson 8.4 and the laboratories use; published estimates for Sweden differ by a factor of several depending on whether only direct combustion, full life-cycle emissions or imports are counted, a useful reminder that grid factors are an accounting choice); the EU-27 average was about 211 g; the world average about 471 g; and grids still built on coal, such as Poland's or India's, emit 600–700 g. That is a factor of about 20 between the cleanest and the dirtiest European grids.

But which of these numbers belongs in your LCA? The choice is not a detail. Molin and Martin (2018), assessing basil grown in a vertical farm in Stockholm, used the Nordic mix (100 g kWh⁻¹) and noted that switching to the Swedish mix (≈ 49 g kWh⁻¹) would cut the total footprint by nearly 40 %. Three accounting bases are in common use:

  • Location-based average mix — the average intensity of the grid where the electricity is consumed. Simple and transparent, the standard choice in attributional LCA.
  • Market-based (residual) mix — if the farm buys guarantees of origin for renewable power, it reports their (near-zero) factor, and everybody else gets a slightly dirtier "residual" mix. Whether the purchase actually reduces emissions depends on additionality: did it cause new renewable capacity to be built?
  • Marginal mix — the emissions of the power plants that actually respond to the extra demand. This is the consequential view (§15.1.9), and in an interconnected European grid the short-term marginal plant is frequently fossil even when the average mix is clean.
Worked example 1The same farm on five grids

A vertical farm uses 12 kWh of electricity per kilogram of lettuce. Its non-electricity emissions (seeds, substrate, nutrients, packaging, building and equipment, local delivery) add \(G_0 = 0.25\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹. Calculate its footprint on the Swedish production mix, the Swedish consumption mix, the Nordic mix, the EU average and a coal-heavy grid (650 g kWh⁻¹). Compare with imported field lettuce at ≈ 0.6 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Model. Eq. 15.1.1 with two terms: \(G = e_{\text{el}}\,\text{EF}_{\text{grid}} + G_0\).
  2. Convert units. 35 g kWh⁻¹ = 0.035 kg kWh⁻¹, and so on.
  3. Compute. Sweden (production): \(12\times0.035+0.25 = 0.67\). Sweden (consumption): \(12\times0.049+0.25=0.84\). Nordic: \(12\times0.100+0.25 = 1.45\). EU: \(12\times0.211+0.25 = 2.78\). Coal-heavy: \(12\times0.650+0.25 = 8.05\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹.
  4. Interpret. The electricity term goes from 63 % to 97 % of the total. On the Swedish production mix the farm comes within about 10 % of imported lettuce; on the EU average it is 4–5 times worse; on a coal grid 13 times worse. The farm did not change — only the accounting of its background system did.
  5. Sanity check. Pennisi et al. (2025) report an average of 2.9 kg CO₂ per kg fresh weight across vertical-farm studies — right next to our EU-average value, as expected because most studies use average European grids.
0.67 · 0.84 · 1.45 · 2.78 · 8.05 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹

Heat: the hidden giant of Nordic greenhouses

A glass greenhouse at 56–60° N loses heat through its cover for most of the year. Nilsson and Nimmermark (2013), as summarised by Molin and Martin (2018), modelled a 2 000 m² Swedish greenhouse with different covers and set-points and found that year-round production at 20 °C needs 120–388 kWh of heat per m² and year, about 250 kWh m⁻² yr⁻¹ on average; according to the Swedish Board of Agriculture the sector had already cut its average energy use from 371 to 215 kWh m⁻² (Jordbruksverket, 2013, cited by the same report). Spread over a year-round lettuce yield of a few tens of kilograms per square metre, that is several kWh of heat per kilogram on average — and much more in January, when heat demand peaks and yields are lowest. Whether that heat is climate-relevant depends entirely on its source.

Heating footprint: boiler and heat pump(15.1.3)
\[ \text{(a) boiler: } G_{\text{heat}} = \frac{q_{\text{heat}}}{\eta_{\text{b}}}\,\text{EF}_{\text{fuel}} \qquad \text{(b) heat pump: } G_{\text{heat}} = \frac{q_{\text{heat}}}{\text{COP}}\,\text{EF}_{\text{grid}} \]

with a boiler, the heat needed per kilogram is divided by the boiler efficiency to give the fuel burned, which is multiplied by the fuel's emission factor; with a heat pump, the heat is divided by the coefficient of performance to give the electricity used, which is multiplied by the grid's emission factor.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical
\(q_{\text{heat}}\)useful heat delivered per kg of producekWh kg⁻¹2–15 (seasonal)
\(\eta_{\text{b}}\)boiler efficiency (heat out / fuel energy in)–0.85–0.95
\(\text{EF}_{\text{fuel}}\)life-cycle emission factor per kWh of fuel (net CV)kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹gas 0.236; wood chips 0.019*
COPcoefficient of performance (heat out / electricity in)–3–4.5

*UK DESNZ (2025) conversion factors: natural gas 0.2027 kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ combustion + 0.0335 well-to-tank; wood chips 0.0115 (CH₄ and N₂O only; the 0.35 kg of biogenic CO₂ is reported "outside of scopes", i.e. assumed to be re-absorbed by regrowing forest) + 0.0079 well-to-tank.

Follow the derivation
  1. Energy conservation for the boiler: fuel energy × efficiency = useful heat, so fuel per kg = \(q_{\text{heat}}/\eta_{\text{b}}\).
  2. Each kWh of fuel carries \(\text{EF}_{\text{fuel}}\) kg CO₂e (combustion plus upstream extraction and transport): multiply.
  3. A heat pump moves heat from a cold source (air, ground, waste water) to the greenhouse; by definition \(\text{COP} = Q_{\text{out}}/W_{\text{el}}\), so electricity per kg = \(q_{\text{heat}}/\text{COP}\), which is multiplied by the grid factor (Eq. 15.1.2).
  4. Crossover: the heat pump is better when \(\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}/\text{COP} < \text{EF}_{\text{fuel}}/\eta_{\text{b}}\), i.e. when \(\text{EF}_{\text{grid}} < \text{COP}\cdot\text{EF}_{\text{fuel}}/\eta_{\text{b}}\). With COP = 3.5, gas at 0.236 and \(\eta_b = 0.9\): 0.92 kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ — higher than almost any grid on Earth.
Assumptions and validity

Combined heat and power (CHP) plants, common in Dutch greenhouses, produce heat, electricity and CO₂ for enrichment simultaneously; their emissions must be allocated between these products, and the result depends strongly on the allocation rule. Treating biogenic CO₂ as neutral assumes that the harvested forest regrows within the time horizon of interest — a contested assumption for slow-growing boreal forests. District heat factors vary widely between Swedish networks.

\(\dfrac{\text{kWh}_{\text{heat}}\,\text{kg}^{-1}}{\text{kWh}_{\text{heat}}\,\text{kWh}_{\text{fuel}}^{-1}}\cdot\dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{kWh}_{\text{fuel}}} = \dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{kg}}\) ✓

In January a greenhouse needs 8 kWh of heat per kilogram of lettuce, plus 3.5 kWh kg⁻¹ of electricity for supplementary LEDs, fans and pumps from the Swedish consumption mix (49 g kWh⁻¹). Other inputs, packaging and 600 km of truck transport add 0.25 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹. Compare heating with (a) natural gas (boiler 90 %), (b) wood chips (boiler 85 %) and (c) an air-source heat pump with COP 3.0.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Common terms: electricity \(3.5\times0.049 = 0.17\); other 0.25 → 0.42 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹.
  2. (a) Gas: \(8/0.90\times0.236 = 2.10\) → total 2.52 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹.
  3. (b) Wood chips: \(8/0.85\times0.019 = 0.18\) → total 0.60.

Transport, packaging and food waste

Transport is the most visible part of the food chain, and consumers routinely overestimate it. Its footprint is the transport work — mass times distance, in tonne-kilometres — multiplied by an emission factor that depends above all on the mode.

Transport emissions from tonne-kilometres(15.1.4)
\[ G_{\text{tr}} = \sum_{\text{legs}} m\,d_{\ell}\,\text{EF}_{\text{tkm},\ell} \]

the transport footprint per functional unit is the sum over all transport legs of the mass carried (in tonnes) times the distance of the leg times the emission factor per tonne-kilometre of the vehicle used.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(m\)mass transported per functional unit (product plus its share of packaging)t (1 kg = 10⁻³ t)
\(d_\ell\)distance of transport leg \(\ell\)km
\(\text{EF}_{\text{tkm},\ell}\)emission factor per tonne-kilometre (average load factor and empty returns included)kg CO₂e t⁻¹ km⁻¹
Where do the factors come from?
  1. A truck burns fuel \(F\) (L km⁻¹) that depends on its mass and load. Dividing by the average payload (including empty return trips) gives fuel per t·km; multiplying by the fuel's emission factor gives \(\text{EF}_{\text{tkm}}\).
  2. UK DESNZ (2025) factors for an average-laden articulated truck > 33 t: 0.077 kg CO₂e t⁻¹ km⁻¹ (tank-to-wheel) without refrigeration and 0.089 with refrigeration; adding well-to-tank fuel supply (+0.018/+0.021) gives ≈ 0.095 and ≈ 0.110 well-to-wheel.
  3. For comparison (same source): refrigerated cargo ship 0.013, freight train 0.028, long-haul air freight 0.53 without and 0.90 with the non-CO₂ radiative-forcing uplift.
  4. Lettuce is bulky: a truck full of lettuce "cubes out" before it "weighs out", so the real factor per kg of lettuce can be higher than the average-laden value. Treat these numbers as lower-middle estimates.
\(\text{t}\cdot\text{km}\cdot\dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e}}{\text{t km}} = \text{kg CO}_2\text{e}\) per FU ✓
Worked example 3Almería to Stockholm by truck, ship or plane

The road distance from Almería to Stockholm is about 3 500 km (≈ 2 900 km great-circle). Estimate the transport footprint of 1 kg of lettuce by refrigerated truck (0.110 kg CO₂e t⁻¹ km⁻¹ well-to-wheel). What would it be by air freight over a similar 3 000 km (0.90 with radiative forcing, using the long-haul factor as an optimistic proxy)? And for comparison, a lettuce shipped 9 000 km in a refrigerated container ship (0.016)?

Reveal the reasoning

  2. Truck: \(3.5\times0.110 = 0.385\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹.
  3. Air: \(3.0\times0.90 = 2.7\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ — seven times the truck, and short-haul flights have even higher factors (1.28 in the DESNZ set).
  4. Ship: \(9.0\times0.016 = 0.14\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ — less than the truck despite 2.6 times the distance.

Packaging

Packaging is small per kilogram but not negligible for a light, low-impact product. For potted herbs from a Stockholm vertical farm, the growing medium and pots were among the most important contributors: in the scenario with a plastic pot and garden soil, soil production caused about 40 % of the GHG emissions and lighting another 40 %; switching to paper pots and coir substantially reduced the footprint (Molin & Martin, 2018; Martin & Molin, 2019). Blom et al. (2022) therefore used identical packaging for all systems in their alternative scenario — a reminder that comparative LCAs must not let incidental choices (a clamshell here, a bag there) masquerade as system differences.

Food waste: why the functional unit should be "eaten"

Leafy vegetables are perishable, and a large share of what is produced is never eaten. Globally, around 14 % of food is lost between harvest and retail (FAO, 2019), and households, food service and retail waste a further 19 % of the food available to consumers — about 1.05 billion tonnes in 2022 (UNEP, 2024). If a product's shelf life differs between systems — vertical-farm lettuce is often sold within a day of harvest, or living with roots — then comparing footprints per kilogram sold misses a real difference.

Footprint per kilogram eaten(15.1.5)
\[ G_{\text{eaten}} = \frac{G_{\text{sold}}}{1-w} + \frac{w}{1-w}\,g_{\text{EoL}} \]

the footprint of one kilogram actually eaten equals the footprint per kilogram sold divided by one minus the waste fraction, plus the treatment emissions of the wasted part.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(G_{\text{sold}}\)footprint per kg reaching the shop shelfkg CO₂e kg⁻¹
\(w\)fraction of purchased/sold mass wasted downstream (retail + household)–
\(g_{\text{EoL}}\)end-of-life emissions per kg of waste (landfill, compost, biogas; may be negative with credits)kg CO₂e kg⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. To eat 1 kg when a fraction \(w\) of what is sold is wasted, you must sell \(S\) kg with \((1-w)S = 1\), so \(S = 1/(1-w)\).
  2. Every sold kilogram carries \(G_{\text{sold}}\): upstream footprint \(= G_{\text{sold}}/(1-w)\).
  3. The wasted mass is \(wS = w/(1-w)\) kg, each kilogram causing \(g_{\text{EoL}}\) at treatment.
  4. Example: \(G_{\text{sold}}=0.6\), \(w = 0.25\), \(g_{\text{EoL}} = 0.05\): \(0.6/0.75 + 0.333\times0.05 = 0.80 + 0.02 = 0.82\).
Assumptions and validity

Waste fractions are notoriously uncertain; measured values for bagged salads and whole heads differ, and consumers' behaviour is not a property of the production technology. Use scenario ranges and say so. Losses before the point of sale belong in \(G_{\text{sold}}\) already.

\(\dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e kg}_{\text{sold}}^{-1}}{\text{kg}_{\text{eaten}}\,\text{kg}_{\text{sold}}^{-1}}\) = kg CO₂e per kg eaten ✓ (\(1-w\) has units kg eaten per kg sold)

The break-even grid intensity

Figure 2 suggests a sharper question than "is vertical farming green?": how clean must the electricity be for a vertical farm to beat the alternative? Because the vertical-farm footprint is linear in the grid factor, the answer is a single number that you can derive in three lines — and that is far more useful to a decision-maker than any single footprint.

Break-even grid intensity(15.1.6)
\[ \text{EF}^{*}_{\text{grid}} = \frac{G_{\text{ref}} - G_0}{e_{\text{el}}} \qquad\text{and equivalently}\qquad e^{*}_{\text{el}} = \frac{G_{\text{ref}} - G_0}{\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}} \]

the grid emission factor below which the new system has the lower footprint equals the footprint of the reference product minus the new system's non-electricity footprint, divided by the new system's electricity use per kilogram. Read the other way, for a given grid, it gives the largest electricity use per kilogram that the new system can afford.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\text{EF}^{*}_{\text{grid}}\)break-even grid emission factorkg CO₂e kWh⁻¹
\(G_{\text{ref}}\)footprint of the reference (incumbent) product delivered to the same place, same FUkg CO₂e kg⁻¹
\(G_0\)footprint of the new system excluding its electricitykg CO₂e kg⁻¹
\(e_{\text{el}}\)electricity use of the new systemkWh kg⁻¹
\(e^{*}_{\text{el}}\)break-even electricity intensity for a given gridkWh kg⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Write both footprints with Eq. 15.1.1, separating the electricity term of the new system: \(G_{\text{new}} = e_{\text{el}}\,\text{EF}_{\text{grid}} + G_0\).
  2. The reference product's footprint \(G_{\text{ref}}\) is (to a good approximation) independent of the local grid, because its energy is diesel and foreign electricity.
  3. Break-even means \(G_{\text{new}} = G_{\text{ref}}\): \(e_{\text{el}}\,\text{EF}^{*} + G_0 = G_{\text{ref}}\).
  4. Solve for \(\text{EF}^{*}\): subtract \(G_0\), divide by \(e_{\text{el}}\). The new system wins when \(\text{EF}_{\text{grid}} < \text{EF}^{*}\). If \(G_0 \ge G_{\text{ref}}\), no grid is clean enough: the non-electric inputs alone already exceed the reference.
  5. The sensitivity \(\partial \text{EF}^{*}/\partial e_{\text{el}} = -(G_{\text{ref}}-G_0)/e_{\text{el}}^{2}\) shows why energy efficiency matters doubly: halving \(e_{\text{el}}\) doubles the break-even intensity.
Assumptions and validity

Same functional unit and point of delivery for both systems; linear model; the reference footprint does not depend on the local grid. If the reference is itself a Swedish greenhouse with a heat pump, it also depends on \(\text{EF}_{\text{grid}}\) and the break-even condition becomes \(\text{EF}^{*} = (G_{\text{ref},0}-G_0)/(e_{\text{el}} - e_{\text{el,ref}})\).

\(\dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e kg}^{-1}}{\text{kWh kg}^{-1}} = \text{kg CO}_2\text{e kWh}^{-1}\) ✓
Worked example 4Break-even for a Stockholm vertical farm

Imported field lettuce reaches Stockholm at \(G_{\text{ref}} = 0.23 + 0.385 = 0.62\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ (cultivation, packing and packaging 0.23; transport 0.385 from Worked example 3). A vertical farm has \(G_0 = 0.25\) and \(e_{\text{el}} = 12\) kWh kg⁻¹. (a) Find the break-even grid factor. (b) Which of the accounting bases in Table 1 pass? (c) How far must the farm cut its electricity use to break even on the Nordic mix?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(\text{EF}^{*} = (0.62-0.25)/12 = 0.031\) kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ = 31 g kWh⁻¹.
  2. (b) None — but the Swedish production mix (35 g) misses the 31 g threshold by only about 10 %, while the consumption mix (49 g) and everything dirtier fail clearly. On the production mix a 12 % cut in electricity use would already flip the verdict (\(e^{*}_{\text{el}} = 0.37/0.035 = 10.6\) kWh kg⁻¹), and so would a contract for additional wind power (life-cycle factor of the order of 10 g kWh⁻¹). The verdict therefore hinges on accounting choices and modest efficiency gains that are invisible in most headlines.
  3. (c) With the Nordic mix, \(e^{*}_{\text{el}} = (0.62-0.25)/0.100 = 3.7\) kWh kg⁻¹ — about 70 % less electricity than today. That is beyond what better LEDs alone can deliver (they cut lighting electricity perhaps by a quarter), so it would require higher yields per photon, better heat recovery and cleaner power.
  4. Sanity check. Casey et al. (2022) reached a similar conclusion: a vertical farm could beat most field supply chains (0.48 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ in their best case) only with renewable electricity that is genuinely additional and with credit for land spared.
EF* ≈ 31 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹; on the Nordic mix, e* ≈ 3.7 kWh kg⁻¹

Common misconception

"Local food has a lower carbon footprint because it travels less." Transport by truck or ship is usually a minor part of a vegetable's footprint; how it is grown matters more. Heated greenhouses with fossil fuels and air freight are the two big exceptions that make a product high-carbon — distance by itself rarely does (Stoessel et al., 2012; Poore & Nemecek, 2018).

Attributional, consequential and prospective LCA

Everything so far has been attributional: we attributed to one kilogram of lettuce its share of the average emissions of the systems that produce it. That answers "what is the footprint of this product as it exists?" It does not answer "what happens to global emissions if we build this farm?" — the question a policy-maker or investor usually asks. That is the domain of consequential LCA, which models the changes caused by a decision (Ekvall & Weidema, 2004):

  • Marginal suppliers. Extra electricity demand is met by the plants that respond to the change. In the short run that is often a fossil plant somewhere in the interconnected grid; in the long run, new capacity (increasingly wind and solar) is built.
  • Displacement. A kilogram of vertical-farm lettuce may displace imported lettuce, or may simply add to consumption. Only displaced production counts as avoided.
  • Land use change. If the vertical farm frees farmland, the land can regrow forest and store carbon — but only if someone lets it. Casey et al. (2022) stress that the land-sparing credit is real only if policy ensures that the spared land is used for nature-based solutions such as afforestation.

Prospective LCA assesses an emerging technology at a future, more mature state and compares it with the incumbent at the same future time (Arvidsson et al., 2018). Both the foreground (LED efficacy, yields, automation, heat recovery) and the background (grid decarbonisation, fertiliser production, transport fuels) change. The simplest honest approach is scenario-based: define plausible trajectories and show the footprint along each, rather than a single "future" value.

A simple prospective trajectory(15.1.7)
\[ G(t) = e_{\text{el},0}\,(1-\lambda)^{\,t-t_0}\;\text{EF}_{0}\,(1-k)^{\,t-t_0} + G_0 \]

the footprint in year t equals today's electricity use, reduced by a fractional efficiency gain λ per year, times today's grid factor, reduced by a fractional decarbonisation rate k per year, plus the non-electric footprint.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(t, t_0\)year and base yearyr
\(\lambda\)annual reduction of electricity use per kg (LEDs, yields, HVAC)yr⁻¹ (fraction)
\(k\)annual reduction of the grid factor (decarbonisation)yr⁻¹ (fraction)
\(e_{\text{el},0}, \text{EF}_0\)electricity use and grid factor in the base yearkWh kg⁻¹; kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. A constant fractional improvement per year compounds: after one year \(e = e_0(1-\lambda)\), after \(n\) years \(e_0(1-\lambda)^n\) — the same arithmetic as discounting (Lesson 15.3).
  2. The grid factor declines in the same way with rate \(k\).
  3. The product of the two gives the electricity term; the rates combine approximately additively: \((1-\lambda)(1-k)\approx 1-(\lambda+k)\) for small rates. Example: EU grid 2023→2024 fell about 11 % in a single year (EEA, 2026).
  4. A useful derived quantity is the year in which the new system breaks even: solve \(G(t) = G_{\text{ref}}(t)\).
Assumptions and validity

Constant rates are a caricature; real trajectories have plateaus and jumps (a nuclear plant closing, a new interconnector). The incumbent also improves: electric trucks and cleaner fertiliser reduce \(G_{\text{ref}}\). Prospective LCA must compare both systems in the same future — comparing a future vertical farm with today's field chain is a common and serious error.

kWh kg⁻¹ × (–) × kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ × (–) + kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ = kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ ✓

Consequential thinking also brings in land. Vertical farms yield 60–105 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (Pennisi et al., 2025), open fields a few kilograms (3.3 kg m⁻² in the farms studied by Gargaro et al., 2025). The land they do not use has a carbon opportunity cost: had it not been farmed, it could store carbon in vegetation and soil (Searchinger et al., 2018).

Footprint including the carbon opportunity cost of land(15.1.8)
\[ G_{\text{tot}} = G_{\text{proc}} + \frac{c_{\text{opp}}}{Y} \]

the total footprint equals the process footprint plus the carbon that the land could have stored per year, divided by the yield per square metre per year.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(G_{\text{proc}}\)process (attributional) footprintkg CO₂e kg⁻¹
\(c_{\text{opp}}\)carbon opportunity cost of occupying land: foregone sequestration or storage, annualisedkg CO₂e m⁻² yr⁻¹
\(Y\)yield per land areakg m⁻² yr⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Producing 1 kg occupies \(1/Y\) m²·yr of land.
  2. Each m²·yr of occupation forgoes \(c_{\text{opp}}\) kg CO₂e of storage that natural vegetation could have achieved.
  3. Multiply and add. Example: field lettuce with \(Y = 3.3\) and an assumed \(c_{\text{opp}} = 0.5\) adds \(0.5/3.3 = 0.15\) kg CO₂e kg⁻¹; a vertical farm with \(Y = 90\) (per footprint of a multi-tier building, even more) adds only 0.006.
Assumptions and validity

\(c_{\text{opp}}\) depends on the land's potential natural vegetation and the time horizon; regrowing temperate forest typically sequesters a few tonnes of CO₂ per hectare per year, i.e. of order 0.1–1 kg CO₂ m⁻² yr⁻¹ (treat the slider as a scenario, not a measurement). The credit only materialises if the spared land is actually restored — a consequential, not attributional, claim.

\(\text{kg CO}_2\text{e kg}^{-1} + \dfrac{\text{kg CO}_2\text{e m}^{-2}\text{yr}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-2}\text{yr}^{-1}}\) = kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ ✓

Uncertainty and Monte Carlo analysis

Every input in the model above is uncertain: the kWh per kilogram varies between farms and seasons, the grid factor depends on the accounting method, the truck may run half-empty, the waste fraction is a guess. Three kinds of uncertainty are usually distinguished: parameter uncertainty (the true value of an input is unknown), scenario uncertainty (choices such as the grid basis or the allocation rule) and model uncertainty (the structure itself, e.g. linearity). Monte Carlo analysis handles the first kind and, with care, the second.

The procedure is simple. Give each uncertain parameter a probability distribution; draw one value from each; compute the footprints of both systems with the same draw for any shared parameter; record which system wins; repeat thousands of times. The fraction of runs in which system A beats system B estimates the probability that A really is better — a far more honest statement than two point values.

Monte Carlo probability of superiority and its standard error(15.1.9)
\[ \hat p = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\left[G_A^{(j)} < G_B^{(j)}\right], \qquad \text{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p\,(1-\hat p)}{N}} \]

the estimated probability that A has a lower footprint than B is the fraction of the N paired simulation runs in which A's footprint was lower; its standard error is the square root of p times one minus p divided by N.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(N\)number of Monte Carlo runs–
\(G_A^{(j)}, G_B^{(j)}\)footprints of A and B in run \(j\), computed from the same random draw of shared parameterskg CO₂e FU⁻¹
\(\mathbf{1}[\cdot]\)indicator: 1 if the condition is true, else 0–
\(\hat p\)estimated probability that A is better–
Follow the derivation
  1. Each run is a Bernoulli trial: "A wins" with unknown probability \(p\).
  2. The number of wins in \(N\) independent runs is binomial with mean \(Np\) and variance \(Np(1-p)\).
  3. Dividing by \(N\): \(\hat p\) has mean \(p\) and variance \(p(1-p)/N\), so \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\).
  4. Invert to plan the number of runs: \(N = p(1-p)/\text{SE}^2\). For \(p\approx0.5\) and a target SE of 0.01 you need 2 500 runs; for SE = 0.005, 10 000 (see Heijungs, 2020, for a fuller treatment of run numbers in comparative LCA).
  5. Why paired runs? If A and B share an uncertain parameter (the same fertiliser factor, the same packaging), drawing it independently for each would add spurious noise to the difference and blur a real ranking.
Assumptions and validity

The result is only as good as the input distributions; ranges picked "to look reasonable" produce a precise-looking but arbitrary \(\hat p\). Scenario choices (average vs marginal grid) are better shown as separate Monte Carlo experiments than blended into one distribution.

\(\hat p\) and SE are dimensionless probabilities; the calculator reports SE in percentage points ✓
Worked example 5Is 68 % "better"?

A student runs 500 paired Monte Carlo iterations and finds that the vertical farm has the lower footprint in 340 of them. (a) Estimate \(\hat p\) and its standard error. (b) Can she claim that the vertical farm is better "with at least 60 % probability"? (c) How many runs would reduce the SE to 1 percentage point?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(\hat p = 340/500 = 0.68\); \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.68\times0.32/500} = 0.0209\), i.e. 2.1 percentage points.
  2. (b) An approximate 95 % interval is \(0.68\pm1.96\times0.021 = [0.64, 0.72]\): yes, the Monte Carlo sampling error is small enough. But the claim is conditional on the input distributions; a different grid scenario could move \(\hat p\) far more than 4 points.
  3. (c) \(N = 0.68\times0.32/0.01^2 = 2176\) runs.
  4. Interpretation. "The vertical farm is better in about two out of three plausible states of the world" is an honest summary — and it tells a decision-maker that the question is not settled.
p̂ = 0.68 ± 0.021; about 2 200 runs for SE = 0.01

Rebound effects and system consequences

An LCA per kilogram assumes that the new product replaces an old one kilogram for kilogram. Real economies respond. A rebound effect is any behavioural or market response that eats into the savings an efficiency gain was supposed to deliver:

  • Direct rebound: cheaper or more convenient lettuce (year-round, local, longer shelf life) may increase lettuce consumption rather than displacing imports one-to-one.
  • Indirect rebound: money saved by a cheaper product is spent on something else with its own footprint.
  • Market-level effects: reduced demand for imports lowers their price elsewhere, raising consumption there; a new large electricity consumer can delay the closure of a fossil plant or, conversely, underwrite new renewable capacity.
  • Land: spared farmland may be restored — or used for another crop, cancelling the land credit of Eq. 15.1.8.

The quantitative treatment of rebound (with price elasticities) is in Lesson 15.3, because the same mechanisms decide who can afford new foods. The practical lesson for LCA is modest but important: state the replacement assumption explicitly ("each kg of VF lettuce displaces 1 kg of imported lettuce"), and test what happens if it is only 0.5 kg.

Frontier 2024–2026

Newer LCAs of controlled-environment agriculture increasingly use time-resolved electricity factors (hourly marginal emissions), because a farm that runs its lights at night — when demand and prices are low and, in many grids, wind supplies a larger share — has a different footprint from one that lights by day. Combined with flexible "demand response" operation, this turns the farm from a passive consumer into a grid resource. Reviews also call for more social and economic indicators alongside LCA (Dziumla et al., 2025) — the subject of Lesson 15.4.

A checklist for reading any food-technology LCA

(1) What is the functional unit — kg sold, kg eaten, kg dry matter, kg protein? (2) Where is the system boundary — cradle to farm gate, to shop, to plate? (3) Which electricity factor, and on what accounting basis? (4) What is the reference system, delivered to the same place? (5) Which allocation for co-products? (6) Is the result attributional or consequential, and is it prospective? (7) Is there a contribution, sensitivity and uncertainty analysis? (8) Who funded the study, and are the data public?

Key terms

Functional unit
The quantified function that all compared systems deliver (e.g., 1 kg of lettuce eaten in Stockholm in January); all flows are scaled to it.
System boundary
The set of processes included in the assessment (e.g., cradle-to-gate, cradle-to-consumer, cradle-to-grave).
Emission factor
Life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions per unit of an activity (per kWh, per t·km, per kg of input), in kg CO₂e.
Grid emission intensity
GHG emissions per kWh of electricity; can be a production, consumption, residual (market-based) or marginal mix.
Break-even grid intensity
The grid emission factor below which an electricity-intensive system has a lower footprint than its reference: (G_ref − G₀)/e_el.
Contribution analysis
Decomposing a footprint into its terms to identify the processes that dominate it.
Tonne-kilometre (t·km)
Unit of transport work: one tonne moved one kilometre.
Attributional LCA
LCA that attributes average emissions of existing systems to a product; answers "what is its footprint?".
Consequential LCA
LCA that models the changes caused by a decision, using marginal suppliers, displacement and induced effects.
Prospective LCA
LCA of an emerging technology at a future, more mature state, compared with the incumbent at the same future time.
Carbon opportunity cost
Carbon that land could have stored in natural vegetation and soil had it not been used for production.
Monte Carlo analysis
Propagating input uncertainty by repeated random sampling; in comparative LCA, estimating the probability that one option is better.
Rebound effect
A behavioural or market response that offsets part of the savings expected from an efficiency improvement.
