Principles of precision farming

A 40-hectare field of wheat is not one field. Walk across it and you cross sandy ridges and clay hollows, wet corners and compacted headlands; at harvest the yield can easily differ by a factor of two between its best and worst parts. Conventional farming treats the field as if it were uniform — one seed rate, one fertiliser rate, one spray dose. Precision farming asks a simple, powerful question: what if every square metre got exactly what it needs? This lesson builds the toolkit that makes that question answerable — satellite positioning to centimetres, sensors on combines, statistics that turn scattered soil samples into maps, and the algorithms that translate maps into machine instructions.

  • Design a simple variable-rate prescription, evaluate it with a partial budget and discuss the evidence on benefits and the barriers to adoption, including Swedish examples (LO1, LO7).

What precision agriculture is — and is not

Farmers have always known that fields are variable. Before tractors, a farmer who worked a few hectares by hand could adjust manure, seed and weeding to each patch of soil almost intuitively. Mechanisation changed that: a 24-metre sprayer or a 9-metre combine moving at 8 km h⁻¹ cannot "see" a patch of poor soil, and the logic of large machines pushed farming towards uniform management of whole fields. Precision agriculture is, in a sense, the attempt to regain the attention to detail of the small farmer while keeping the productivity of large machines — using sensors, positioning and computation instead of eyes and memory.

Because the term has been used loosely for decades (one survey of the literature counted more than twenty competing definitions), the International Society of Precision Agriculture (ISPA) adopted an official definition in 2019:

"Precision Agriculture is a management strategy that gathers, processes and analyzes temporal, spatial and individual data and combines it with other information to support management decisions according to estimated variability for improved resource use efficiency, productivity, quality, profitability and sustainability of agricultural production." — International Society of Precision Agriculture (2019)

Four features of this definition deserve attention. First, precision agriculture is a management strategy, not a gadget: a GPS receiver on a tractor is a tool, not precision agriculture. Second, it rests on data that are spatial (where?), temporal (when?) and individual (which plant or animal?). Third, the data are used to support decisions — the loop is only closed when something is done differently. Fourth, the goals are explicitly multiple: resource-use efficiency, productivity, quality, profitability and sustainability. A popular shorthand is the "4 Rs" borrowed from nutrient stewardship: the right input, at the right place, at the right time, in the right amount — the module's tagline.

Common misconception

"Precision agriculture means applying less." Not necessarily. Precision agriculture means applying differently: less where the extra input would be wasted, and sometimes more where it pays. A variable-rate nitrogen plan may use exactly the same total amount of fertiliser as a uniform plan and still raise yield and reduce leaching, simply by moving nitrogen from where the crop cannot use it to where it can.

A short history

The ingredients of precision agriculture — soil maps, fertiliser recommendations, positioning — each have long histories, but they came together in the late 1980s and 1990s (Stafford, 2000; Mulla & Khosla, 2015). The timeline below highlights the milestones most relevant to this lesson.

Mid-1980s

First field experiments with variable lime and fertiliser rates based on grid soil sampling in the US Midwest.

1989

Searcy and colleagues publish a method to map spatially variable grain yield during combining (Searcy et al., 1989).

Early 1990s

Commercial combine yield monitors appear, based on an impact-plate mass-flow sensor at the top of the clean-grain elevator; yield maps reveal how large within-field variability really is.

1995

The US Global Positioning System (GPS) reaches full operational capability.

2 May 2000

"Selective Availability" — deliberate degradation of civilian GPS signals, with typical errors of about 50 m — is switched off. Metre-level positioning becomes available to everyone.

2000s

RTK-based auto-steering spreads rapidly; GNSS guidance becomes one of the fastest-adopted technologies in agricultural history (Lowenberg-DeBoer & Erickson, 2019). Europe's EGNOS augmentation system officially starts operating in 2009.

2010

Gebbers and Adamchuk review precision agriculture as a contribution to food security in Science.

2015

Sentinel-2A is launched, providing free 10 m multispectral images every few days; in Sweden, the CropSAT decision-support service starts offering satellite-based variable-rate nitrogen maps free of charge (Söderström et al., 2017).

2019

ISPA adopts its official definition of precision agriculture.

2020s

Machine learning, cheap drones and autonomous field robots extend precision agriculture from "zones" towards individual plants (see Lesson 11.4).

Spatial and temporal variability

Precision agriculture is only worthwhile if the field is variable enough, in a way that is predictable enough, for different management to make a difference. It is useful to separate three kinds of variation.

  • Spatial variability: differences from place to place at the same time — soil texture, organic matter, depth to subsoil, drainage, pH, nutrient status, weed patches, topography. Much of it is inherited from geology and landscape history and changes little from year to year.
  • Temporal variability: differences from time to time at the same place — weather, the crop's growth stage, mineralisation of soil nitrogen, pest outbreaks. It is why a map from last year is not a prescription for this year.
  • Individual variability: differences between individual plants or animals — the level at which robots that treat single weeds, and milking robots that know each cow, operate.

The simplest measure of how variable a property is across a field is its coefficient of variation, which expresses the spread relative to the mean:

Coefficient of variation(11.1.1)
\[ \text{CV} = \frac{s}{\bar z}\times 100\,\%, \qquad s=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(z_i-\bar z\right)^2},\qquad \bar z=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i \]

the coefficient of variation is the sample standard deviation of the observations divided by their mean, expressed as a percentage. It tells you how large the typical deviation from the field average is, relative to that average.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(z_i\)observation \(i\) (e.g. yield in a 10 m × 10 m cell)unit of the property (t ha⁻¹)
\(\bar z\)mean of all \(n\) observationssame as \(z\)
\(s\)sample standard deviationsame as \(z\)
CVcoefficient of variation% (dimensionless)
Why divide by the mean?

A standard deviation of 1 t ha⁻¹ means something very different in a 2 t ha⁻¹ oat crop in a dry year (enormous variation) and in a 10 t ha⁻¹ winter wheat crop (modest variation). Dividing by the mean makes the spread dimensionless and comparable across crops, years and properties. The \(n-1\) in the standard deviation (Bessel's correction) makes \(s^2\) an unbiased estimate of the population variance; with thousands of yield-monitor points the difference from \(n\) is negligible.

Assumptions and validity

The CV says nothing about where the variation is. A field whose high and low yields form two large coherent zones and a field where they are scattered randomly metre by metre can have the same CV — but only the first can be managed site-specifically with ordinary machines. Spatial structure is measured with the semivariogram (Eq. 11.1.9). The CV is meaningless for properties without a true zero (e.g. temperature in °C) or with a mean near zero.

\([s]/[\bar z]\) = (t ha⁻¹)/(t ha⁻¹) = dimensionless, × 100 → % ✓

Yield maps commonly reveal within-field differences of several tonnes per hectare, and soil properties such as clay content or available phosphorus often vary even more strongly (Pierce & Nowak, 1999). But a large CV is not enough. Whelan and McBratney (2000) framed the key question as a "null hypothesis" for precision agriculture: given the large temporal variation relative to spatial variation, the optimal risk-averse strategy is uniform management. Site-specific management is justified only when the spatial pattern is (i) large relative to measurement error, (ii) reasonably stable between years or predictable from sensors in the current season, and (iii) connected to a management action whose response differs between zones. Blackmore et al. (2003) analysed six years of yield maps from one English field and found that year-to-year variation at a given point was often as large as the spatial variation — a sobering reminder that "high-yielding zones" are not always high-yielding.

Key idea

Precision agriculture pays when variability is large, spatially structured, temporally stable (or measurable in season) and manageable. Much of this lesson is about measuring these four properties: positioning to know where, sensors to know how much, geostatistics to know how structured, and clustering to turn structure into manageable zones.

Knowing where you are: GNSS positioning

Every precision-agriculture datum and action is tied to a position, so positioning accuracy sets the finest scale at which a field can be managed. Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) — the American GPS, the European Galileo, the Russian GLONASS and the Chinese BeiDou — all work on the same principle: satellites carrying atomic clocks broadcast signals that say, in effect, "I am satellite i, this is my position, and this signal left me at time \(t_i\)". The receiver notes the arrival time and multiplies the travel time by the speed of light to get a distance.

Trilateration with an imperfect clock

If the receiver's clock were perfect, three distances to three satellites of known position would define three spheres whose intersection is the receiver's position: trilateration. But a receiver has a cheap quartz clock, and a timing error of only one microsecond corresponds to a distance error of about 300 m. The receiver therefore measures a pseudorange — a range contaminated by its own clock error, which is the same for all satellites at a given instant:

GNSS pseudorange(11.1.2)
\[ \rho_i = \sqrt{(x-x_i)^2+(y-y_i)^2+(z-z_i)^2}\; +\; c\,\delta t_r\; +\; \varepsilon_i ,\qquad i=1,\dots,n \]

the pseudorange measured to satellite \(i\) equals the true geometric distance between the receiver and the satellite, plus the speed of light times the receiver clock error, plus all the other errors affecting that particular signal.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\rho_i\)pseudorange to satellite \(i\) = (arrival time − transmit time) × \(c\)m
\(x, y, z\)unknown receiver coordinates (Earth-centred)m
\(x_i, y_i, z_i\)satellite coordinates, broadcast in the navigation messagem
\(c\)speed of light, 2.998 × 10⁸m s⁻¹
\(\delta t_r\)receiver clock error (unknown)s
\(\varepsilon_i\)other errors: ionosphere, troposphere, satellite orbit and clock, multipath, receiver noisem
Follow the reasoning: why four satellites?
  1. The unknowns are the three coordinates \(x, y, z\) and the clock error \(\delta t_r\) — four unknowns in total.
  2. Each satellite gives one equation of the form (11.1.2). Four independent equations are needed to solve for four unknowns, so a receiver needs at least four satellites in view.
  3. The equations are non-linear (square roots). Receivers linearise them around an approximate position \(\mathbf{p}_0\): \(\rho_i \approx \lVert \mathbf{p}_0-\mathbf{s}_i\rVert - \mathbf{u}_i\cdot\Delta\mathbf{p} + b\), where \(\mathbf{u}_i\) is the unit vector from the receiver towards satellite \(i\) and \(b=c\,\delta t_r\).
  4. Stacking the equations gives \(\Delta\boldsymbol\rho = H\,\Delta\mathbf{x}\) with rows \(H_i = [-u_{ix}, -u_{iy}, -u_{iz}, 1]\). With more than four satellites the system is over-determined and is solved by least squares: \(\Delta\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (H^{\mathsf T}H)^{-1}H^{\mathsf T}\Delta\boldsymbol\rho\), iterated until the correction is negligible.
  5. The matrix \((H^{\mathsf T}H)^{-1}\) depends only on the satellite geometry. Its diagonal elements give the dilution of precision (DOP): the horizontal position error is approximately \(\sigma_{\text{pos}} \approx \text{HDOP}\times\sigma_{\rho}\), where \(\sigma_\rho\) is the pseudorange error. Satellites spread across the sky give a small DOP; satellites bunched in one direction give a large one.
Assumptions and validity

The clock error is assumed identical for all satellites observed at the same instant (true: it is the receiver's clock). Satellite positions and clock offsets are taken from the broadcast message and are themselves slightly wrong; the atmospheric delays differ between satellites because their signals travel through different parts of the atmosphere. These residual errors are lumped into \(\varepsilon_i\).

m + (m s⁻¹ × s) + m = m ✓ — every term is a distance.

The pseudorange errors \(\varepsilon_i\) come from several physical sources (Misra & Enge, 2006). Differential GNSS (DGPS) uses a reference station at a surveyed position: because it knows where it is, it can compute the error in each pseudorange and broadcast corrections. Satellite-based augmentation systems such as Europe's EGNOS broadcast wide-area corrections from geostationary satellites for free; in practice they give metre-level horizontal accuracy. Real-time kinematic (RTK) positioning goes much further by using the phase of the carrier wave itself (wavelength about 19 cm for GPS L1) rather than the coded signal. Once the receiver has resolved the whole number of carrier cycles between itself and each satellite (the "integer ambiguity"), its position relative to the base station is known to millimetres to centimetres.

The coefficients are receiver-specific (values of the order of 8 mm + 1 ppm are typical of survey-grade receivers). Beyond some tens of kilometres, resolving the integer ambiguities becomes unreliable. Network RTK solves this: a network of permanent reference stations models the atmospheric errors over a whole region and generates corrections for a "virtual reference station" close to the user. In Sweden, Lantmäteriet's SWEPOS network of permanent reference stations provides such a network RTK service at centimetre level over the country.

mm + (mm km⁻¹ × km) = mm ✓

Why does a farmer need centimetres? Because the precision required depends on the operation. Spreading fertiliser with a 36 m spinning-disc spreader tolerates decimetre errors. Planting row crops and then hoeing between those rows at 8 km h⁻¹ with a few centimetres of clearance to the plants does not; nor does returning to the same wheel tracks every year in controlled-traffic farming.

Guidance, auto-steering and controlled traffic

The first large-scale economic win of GNSS in agriculture was not maps but guidance. Without guidance, a driver steering a 24 m sprayer across a bare or uniformly green field must judge where the previous pass ended; to avoid leaving untreated strips (skips) drivers deliberately overlap. With light-bar guidance, the driver steers by an indicator; with auto-steer the machine steers itself along parallel lines computed from GNSS, and with RTK the pass-to-pass error falls to a few centimetres. Lowenberg-DeBoer and Erickson (2019) conclude that GNSS guidance and the related automatic section control of sprayer booms and planter rows have been adopted about as fast as any major agricultural technology in history.

The economics follow from simple geometry. Each pass covers a strip of width \(w\) but advances the treated area by only \(s\), so the product applied per hectare of field is larger than the intended rate by the factor \(w/s\):

Excess application caused by pass-to-pass overlap(11.1.4)
\[ E_{\text{ov}} = \frac{w}{s}-1 = \frac{o}{w-o},\qquad \text{saving from guidance } = 1-\frac{1+E_{\text{ov,guided}}}{1+E_{\text{ov,manual}}} \]

the fractional excess of product applied equals the working width divided by the actual pass spacing, minus one — equivalently the overlap divided by the pass spacing. The saving from guidance is the relative reduction of total product between the manual and the guided case.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(w\)effective working width of the implementm
\(s\)actual spacing between adjacent pass centre-linesm
\(o = w - s\)mean overlap between adjacent passesm
\(E_{\text{ov}}\)excess application relative to the target rate– (× 100 → %)
Follow the derivation
  1. Consider a field of width \(W\) (across the passes) and length \(L\). With spacing \(s\) you need \(N = W/s\) passes.
  2. Each pass treats an area \(wL\) at the target rate \(R\) (kg ha⁻¹), so the product used is \(Q = N\,w\,L\,R = (W/s)\,w\,L\,R\).
  3. The product actually needed is \(Q_0 = W\,L\,R\). The ratio is \(Q/Q_0 = w/s\), and the excess is \(w/s - 1\).
  4. Substituting \(s = w - o\): \(E_{\text{ov}} = w/(w-o) - 1 = o/(w-o)\).
  5. Comparing two steering methods with the same target rate: saving \(= (Q_{\text{man}} - Q_{\text{guid}})/Q_{\text{man}} = 1 - (w/s_{\text{guid}})/(w/s_{\text{man}})\).
Assumptions and validity

The formula treats overlap as a constant mean width over straight parallel passes. Real fields have irregular boundaries, headlands and point rows where wedges are sprayed twice; there automatic section control (switching boom sections or planter rows off over already-treated ground) saves additional product that the formula does not capture. Overlap also has agronomic costs — double doses of herbicide can damage crops and double nitrogen raises lodging and leaching risk — and skips have costs too (untreated weed strips).

Controlled-traffic farming

Modern tractors, combines and grain carts weigh from 10 to more than 30 tonnes, and every pass compacts the soil beneath the tyres, reducing pore space, infiltration and root growth. When machines drive at random, the tracks of successive operations cover most of the field: estimates suggest that around three-quarters of the surface can be run over within a single season. Controlled-traffic farming (CTF) uses RTK guidance to confine all heavy wheels to the same permanent traffic lanes, year after year, so that the cropped beds between them are never compacted (Tullberg et al., 2007). All machines are matched to a common working width (a "module", e.g. 9 or 12 m) and a common track gauge.

Trafficked fraction of a controlled-traffic system(11.1.5)
\[ f_{\text{track}} = \frac{n_t\,b}{M} \]

the fraction of the field surface that is permanently trafficked equals the number of wheel tracks per module, times the width of each track, divided by the module width.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(f_{\text{track}}\)fraction of the area under permanent traffic lanes–
\(n_t\)number of wheel tracks per module width (usually 2 for one pair of lanes)–
\(b\)width of one track (tyre width)m
\(M\)module (implement) width = spacing between lane pairsm
Assumptions and validity

All machines use the same track gauge and exact multiples of the module width, and RTK keeps them within the lanes. In practice some machines (e.g. combines with wide headers or grain carts) do not match, adding a few extra lanes; headland turns are also trafficked. The formula is therefore a lower bound.

(– × m)/m = dimensionless ✓

With a 12 m module and 0.6 m tyres, only 10 % of the field is trafficked, compared with most of the field under random traffic. Well-implemented CTF systems keep the trafficked area at around 15 % once headlands and mismatched machines are included (Tullberg et al., 2007). The uncompacted beds need less energy for tillage, infiltrate rain better and are often easier to direct-drill.

Yield mapping: the combine as a sensor

The yield map is the report card of a season: it integrates soil, weather, management and their interactions. A yield monitor on a combine harvester combines four measurements every second: the flow of clean grain, the grain moisture, the ground speed and the GNSS position, plus the cutting width set by the operator.

Yield from grain mass flow(11.1.6)
\[ Y_{\text{wet}} = \frac{q}{v\,w}\times 10 \qquad\left[\text{t ha}^{-1}\right] \]

the wet-grain yield equals the grain mass flow divided by the area harvested per second (speed times cutting width); the factor 10 converts kilograms per square metre to tonnes per hectare.

Cleaning yield data

Raw yield-monitor data contain systematic errors that can produce striking but false patterns. Arslan and Colvin (2002) and Sudduth and Drummond (2007) catalogue the main ones:

  • Flow delay. Grain takes several seconds to travel from the cutter bar through threshing and cleaning to the sensor. Unless the flow record is shifted back by this delay, each yield value is assigned to a position several metres (at 1.6 m s⁻¹, ten seconds is 16 m) behind where it was cut — and alternate passes in opposite directions produce a characteristic zig-zag pattern.
  • Filling and emptying. At the start of a pass the machine is filling up and the flow ramps up; at the end it ramps down. These transients must be removed.
  • Wrong cutting width. Narrow finishing passes or point rows with the full width set in the monitor give falsely low yields.
  • Speed changes and stops. Sudden decelerations change the flow–area relationship; points recorded while stationary are meaningless.
  • Position errors and outliers. GNSS jumps, overlapping passes, and physically impossible values (e.g. above twice the field mean).

Software filters (such as the freely available Yield Editor of Sudduth & Drummond, 2007) apply flow-delay shifts, remove start/end-of-pass points, and filter by speed, width and statistical thresholds. Depending on the field and the operator, a considerable share of the raw points may be removed. Clean point data are then interpolated to a regular grid — using the methods in the next section.

From samples to maps: spatial interpolation

Yield monitors produce thousands of points per hectare, but soil analyses are expensive: a laboratory analysis of pH, phosphorus, potassium and texture costs tens of euros per sample. Soil is therefore sampled on a grid (for example one composite sample, made of several cores, per hectare) or in targeted locations chosen from other maps (e.g. electrical conductivity or yield zones). To make a map we must estimate the value at unsampled locations from the values at sampled ones. Every interpolation method rests on the same intuition, often called Tobler's first law of geography: near things are more related than distant things.

Inverse-distance weighting

The simplest method that honours this intuition is inverse-distance weighting (IDW), formalised by Shepard (1968). The estimate at an unsampled point is a weighted average of the surrounding samples, with weights that decrease with distance.

Inverse-distance weighting(11.1.8)
\[ \hat z(\mathbf{x}_0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i\,z(\mathbf{x}_i),\qquad \lambda_i=\frac{d_{i0}^{-p}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} d_{j0}^{-p}},\qquad d_{i0}=\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_0\rVert \]

the estimated value at the target location is a weighted average of the sample values; each sample's weight is its distance to the target raised to the power minus p, divided by the sum of these quantities over all samples, so that the weights add up to one.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\hat z(\mathbf{x}_0)\)estimate at the target location \(\mathbf{x}_0\)unit of the property
\(z(\mathbf{x}_i)\)measured value at sample location \(\mathbf{x}_i\)unit of the property
\(\lambda_i\)weight of sample \(i\); \(\sum\lambda_i = 1\)–
\(d_{i0}\)distance from sample \(i\) to the targetm
\(p\)power parameter (usually 1–3; 2 is the default)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Requirement 1 — a weighted average. We want \(\hat z = \sum \lambda_i z_i\) with \(\sum\lambda_i = 1\), so that a field with a constant value is reproduced exactly (the estimator is unbiased for a constant mean).
  2. Requirement 2 — closer counts more. Let the raw weight be a decreasing function of distance, \(w_i = d_{i0}^{-p}\) with \(p>0\).
  3. Normalise. Divide each raw weight by the sum of all raw weights: \(\lambda_i = w_i/\sum_j w_j\). Now the weights sum to one automatically.
  4. Exact interpolator. As \(\mathbf{x}_0\to\mathbf{x}_k\), \(d_{k0}\to0\) and \(w_k\to\infty\), so \(\lambda_k\to1\) and \(\hat z\to z_k\): the surface passes through the data (in code, return \(z_k\) when \(d_{k0}=0\)).
  5. Role of \(p\). Large \(p\) makes the nearest sample dominate (a map of "tiles" around each sample, approaching nearest-neighbour interpolation); small \(p\) spreads influence widely and smooths the map towards the field mean.
Assumptions and validity

IDW is purely geometric: it does not use the data to decide how quickly similarity decays with distance, it cannot say how uncertain the estimate is, and it produces "bull's-eyes" around samples. Estimates are always between the minimum and maximum of the data (no extrapolation of trends). Clustered samples get too much total weight, because IDW does not account for redundancy between nearby samples. Kriging addresses all of these points.

\(\lambda_i = \text{m}^{-p}/\text{m}^{-p}\) is dimensionless, so \(\hat z\) has the unit of \(z\) ✓
Worked example 3Estimating soil pH between three samples

Three soil samples surround an unsampled point: pH 6.2 at 20 m, pH 5.4 at 35 m and pH 7.0 at 50 m. Estimate the pH at the point by IDW with \(p = 2\) and with \(p = 1\).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Raw weights, \(p=2\): \(20^{-2}=0.002500\), \(35^{-2}=0.000816\), \(50^{-2}=0.000400\); sum \(=0.003716\) m⁻².
  2. Normalised weights: \(\lambda = 0.6727,\ 0.2197,\ 0.1076\) (check: sum = 1.000).
  3. Estimate: \(0.6727\times6.2+0.2197\times5.4+0.1076\times7.0 = 4.171+1.186+0.753 = 6.11\).
  4. With \(p=1\): raw weights \(0.0500, 0.0286, 0.0200\), normalised \(0.507, 0.290, 0.203\); estimate \(=3.145+1.565+1.420=6.13\).
  5. Interpretation. With \(p=2\) the nearest sample carries two-thirds of the weight; with \(p=1\) the more distant samples matter more and the estimate moves towards their mean. Both values are between the data minimum (5.4) and maximum (7.0), as IDW guarantees.
pH ≈ 6.11 (p = 2); ≈ 6.13 (p = 1)

Kriging and the semivariogram

Kriging, developed in mining geostatistics and brought to soil science in the 1980s, replaces IDW's arbitrary distance weights with weights derived from the data's own spatial structure (Webster & Oliver, 2007; Oliver & Webster, 2014). That structure is summarised by the semivariogram, which measures how dissimilar two observations are, on average, as a function of the distance (the lag) that separates them:

Experimental semivariogram and the spherical model(11.1.9)
\[ \hat\gamma(h)=\frac{1}{2N(h)}\sum_{k=1}^{N(h)}\left[z(\mathbf{x}_k)-z(\mathbf{x}_k+\mathbf{h})\right]^2,\qquad \gamma_{\text{sph}}(h)=\begin{cases} c_0+c_1\left[\dfrac{3h}{2a}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{h}{a}\right)^{3}\right], & 0<h\le a\\[4pt] c_0+c_1, & h>a\end{cases} \]

the experimental semivariance at lag h is half the average squared difference between all pairs of observations separated by approximately h. The spherical model rises from the nugget variance at very short distances to the sill at the range, beyond which observations are no longer spatially correlated.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(h\)lag distance between two locationsm
\(N(h)\)number of pairs of observations in the lag class around \(h\)–
\(\hat\gamma(h)\)experimental semivariance(unit of \(z\))²
\(c_0\)nugget: variance at (near) zero distance = measurement error + variation at scales shorter than the sampling interval(unit of \(z\))²
\(c_0+c_1\)sill: total variance of the field(unit of \(z\))²
\(a\)range: distance beyond which observations are uncorrelatedm
Follow the reasoning
  1. If values are spatially structured, pairs of points close together have similar values, so their squared differences are small; pairs far apart differ as much as any two random points in the field.
  2. Grouping all pairs by separation distance and averaging their squared differences gives \(2\hat\gamma(h)\); the "semi" (half) makes \(\gamma\) equal to the variance at large lags: if \(z_1, z_2\) are independent with variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\tfrac12\text{E}[(z_1-z_2)^2] = \tfrac12(\sigma^2+\sigma^2) = \sigma^2\).
  3. The experimental points are noisy, so a smooth, mathematically valid ("conditionally negative definite") model is fitted. The spherical model is popular because it reaches its sill at a finite range \(a\).
  4. Kriging then chooses the weights \(\lambda_i\) that make the estimate unbiased (\(\sum\lambda_i=1\)) and minimise the estimation variance \(\text{E}[(\hat z_0-z_0)^2]\). Setting derivatives to zero with a Lagrange multiplier \(\mu\) gives the ordinary-kriging system \(\sum_j \lambda_j\gamma(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)+\mu=\gamma(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_0)\) for every sample \(i\) — a small set of linear equations solved for each target point.
  5. A by-product is the kriging variance: a map of the uncertainty of the interpolated map, which IDW cannot give.
What the semivariogram tells a farmer

The range tells you how far apart samples can be and still inform each other: a common rule of thumb is to sample at less than half the range. A large nugget-to-sill ratio (close to 1) means most variation is at scales finer than the sampling grid or is measurement noise — a field that cannot usefully be mapped at that sampling density (Oliver & Webster, 2014).

γ has units of \((z)^2\), e.g. (t ha⁻¹)²; \(h/a\) is dimensionless ✓

Management zones

Most machines cannot change rate every metre, and most farmers cannot manage thousands of different "treatments" in their heads. The pragmatic middle ground is the management zone: a sub-region of a field that is relatively homogeneous in yield-limiting factors, so that a single rate of a given input is appropriate (Fridgen et al., 2004). Zones are usually delineated by clustering several stable data layers — multi-year yield maps, apparent electrical conductivity (a proxy for clay and water-holding capacity), elevation, soil organic matter, satellite vegetation indices — so that points within a zone are as similar as possible and points in different zones as different as possible.

k-means clustering objective(11.1.10)
\[ \min_{C_1,\dots,C_k}\; J=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\;\sum_{\mathbf{z}_i\in C_j}\lVert \mathbf{z}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2,\qquad \boldsymbol\mu_j=\frac{1}{|C_j|}\sum_{\mathbf{z}_i\in C_j}\mathbf{z}_i \]

choose k groups of map cells so that the total squared distance between every cell's data vector and the mean vector of its group is as small as possible.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(k\)number of zones, chosen by the analyst (typically 2–5)–
\(\mathbf{z}_i\)vector of (standardised) data layers in map cell \(i\)– (z-scores)
\(C_j\)set of cells assigned to zone \(j\)–
\(\boldsymbol\mu_j\)centroid (mean vector) of zone \(j\)–
\(J\)within-zone sum of squares (to be minimised)–
Follow the algorithm (Lloyd's iterations)
  1. Standardise each layer (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) so that layers measured in different units carry equal weight.
  2. Initialise \(k\) centroids, e.g. at quantiles of the data or at random cells.
  3. Assignment step: assign every cell to its nearest centroid. For fixed centroids this minimises \(J\) over the assignments.
  4. Update step: recompute each centroid as the mean of its cells. For fixed assignments the mean minimises the sum of squared distances (set \(\partial J/\partial\boldsymbol\mu_j = -2\sum(\mathbf{z}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_j)=0\)).
  5. Repeat steps 3–4 until assignments no longer change. \(J\) never increases, so the algorithm converges — but possibly to a local minimum, so it is run from several starts.
  6. Evaluate the variance reduction \(1 - J/\text{SST}\), where SST is the total sum of squares: the fraction of the field's variance "explained" by the zones. Fuzzy k-means (as in Fridgen et al., 2004) gives each cell a degree of membership in every zone and uses indices such as the fuzziness performance index to choose \(k\).
Assumptions and validity

k-means ignores spatial position: zones may be fragmented into many small patches that no machine can follow. In practice the zone map is smoothed (majority filters, minimum polygon size) and checked against the farmer's knowledge. The number of zones is a compromise: more zones explain more variance but each must still be large enough to manage and to verify with on-farm trials.

\(J\) is a sum of squared z-scores — dimensionless ✓

Try the "Management zones" view in Figure 5. For the default virtual field, two zones explain about two-thirds of the variance of the interpolated map, three zones about 85 % and four about 90 %: each additional zone explains less and less extra — a classic "elbow" that helps choose \(k\). Remember that this is variance of the map, which is smoother than reality; the real test is whether the zones differ in their response to management.

Variable-rate application and prescription maps

A prescription map assigns an application rate to every zone or grid cell and is exported (e.g. as a shapefile or ISO 11783 "ISOBUS" task file) to the terminal of a variable-rate spreader, sprayer or seed drill. As the machine moves, its GNSS position is looked up in the map and the metering system adjusts the rate — with a delay of a few seconds that controllers compensate for by looking ahead. There are two ways to generate rates:

  • Map-based VRA uses maps made before the operation — soil maps for lime and phosphorus, zone maps for seed rate, satellite vegetation-index maps for nitrogen.
  • Sensor-based (real-time) VRA uses sensors on the machine that measure the crop as it passes, such as tractor-mounted canopy reflectance sensors for nitrogen (see Lesson 11.2).

For the second (supplementary) nitrogen dose in cereals, a widely used rule is compensating: where the crop is thinner (lower vegetation index, lower N uptake so far), apply somewhat more; where it is dense, apply less — keeping the field total equal to the planned uniform amount. A simple linear rule does exactly this:

Linear, mass-conserving nitrogen prescription(11.1.11)
\[ N_i=\bar N\left[1-\beta\,\frac{V_i-\bar V}{\bar V}\right],\qquad \bar V=\frac{\sum_i A_i V_i}{\sum_i A_i} \]

the nitrogen rate in zone i equals the planned mean rate, reduced in proportion to how much the zone's vegetation index exceeds the field's area-weighted mean index (or increased where it falls below it). The factor beta sets how strongly the rate responds.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(N_i\)nitrogen rate in zone \(i\)kg N ha⁻¹
\(\bar N\)planned mean rate for the fieldkg N ha⁻¹
\(V_i\), \(\bar V\)vegetation index of zone \(i\); area-weighted field mean–
\(A_i\)area of zone \(i\)ha
\(\beta\)response factor (0 = uniform; 1 = rate changes by the same percentage as the index; negative = "reinforcing" rule)–
Prove that the total amount of nitrogen is conserved
  1. Total N applied: \(\sum_i A_iN_i = \bar N\sum_i A_i - \dfrac{\beta\bar N}{\bar V}\sum_i A_i(V_i-\bar V)\).
  2. By the definition of the area-weighted mean, \(\sum_i A_i V_i = \bar V\sum_i A_i\), so \(\sum_i A_i(V_i-\bar V)=0\).
  3. Hence \(\sum_i A_iN_i = \bar N\sum_i A_i\): the field receives exactly the planned total. Only its distribution changes.
Assumptions and validity: compensate or reinforce?

Compensating (\(\beta>0\)) assumes low index values reflect nitrogen shortage that extra N can correct. If instead low values reflect a permanent limitation — drought-prone sand, waterlogging, a weedy patch — extra N will not be used and will be at risk of leaching; then a reinforcing rule (more N where the crop is strong, \(\beta<0\)) may be better. The right choice depends on the cause of the variation, which is why agronomic knowledge and on-farm trials remain essential. Rates are usually capped at agronomic minimum and maximum values.

kg N ha⁻¹ × [1 − (– × –/–)] = kg N ha⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 4A three-zone nitrogen prescription from a satellite map

A winter-wheat field is split into three zones from a Sentinel-2 vegetation-index map in May: zone A (30 % of the area) has an index of 0.55, zone B (50 %) 0.65 and zone C (20 %) 0.72. The planned supplementary dose is 60 kg N ha⁻¹. Compute a compensating prescription with \(\beta = 1\) and verify that the field total is unchanged.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Area-weighted mean index: \(\bar V = 0.30\times0.55+0.50\times0.65+0.20\times0.72 = 0.165+0.325+0.144 = 0.634\).
  2. Zone A: \((0.55-0.634)/0.634 = -0.1325\) → \(N_A = 60\times(1+0.1325) = 67.9\) kg N ha⁻¹.
  3. Zone B: \((0.65-0.634)/0.634 = +0.0252\) → \(N_B = 60\times0.9748 = 58.5\) kg N ha⁻¹.
  4. Zone C: \((0.72-0.634)/0.634 = +0.1356\) → \(N_C = 60\times0.8644 = 51.9\) kg N ha⁻¹.
  5. Check the total: \(0.30\times67.9+0.50\times58.5+0.20\times51.9 = 20.4+29.2+10.4 = 60.0\) kg N ha⁻¹ ✓.
  6. Agronomic check. Before exporting the file, the agronomist confirms that zone A is thin because of a late-sown patch (N-responsive) rather than a drought-prone sand lens (not responsive); otherwise \(\beta\) should be reduced or reversed.
A: 68, B: 58, C: 52 kg N ha⁻¹; field mean still 60 kg N ha⁻¹

Sweden: CropSAT and satellite-based nitrogen

Sweden has been an early adopter of satellite-supported nitrogen management. CropSAT was developed in a research project at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences (SLU) and launched in 2015, in a public–private partnership that included the companies Agroväst Livsmedel and DataVäxt and the national advisory initiative Greppa Näringen (Focus on Nutrients), administered by the Swedish Board of Agriculture (Söderström et al., 2017; Alshihabi et al., 2020). The web service is free: a farmer draws the field boundary, chooses a recent cloud-free image — originally from the DMC constellation (22 m) and Landsat 8, and since 2016 mainly from Sentinel-2 at 10 m — sees a vegetation-index map, and creates a variable-rate file for nitrogen (or for fungicides and growth regulators) by setting the mean rate and the range of variation. Söderström et al. (2017) showed how index maps can be calibrated to estimated crop nitrogen uptake by combining them with a few tractor-sensor or hand-held measurements. Tractor-mounted reflectance sensors, notably the Yara N-Sensor, are also widely used on large Swedish cereal farms; they are the subject of Lesson 11.2.

Does it pay? Evidence, economics and adoption

The decision to adopt a precision technology is, for a farmer, an investment decision. The standard tool is the partial budget, which counts only the costs and revenues that change:

Partial budget for a precision-agriculture technology(11.1.12)
\[ \Delta\Pi = p_y\,\Delta Y + p_x\,\Delta X_{\text{saved}} - C_{\text{tech}} \]

the change in profit per hectare equals the crop price times the yield gain, plus the input price times the input saved, minus the annualised cost of the technology per hectare.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\Delta\Pi\)change in profit€ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
\(p_y\), \(\Delta Y\)crop price; change in yield€ t⁻¹; t ha⁻¹
\(p_x\), \(\Delta X_{\text{saved}}\)input price; input saved (negative if more is used)€ kg⁻¹; kg ha⁻¹
\(C_{\text{tech}}\)annualised cost of equipment, subscriptions, time and training per hectare€ ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
Annualising an investment

A capital cost \(K\) with lifetime \(n\) years and interest rate \(r\) corresponds to an annual cost \(K\,r/[1-(1+r)^{-n}]\) (the annuity factor). Divide by the hectares on which the technology is used — which is why fixed-cost technologies favour large farms.

Assumptions and validity

Everything else is assumed constant — no change in rotation, labour organisation or risk. Partial budgets can also value environmental benefits (e.g. reduced leaching) if a price or policy payment exists; otherwise these remain external benefits that the farmer does not capture.

€ t⁻¹ × t ha⁻¹ + € kg⁻¹ × kg ha⁻¹ − € ha⁻¹ = € ha⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 5A partial budget for variable-rate nitrogen

On-farm strip trials over three years suggest that sensor-based variable-rate N raises wheat yield by 0.15 t ha⁻¹ on average and saves 5 kg N ha⁻¹. Wheat sells at €200 t⁻¹ and N costs €1.20 kg⁻¹. A sensor system costing €30 000 lasts 8 years at 5 % interest and is used on 400 ha; add €5 ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ for maintenance and time. Is it profitable? (Prices are illustrative.)

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Annuity factor: \(r/[1-(1+r)^{-n}] = 0.05/[1-1.05^{-8}] = 0.05/(1-0.6768) = 0.1547\) yr⁻¹.
  2. Annual capital cost: \(30\,000\times0.1547 = \text{EUR}\,4\,642\) yr⁻¹ → per hectare \(4\,642/400 = \text{EUR}\,11.6\) ha⁻¹; plus €5 → \(C_{\text{tech}} = \text{EUR}\,16.6\) ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹.
  3. Benefits: \(200\times0.15 = \text{EUR}\,30.0\) ha⁻¹ from yield; \(1.20\times5 = \text{EUR}\,6.0\) ha⁻¹ from fertiliser.
  4. Net: \(\Delta\Pi = 30.0+6.0-16.6 = +\text{EUR}\,19.4\) ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹, i.e. about €7 800 per year for the farm.
  5. Sensitivity: on 100 ha, the capital cost alone would be €46 ha⁻¹ and the investment would lose money — the classic farm-size barrier. A free satellite service (such as CropSAT) plus a variable-rate spreader the farm already owns changes the picture completely.
≈ +€19 ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ on 400 ha; negative on 100 ha

What the evidence says

Reviews of the economics consistently find that the benefits of precision agriculture are real but often modest, variable between fields and years, and hard to measure (Bullock et al., 2002; Lowenberg-DeBoer & Erickson, 2019). Guidance and section control deliver reliable input savings and labour benefits, which is why they have spread fastest. Variable-rate technology (VRT) adoption "rarely exceeds 20 % of farms" in national surveys, which Lowenberg-DeBoer and Erickson (2019) interpret as a sign that farmers like the idea of VRT but are not convinced of its value. On the environmental side, a review by Balafoutis et al. (2017) found that guidance, variable-rate nutrient application and controlled traffic can all reduce greenhouse-gas emissions — mainly through lower fuel use, less overlap and better-matched nitrogen, which reduces nitrous oxide emissions per tonne of crop. Gebbers and Adamchuk (2010) argue that the largest long-term value may lie in improved resource-use efficiency and food security rather than in short-term profit.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Three developments are changing precision farming: (1) on-farm experimentation, in which variable-rate machines apply randomised strips of different rates across whole fields so that yield maps estimate site-specific response curves; (2) the move from zones to individual plants by camera-guided robots (Lesson 11.4); and (3) data fusion of satellite time series, weather and soil maps with machine learning to predict in-season nitrogen needs.

Key terms

Precision agriculture
A management strategy that gathers, processes and analyses temporal, spatial and individual data to support decisions according to estimated variability (ISPA, 2019).
Spatial variability
Differences in soil, crop or yield properties from place to place within a field at a given time.
Temporal variability
Differences in a property at the same place from one time (day, season, year) to another.
Pseudorange
The satellite-to-receiver distance computed from signal travel time, contaminated by the receiver clock error and other errors.
Dilution of precision (DOP)
A factor, determined by satellite geometry, that multiplies range errors into position errors.
RTK (real-time kinematic)
GNSS positioning using carrier-phase measurements and corrections from a nearby base station or network, giving centimetre accuracy.
Section control
Automatic switching of sprayer boom sections or planter rows over already-treated or out-of-field areas.
Controlled-traffic farming
Confining all heavy machinery wheels to permanent traffic lanes using RTK guidance, to avoid compacting the cropped area.
Yield monitor
A combine-mounted system that measures grain mass flow, moisture, speed and position to produce yield maps.
Inverse-distance weighting
An interpolation method that estimates unsampled values as a weighted average of samples with weights proportional to distance to the power −p.
Semivariogram
A function describing half the mean squared difference between observations as a function of their separation; characterised by nugget, sill and range.
Kriging
Geostatistical interpolation that chooses unbiased, minimum-variance weights from a fitted semivariogram and also estimates uncertainty.
Management zone
A sub-field region with relatively homogeneous yield-limiting factors, managed with a single rate of an input.
Prescription map
A georeferenced map of application rates read by a variable-rate controller.
Partial budget
An economic analysis that counts only the costs and revenues that change when a technology is adopted.
