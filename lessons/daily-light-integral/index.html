Daily light integral, photoperiod and the solar resource

On a clear day in June a lettuce crop in Västerås can receive more than 40 moles of photons per square metre — for free. On a December day the same square metre receives little more than one mole, because the sun barely clears the horizon for five and a half hours. A crop does not grow on the brightest moment of the day; it grows on the total. This lesson shows you how to calculate that daily total from first principles — from the tilt of the Earth's axis to the glass of the greenhouse roof — and how to decide how much electric light is needed to close the gap.

What you will be able to do

  • Define the daily light integral (DLI) as the time integral of PPFD and calculate it for constant, sinusoidal and measured light, including numerical integration with the trapezoidal rule (LO3).
  • Relate crop DLI requirements to growth, quality and physiological disorders, and use the "1 % rule" to value extra light (LO2, LO3).
  • Derive the solar declination, hour angle, solar elevation, day length and extraterrestrial radiation, and estimate clear-sky and cloudy-sky DLI with the FAO-56 and Ångström–Prescott equations (LO3).
  • Quantify the seasonal DLI deficit of a greenhouse at high latitude and size supplementary lighting in hours, PPFD and kWh m⁻² per year (LO3).
  • Explain photoperiodism and critical night length, and justify why longer photoperiods at lower PPFD usually use a given DLI more efficiently (LO2).

Why the daily dose of photons matters

In Lesson 4.1 you learned to measure light as a photon flux density, PPFD, in µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. PPFD is an instantaneous rate, like the speed shown on a car's speedometer. But a crop's growth over a day depends on how far it has travelled, not on its speed at one moment: the total number of photons absorbed between sunrise and sunset (plus any electric light). That total — the daily light integral, DLI, in mol m⁻² d⁻¹ — is the single most useful number for describing the light environment of a crop, for comparing locations and seasons, and for sizing supplementary lighting (Faust & Logan, 2018).

Two reasons make the DLI more useful than a spot reading of PPFD. First, sunlight fluctuates: a quantum sensor in a greenhouse may read 1200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at noon and 50 an hour later when a cloud passes. A single reading says almost nothing about the day. Second, plants integrate: over hours to days, the carbohydrates a crop produces are, to a good first approximation, proportional to the photons it intercepts. The same DLI delivered as a bright short day or a dim long day produces broadly similar growth — with important exceptions that we will quantify in the last section.

For growers at high latitudes the DLI is also an economic variable. In a Swedish greenhouse the natural DLI varies roughly thirty-fold between midsummer and midwinter. Every mole missing in winter must either be bought as electricity or accepted as lost yield. How much yield? Dutch experiments summarised by Marcelis et al. (2006) give a remarkably robust rule of thumb: 1 % more light gives about 0.5–1 % more harvestable product for most greenhouse crops. You will use that rule, together with the physics of the sun and the physiology of photoperiodism, to reason about light as a resource that is free but unevenly delivered.

Big idea

PPFD tells you how bright; DLI tells you how much. Crop growth, crop requirements and the electricity bill for supplementary lighting all scale with the DLI. Design questions therefore start with the DLI and only then turn to the PPFD and photoperiod that deliver it.

The DLI as a time integral of PPFD

If PPFD were constant, the DLI would simply be PPFD × time (Eq. 4.1.6). Under sunlight, PPFD changes continuously, so we must add up many small contributions: during a short interval \(\Delta t\) the canopy receives \(\text{PPFD}\,\Delta t\) micromoles per square metre. Adding all intervals and letting them shrink gives an integral.

Daily light integral(4.2.1)
\[ \text{DLI} = 10^{-6}\int_{0}^{86\,400\ \text{s}} \text{PPFD}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t \;\;\approx\;\; \frac{2}{\pi}\,\text{PPFD}_{\max}\,N\cdot 3600\cdot10^{-6} \quad\text{(clear day, half-sine)} \]

the daily light integral in moles per square metre per day is the area under the PPFD-versus-time curve over one day (86 400 seconds), converted from micromoles to moles. On a clear day, when PPFD rises and falls roughly like half a sine wave, the area is about two-thirds (2/π) of the rectangle "noon PPFD × day length".

Follow the derivation
  1. Divide the day into \(n\) short intervals of length \(\Delta t\). In interval \(i\) the PPFD is almost constant, \(\text{PPFD}_i\), so the photons received are \(\text{PPFD}_i\,\Delta t\) (µmol m⁻²).
  2. The daily total is the sum \(\sum_i \text{PPFD}_i\,\Delta t\). As \(\Delta t\to0\) this Riemann sum becomes the definite integral \(\int \text{PPFD}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. Multiply by \(10^{-6}\) to convert micromoles to moles.
  4. Special case 1 — constant light. If PPFD is constant for \(t_{\text{photo}}\) hours and zero otherwise, the integral is \(\text{PPFD}\cdot t_{\text{photo}}\cdot3600\), recovering Eq. 4.1.6.
  5. Special case 2 — a clear day. Approximate the PPFD between sunrise (\(t=0\)) and sunset (\(t=N\)) by \(\text{PPFD}_{\max}\sin(\pi t/N)\). Then \(\int_0^{N}\text{PPFD}_{\max}\sin(\pi t/N)\,\mathrm{d}t = \text{PPFD}_{\max}\,\frac{N}{\pi}\big[-\cos(\pi t/N)\big]_0^{N} = \frac{2N}{\pi}\,\text{PPFD}_{\max}\).
  6. With \(N\) in hours, multiply by 3600 s h⁻¹; then by \(10^{-6}\). The factor \(2/\pi = 0.637\) says that a sinusoidal day is "worth" 64 % of a day at constant noon brightness.

The integral itself is exact. The half-sine approximation is only a shape assumption. Use it for quick estimates only; for measured data use Eq. 4.2.2.

Real data do not come as a formula but as a series of readings from a logger: one value every minute, every five minutes or every hour. The integral must then be evaluated numerically. The simplest accurate method is the trapezoidal rule: connect successive readings with straight lines and add up the areas of the trapezoids underneath.

DLI from logged data (trapezoidal rule)(4.2.2)
\[ \text{DLI} \approx 10^{-6}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\text{PPFD}_{k-1}+\text{PPFD}_{k}}{2}\,\Delta t = 10^{-6}\,\Delta t\left[\frac{\text{PPFD}_0+\text{PPFD}_n}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\text{PPFD}_k\right] \]

for readings taken every \(\Delta t\) seconds, the DLI is the sum of the areas of the trapezoids between successive readings: the average of the two readings at the ends of each interval, times the interval length. Equivalently, add all readings, count the first and last only half, and multiply by \(\Delta t\).

Follow the derivation — and the error
  1. Between \(t_{k-1}\) and \(t_k\) replace the unknown curve by a straight line. The area under a straight line segment is its mean height, \((\text{PPFD}_{k-1}+\text{PPFD}_k)/2\), times its width \(\Delta t\).
  2. Adding the segments, every interior reading appears twice with weight ½, i.e., once with weight 1; the first and last readings appear once with weight ½. This gives the second form of the equation.
  3. The trapezoidal rule is exact for straight lines. For a curve with second derivative \(f''\), the error of one interval is \(-\Delta t^3 f''/12\). For a concave curve (the top of a sunny day) the chords lie below the curve, so the rule underestimates.
  4. For a half-sine day sampled with \(n\) intervals between sunrise and sunset, a neat closed form exists: the ratio of the trapezoidal estimate to the exact integral is \((h/2)\cot(h/2)\) with \(h=\pi/n\). With 4 intervals the error is −5.2 %, with 12 intervals −0.6 %, with hourly readings over a 14-hour day −0.4 % (try the calculator below).

Logged values must be instantaneous samples at the stated times. Many loggers instead store the average over each interval; then the correct estimate is simply \(\Delta t\sum \overline{\text{PPFD}}_k\) (the rectangle rule), which is exact. On days with broken cloud, PPFD can jump from 200 to 1500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ within a minute; hourly spot samples then give random errors of 10–30 % for a single day. Sample every minute and average, or use a logger that integrates internally.

How much light do crops need?

Crop light requirements are best expressed as DLI ranges: the lower bound is the DLI below which growth, yield or quality become commercially unacceptable, and the upper bound is where the response saturates or disorders appear. Table 1 collects values from peer-reviewed studies and extension syntheses. Treat them as starting points: the optimum depends on temperature, CO₂ concentration, cultivar, plant density and the price of electricity.

Why does lettuce have an upper limit when photosynthesis itself keeps increasing with light? The Cornell work on hydroponic lettuce illustrates the answer. Fast growth at high DLI means fast leaf expansion, and the young inner leaves transpire little because they are enclosed in the head. Calcium moves only with the transpiration stream, so the expanding leaf tips run short of calcium and die: tipburn. Blowing air vertically onto the plants increases transpiration of the inner leaves and allowed 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ to be used without tipburn (Both et al., 1997). Tomato illustrates a second kind of limit: grown under continuous light (24 h), most cultivars develop mottled, chlorotic leaves — continuous-light injury — so the photoperiod, not only the DLI, must be controlled (Velez-Ramirez et al., 2011).

A consistent integral

Albright et al. (2000) showed that lettuce growth becomes highly predictable if every day receives the same DLI: supplementary lamps fill the gap on dark days and shade screens remove the excess on bright ones. Predictable growth means a predictable harvest date — worth a great deal to a grower who has promised a supermarket 10 000 heads every Tuesday.

How much is extra light worth when the crop is below saturation? Marcelis et al. (2006) reviewed Dutch greenhouse experiments and found that the relative yield response to a small relative change in light is surprisingly constant. Economists would call this an elasticity.

The "1 % rule": light–yield elasticity(4.2.3)
\[ \frac{\Delta Y}{Y} \approx \varepsilon\,\frac{\Delta I}{I}, \qquad \varepsilon \approx 0.7\text{--}1 \ \text{(fruit vegetables)},\ 0.8\text{--}1\ \text{(soil-grown vegetables)} \]

the fractional change in harvestable yield is approximately the fractional change in light (DLI summed over the season) multiplied by an elasticity \(\varepsilon\) that is somewhat smaller than one.

Where does it come from?
  1. If yield were exactly proportional to light, \(Y = kI\), then \(\Delta Y/Y = \Delta I/I\) and \(\varepsilon = 1\).
  2. Leaf photosynthesis saturates at high PPFD, so each additional photon adds a little less than the average photon: \(Y(I)\) is concave and its elasticity \(\varepsilon = (\mathrm{d}Y/\mathrm{d}I)(I/Y)\) is below 1.
  3. Marcelis et al. (2006) found, for most crops, "a 1 % light increment results in 0.5 to 1 % increase in harvestable product": 0.7–1 % for fruit vegetables, 0.8–1 % for soil-grown vegetables, 0.6–1 % for cut flowers and 0.5–1 % for flowering pot plants.
  4. \(\varepsilon\) is higher in dark periods (winter, low light) than in bright ones, and depends on CO₂ and temperature, which is why ranges are given.
Assumptions and validity

Valid for small changes (up to roughly ±20 %) around current practice, and for crops whose harvest is vegetative biomass or fruit load. It does not apply above saturation, to quality traits, or where light causes disorders (tipburn, continuous-light injury).

The rule turns small engineering decisions into money. Solar geometry, step by step

How much sunlight reaches the top of the atmosphere above a given place on a given day depends on only three things: how high the sun climbs, how long it stays above the horizon, and how far the Earth is from the Sun. All three follow from geometry. We now derive, one step at a time, the equations used in FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 (Allen et al., 1998) — the same equations implemented in the course library physics.js and in the sun-path laboratory.

Step 1 — Declination: where the sun is overhead

The Earth's rotation axis is tilted by 23.44° relative to the perpendicular of its orbital plane, and it keeps pointing in the same direction in space (towards Polaris) as the Earth orbits the Sun. Seen from the Earth, the Sun therefore migrates north and south during the year. The solar declination \(\delta\) is the latitude at which the Sun is exactly overhead at noon: +23.44° at the June solstice (Tropic of Cancer), 0° at the equinoxes, −23.44° at the December solstice (Tropic of Capricorn).

Solar declination (FAO-56 Eq. 24)(4.2.4)
\[ \delta = 0.409\,\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}J - 1.39\right)\ \ \text{rad} \]

the declination, in radians, oscillates sinusoidally through the year with an amplitude of 0.409 rad (23.4°), crossing zero around day 81 (the March equinox) and peaking around day 172 (the June solstice).

  2. In a frame where the orbital plane is horizontal, the unit vector from Earth to Sun is \(\hat s = (\cos\Gamma, \sin\Gamma, 0)\) and the Earth's axis is tilted by \(\varepsilon_o = 23.44^{\circ}\): \(\hat a = (0, -\sin\varepsilon_o, \cos\varepsilon_o)\).
  3. The declination is the angle between the Sun direction and the equatorial plane: \(\sin\delta = \hat s\cdot\hat a\). The exact result is \(\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon_o\,\sin\Gamma\) (the sign depends on how the axes are oriented).

Step 2 — Hour angle and solar elevation

During the day the Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, i.e., 15° per hour. The hour angle \(\omega\) measures that rotation from solar noon: \(\omega = 15^{\circ}\,(t_{\text{sol}}-12)\), negative in the morning and positive in the afternoon. Solar time \(t_{\text{sol}}\) is defined so that the sun is due south (in the northern hemisphere) at 12:00; it differs from clock time by the longitude offset within the time zone, daylight saving and a small "equation of time" (up to ±16 min). In Västerås (16.5° E, clock time zone centred on 15° E) solar noon occurs around 12:00 in winter and around 13:00 in summer (daylight saving).

Combine the observer's latitude \(\varphi\), the declination and the hour angle, and spherical geometry gives the angle of the sun above the horizon, the solar elevation \(\beta\).

Solar elevation angle(4.2.5)
\[ \sin\beta = \sin\varphi\,\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\,\cos\delta\,\cos\omega, \qquad \omega = \frac{\pi}{12}\,(t_{\text{sol}}-12) \]

the sine of the sun's elevation above the horizon equals the sine of the latitude times the sine of the declination, plus the product of their cosines times the cosine of the hour angle. At solar noon (\(\omega=0\)) this reduces to \(\beta_{\text{noon}} = 90^{\circ} - \varphi + \delta\).

Follow the derivation (vector dot product)
  1. Place the Earth's centre at the origin, with the \(z\)-axis along the rotation axis and the \(x\)-axis in the plane containing the axis and the Sun (so the Sun's meridian is at \(\omega=0\)).
  2. The unit vector pointing to the Sun is \(\hat s = (\cos\delta,\ 0,\ \sin\delta)\) — by definition of the declination.
  3. An observer at latitude \(\varphi\) whose meridian is rotated by the hour angle \(\omega\) away from the Sun's meridian has a local vertical (zenith) direction \(\hat z = (\cos\varphi\cos\omega,\ \cos\varphi\sin\omega,\ \sin\varphi)\).
  4. The Sun is so far away that the direction to it is the same from the observer as from the centre. The zenith angle \(\theta_z\) between \(\hat z\) and \(\hat s\) follows from the dot product: \(\cos\theta_z = \hat z\cdot\hat s = \cos\varphi\cos\omega\cos\delta + \sin\varphi\sin\delta\).
  5. The elevation is the complement of the zenith angle, \(\beta = 90^{\circ} - \theta_z\), so \(\sin\beta = \cos\theta_z\). This is Eq. 4.2.5.
  6. At noon \(\cos\omega = 1\) and \(\sin\beta = \cos(\varphi-\delta)\), so \(\beta_{\text{noon}} = 90^{\circ} - (\varphi - \delta)\). At the equinox in Västerås, \(\beta_{\text{noon}} = 90^{\circ} - 59.6^{\circ} = 30.4^{\circ}\); at midsummer 53.8°; at midwinter only 7.0°.
Assumptions and validity

Geometric elevation of the centre of the solar disc, without atmospheric refraction. Refraction lifts the apparent sun by about 0.5° at the horizon, which (together with the 0.25° radius of the disc) makes the observed day several minutes longer than the geometric one — up to about 20 minutes at 60° N.

Step 3 — Sunrise, sunset and day length

The sun rises and sets when \(\beta = 0\). Setting \(\sin\beta = 0\) in Eq. 4.2.5 and solving for the hour angle gives the sunset hour angle \(\omega_s\); the day is twice that angle, converted from degrees to hours at 15° per hour.

Sunset hour angle and day length (FAO-56 Eqs. 25 and 34)(4.2.6)
\[ \omega_s = \arccos\!\left(-\tan\varphi\,\tan\delta\right), \qquad N = \frac{24}{\pi}\,\omega_s \]

the hour angle at sunset is the angle whose cosine is minus the tangent of the latitude times the tangent of the declination; the number of daylight hours is 24/π times that angle in radians.

Step 4 — Extraterrestrial radiation: the daily budget at the top of the atmosphere

Just outside the atmosphere, a surface facing the sun receives the solar constant, \(G_{sc} \approx 1367\) W m⁻² (FAO-56 uses 0.0820 MJ m⁻² min⁻¹, which is the same number). A horizontal surface receives less, by the cosine law: \(G_{sc}\sin\beta\). Integrating this over the day gives the extraterrestrial radiation \(R_a\), the ceiling for everything that follows.

Daily extraterrestrial radiation (FAO-56 Eqs. 21 and 23)(4.2.7)
\[ R_a = \frac{24\cdot60}{\pi}\,G_{sc}\,d_r\left[\omega_s\sin\varphi\sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\sin\omega_s\right], \qquad d_r = 1 + 0.033\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}J\right) \]

the solar energy reaching one square metre of horizontal ground at the top of the atmosphere in one day equals a constant (minutes per day over π, times the solar constant, times a small Earth–Sun distance correction) multiplied by a bracket that combines the day length with the height of the sun.

Between the top of the atmosphere and a leaf inside a greenhouse, sunlight passes three filters: the clear atmosphere (scattering by molecules and aerosols, absorption by ozone and water vapour), clouds, and the greenhouse cover and structure. The third filter is the greenhouse. Glass or plastic glazing reflects and absorbs part of the light, and the frame, gutters, heating pipes, screens and lamps cast shadows. Extension guidance puts the loss at 30–50 % compared with an open field (Stallknecht, 2025), and Owen and Lopez (2015) note reductions "by up to 50 % or more" from glazing, superstructure and shading. Dirt, condensation and whitewash add further losses. We therefore write \(\text{DLI}_{\text{in}} = \tau\,\text{DLI}_{\text{out}}\) with a greenhouse transmittance \(\tau\) of typically 0.5–0.7 for PAR. Because of the 1 % rule, the transmittance is one of the most valuable properties of a greenhouse — modern Dutch-style glasshouses with anti-reflection coated diffuse glass are designed to push it as high as possible.

Supplementary lighting: hours, intensity and electricity

Once the natural DLI inside the greenhouse is known, the supplementary-lighting problem is simple arithmetic — but with two design variables that trade off against each other: the PPFD the lamps deliver (which sets the number of fixtures, i.e., the capital cost) and the hours they run (which, together with PPFD, sets the electricity cost).

Frontier — lighting that listens to the sun and the market

LEDs can be dimmed instantly, which allows control strategies that were impossible with high-pressure sodium lamps. Van Iersel and Gianino (2017) adjusted the duty cycle of LEDs every few seconds so that lamps supplied only the difference between a threshold PPFD and the measured sunlight; this reduced electricity use by 20–92 % compared with fixed 14-hour lighting, depending on the threshold and the sunlight. Combining such controllers with hourly electricity prices — in Sweden's SE3 price area, which includes Västerås, spot prices can vary several-fold within one winter day — lets the grower deliver the same DLI while buying photons when they are cheap (Avgoustaki & Xydis, 2021). Photoperiodism: day length as a signal

The length of the day is not only a quantity of light; it is also information. In 1920 Wightman Garner and Harry Allard at the US Department of Agriculture reported that the tobacco cultivar 'Maryland Mammoth', which grew into giant plants without flowering in Maryland summers, flowered readily in winter greenhouses — and would flower in summer too if the plants were placed in a dark shed for part of each day. Soybeans sown at different dates flowered at the same calendar time. They concluded that plants measure the relative length of day and night, and named the phenomenon photoperiodism (Garner & Allard, 1920).

The decisive variable is the length of the dark period, not of the light period. Hamner and Bonner (1938) showed this with cocklebur (Xanthium), a short-day plant: plants on long nights flowered, but interrupting the long night with a brief flash of light — a night break — prevented flowering, whereas interrupting the day with darkness had no effect. The night length that separates flowering from non-flowering is the critical night length. They also showed that the leaves perceive the signal and send it to the shoot apex. Today we know that the signal is the protein FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT), produced in leaves when the clock-controlled protein CONSTANS coincides with light at the right time of day — the "external coincidence" model (Song et al., 2015). The photoreceptors that sense the light are phytochromes and cryptochromes, discussed in Lesson 4.4.

For controlled-environment engineering, photoperiodism has two consequences. First, photoperiodic lighting is cheap: plants detect day length with photoreceptors, not photosynthesis, so a few µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ during a night break or a day extension is enough — two orders of magnitude less than photosynthetic lighting. Growers of chrysanthemum or strawberry transplants use such low-intensity lamps to control flowering. Second, the photoperiod constrains the DLI strategy: for a short-day crop you cannot extend the day to reach a DLI target without also preventing flowering, so a higher PPFD during a shorter day is required; for a long-day leafy green grown under 18–20 hours of light, bolting-resistant cultivars matter.

Common misconception

"Short-day plants need short days." They need long uninterrupted nights. A short-day plant given a 10-hour day and a 14-hour night interrupted in the middle by 15 minutes of light behaves as if it experienced two short nights — and does not flower. Conversely, light leaking into a black-out greenhouse from a neighbour's lamps or a street light can ruin a poinsettia crop.

Spreading the dose: photoperiod versus intensity

For a day-neutral crop under electric light, a DLI of 16 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ can be delivered as 444 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 10 hours or 222 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 20 hours. The electricity is the same (Eq. 4.2.9). Is the growth the same? The answer lies in the curvature of the photosynthetic light response (Lesson 2.1): photosynthesis rises steeply at low PPFD and saturates at high PPFD. The first photons are used efficiently, the last ones poorly. Spreading the same photons over more hours keeps the leaves on the steep part of the curve.

Daily photosynthesis at a fixed DLI(4.2.10)
\[ A_d(t) = 3600\,t\;P\!\left(\frac{10^{6}\,\text{DLI}}{3600\,t}\right)\cdot10^{-6} = \text{DLI}\cdot\frac{P(I)}{I}\Big|_{I = 10^{6}\text{DLI}/(3600\,t)} \]

the daily photosynthesis (moles of CO₂ fixed per square metre per day) equals the DLI multiplied by the light-use efficiency \(P(I)/I\) at the PPFD needed to supply that DLI in \(t\) hours. Because the efficiency falls as PPFD rises, longer photoperiods at lower PPFD fix more carbon from the same photons.
