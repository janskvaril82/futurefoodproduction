Designing and balancing aquaponic systems

A lettuce raft does not know how many fish swim upstream of it, and a tilapia does not care how many heads of lettuce leave the greenhouse each week. Only the engineer sees both — and must decide how many square metres of plants, how many kilograms of fish, how many cubic metres of water per hour and how much potassium hydroxide belong together. This lesson turns the nitrogen chemistry of Lesson 6.1 into a design procedure: from a production target to tanks, flows, filters, nutrient budgets and supplements, for both the classic one-loop system and the decoupled multi-loop systems developed in Europe over the past decade — and it ends with the questions that decide whether a farm survives: energy, water, money and scale.

  • Calculate flow rates, hydraulic retention times, steady-state ammonia and biofilter area, and size a gravity clarifier from Stokes' law and the overflow rate (LO3).
  • Build nitrogen and phosphorus budgets for coupled and decoupled designs, including sludge mineralisation, and calculate supplements of potassium, calcium and iron (LO3, LO7).
  • Compare coupled one-loop and decoupled multi-loop systems, and use the evapotranspiration-driven water flow to explain why decoupled systems need more plant area per kilogram of feed (LO2, LO3).
  • Justify a pH–temperature operating point as a compromise between fish, nitrifiers and plants, and evaluate energy use, water use, economics and scale in a Nordic and European context (LO2, LO7).

One input, many constraints

Aquaponic design is a problem with one input and many constraints. The input is fish feed. Everything the system has to do — remove ammonia, supply oxygen, capture faeces, neutralise acid, feed the plants — scales with the kilograms of feed that enter each day, because feed is the only substantial source of nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter (Lesson 6.1, Eq. 6.1.2). The constraints come from three groups of organisms with different preferences. Fish want oxygen, low ammonia and nitrite and a stable temperature. Nitrifying bacteria want surfaces, oxygen, alkalinity and a pH above 7. Plants want light, oxygen at their roots and a complete nutrient supply at a pH between 5.5 and 6.5. And the whole system must pay for its pumps, heaters, feed and labour every day of the year.

Rakocy, Masser and Losordo (2006) describe three ways into a design: copy a proven system such as the University of the Virgin Islands (UVI) design, design for the space you have, or design for a production target of fish or of plants. We follow the last route and start from the plants, for a practical reason: in the few published economic analyses, the vegetables — not the fish — carry the profit (Engle, 2015). Key idea

Size by feed, check by chemistry. Ratios such as grams of feed per square metre give a first design. The feed-rate ratio: how many plants per fish?

The decisive design ratio links the fish side to the plant side through the feed. In Rakocy's words, "the key is the ratio of daily feed input to plant growing area" (Rakocy et al., 2006). If the ratio is too high, nutrient salts accumulate — potentially to phytotoxic levels above about 2000 ppm total dissolved solids or 3.5 mS cm⁻¹ — and more water must be exchanged. If it is too low, the plants show deficiencies and more nutrients must be supplemented. Fortunately, hydroponic plants grow well over a wide range of concentrations, so the ratio does not have to be exact.

Feed-rate ratio and plant growing area(6.2.1)
\[ R_F = \frac{F}{A_p} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A_p = \frac{F}{R_F} \]

the feed-rate ratio is the mass of fish feed added per day divided by the plant growing area; turned round, the plant area needed equals the daily feed divided by the chosen feed-rate ratio.

Follow the derivation
  1. The ratio is an empirical definition: it was found by running systems at different ratios and observing when nutrients accumulated or ran short (Rakocy et al., 2006).
  2. For design, the production target usually fixes one side. If the plant target is known, \(A_p\) follows from plant spacing and cropping time, and the feed is \(F = R_F A_p\). If the fish target is known, \(F\) follows from fish production and feed conversion (Eq. 6.2.3) and \(A_p = F/R_F\).
  3. Because \(F\) is proportional to the nutrients entering the system, choosing \(R_F\) is equivalent to choosing the nutrient load per square metre of crop.
Assumptions and validity

The published ranges assume a feed of about 32 % crude protein (Somerville et al., 2014), fairly complete solids removal, low water exchange and supplementation of K, Ca and Fe (UVI). Rakocy et al. (2006) list the factors that shift the optimum: lower ratios are possible with low water exchange, nutrient-rich source water, incomplete or slow solids removal (more mineralisation) and slow-growing plants; higher ratios are needed with high exchange, soft source water, fast complete solids removal and fast-growing crops. Staggered cropping evens out uptake and allows slightly lower ratios than batch cropping. Gravel and NFT systems contain little water, so they need about a quarter of the raft ratio for the same nutrient concentrations.

Where does the ratio come from? A nutrient-based view

The feed-rate ratio is empirical, but it can be understood — and challenged — with a mass balance. Plants take up nutrients with the water they transpire. A simple and widely used first estimate, recommended for aquaponic sizing by Goddek et al. (2019), assumes that the crop absorbs each nutrient at the concentration in which it is dissolved: uptake per square metre equals crop evapotranspiration times the target concentration of the nutrient solution. The fish, in turn, release a known fraction of each fed nutrient in dissolved form. Setting supply equal to demand gives a feed-rate ratio for every nutrient separately.

Nutrient-limited feed-rate ratio(6.2.2)
\[ R_{F,X} = \frac{u_X}{w_X\,\eta_X} = \frac{ET_c\,\rho_X}{1000\;w_X\,\eta_X} \]

the feed-rate ratio that exactly supplies nutrient X equals the plants' daily uptake of X per square metre divided by the grams of dissolved, plant-available X released per gram of feed; the uptake is estimated as crop evapotranspiration times the target concentration of X.

\(R_{F,X}\)feed-rate ratio balanced on nutrient Xg feed m⁻² d⁻¹
\(u_X\)crop uptake of X per unit areag m⁻² d⁻¹≈ 0.19
\(ET_c\)crop evapotranspiration (1 mm = 1 L m⁻²)L m⁻² d⁻¹1–3 in a greenhouse
\(\rho_X\)target concentration of X in the nutrient solutionmg L⁻¹N ≈ 125, P 31, K 215
\(w_X\)mass fraction of X in the feedg g⁻¹N: 0.16 × 0.32 = 0.051

Follow the derivation
  1. Demand. A crop transpiring \(ET_c\) litres per square metre per day, fed a solution with \(\rho_X\) milligrams of X per litre, takes up \(ET_c\,\rho_X\) mg m⁻² d⁻¹ if uptake follows water uptake: \(u_X = ET_c\,\rho_X/1000\) in g m⁻² d⁻¹ (Goddek et al., 2019; Eck et al., 2019).
  2. Supply. Each gram of feed contains \(w_X\) grams of X, of which a fraction \(\eta_X\) reaches the water in dissolved form (soluble excretion; plus any in-system mineralisation of solids). Per square metre: \(R_F\,w_X\,\eta_X\).
  3. Balance. Steady state without accumulation or supplementation requires \(R_F\,w_X\,\eta_X = u_X\), hence \(R_{F,X} = u_X/(w_X\eta_X)\).
  4. Numbers. Cornell's commercial lettuce solution contains about 125 mg N, 31 mg P and 215 mg K per litre (Brechner & Both, 2013). Montanhini Neto and Ostrensky found that Nile tilapia release 33 % of feed N and 17 % of feed P in soluble form (as reported by Goddek et al., 2019). With \(ET_c = 1.5\) L m⁻² d⁻¹, a 32 % protein feed and 1.2 % P in feed: \(R_{F,N} = 1.5\times125/(1000\times0.0512\times0.33) = 11\) and \(R_{F,P} = 1.5\times31/(1000\times0.012\times0.17) = 23\) g m⁻² d⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

Uptake proportional to water uptake is a deliberate simplification: plants absorb some ions faster (nitrate, potassium) and others slower than water, store surplus nitrate and change their demand with growth stage and light (Eck et al., 2019). \(\eta_X\) is not a constant either — it rises if solids stay in the system and mineralise, and it falls for phosphorus when phosphate precipitates with calcium at pH ≥ 7. Use Eq. 6.2.2 to find which nutrient runs out first, not to fix the plant area to the nearest square metre.

\(\dfrac{\text{L m}^{-2}\text{d}^{-1}\times\text{mg L}^{-1}\times10^{-3}\,\text{g mg}^{-1}}{\text{g g}^{-1}\times(-)} = \text{g feed m}^{-2}\,\text{d}^{-1}\) ✓

The result is striking. Balanced on nitrogen alone, lettuce would need only about 10 g of feed per square metre per day — six to ten times less than the UVI ratio. At 60–100 g m⁻² d⁻¹ the fish therefore deliver several times more dissolved nitrogen than the lettuce can take up, which is exactly what the nitrogen use efficiencies in Lesson 6.1 showed. In the UVI system much of the surplus is removed by denitrification in the sludge that accumulates on the orchard netting of the filter tanks; the operators regulate it — and with it nitrate-N anywhere between about 1 and 100 mg L⁻¹ — simply by cleaning the netting more or less often (Rakocy et al., 2006). Why not design at 10 g m⁻² d⁻¹, then? Because the other nutrients would run out even faster. Fish feed is formulated for fish, and its nutrient ratios do not match a plant's: no single plant-to-fish ratio can supply every nutrient in the right proportion from feed alone (Seawright, Stickney & Walker, 1998, as discussed by Eck et al., 2019). The empirical feed-rate ratio is a compromise between surplus nitrogen and deficient potassium, iron or calcium — a compromise that supplementation (Section 6.2.8) and decoupling (Section 6.2.6) try to escape.

Common misconception

"A well-balanced aquaponic system recycles all the fish waste into plants and needs no other inputs." Even the best-documented coupled systems add bases containing K and Ca, chelated iron and some fresh water, and lose a large part of the nitrogen as gas and much of the phosphorus with the sludge. "Balanced" means that no nutrient accumulates to harmful levels and none limits growth — not that nothing is lost or added.

Fish biomass, feeding rate and stocking density

The feed has to be eaten, so the daily feed fixes the fish biomass. Feeding rate expressed as a percentage of body mass falls steeply as fish grow: Masser, Rakocy and Losordo (1999) give about 5 % per day for 9 g warm-water fish, 3 % at 113 g, 2.75 % at 227 g, 2.2 % at 454 g and 1.8 % at 681 g. The FAO manual uses 1–2 % per day for grow-out fish above 50 g (Somerville et al., 2014). Once the biomass is known, the stocking density fixes the tank volume, and the feed conversion ratio fixes the annual production.

Fish biomass, rearing volume and annual production(6.2.3)
\[ B = \frac{F}{r_f}, \qquad V_R = \frac{B_{\max}}{SD_{\max}}, \qquad P_{\text{fish}} = \frac{365\,\bar F}{\text{FCR}} \]

the standing fish biomass equals the daily feed divided by the feeding rate; the rearing volume equals the largest biomass the tanks must hold divided by the highest acceptable stocking density; and the annual fish gain equals a year's feed divided by the feed conversion ratio.

  2. Stocking density is biomass per volume, \(SD = B/V\). The binding moment is just before harvest, when a tank holds its largest biomass; therefore \(V_R = B_{\max}/SD_{\max}\).
  3. FCR is defined as feed eaten per unit gain, so the gain from a year's feed is \(365\bar F/\text{FCR}\). The harvested mass is the gain plus the mass of the fingerlings stocked.
Assumptions and validity

\(r_f\) must be the mean feeding rate of a population that contains fish of all sizes (Figure 2). FCR depends strongly on temperature, feed quality and feeding management; UVI reported 1.7 for Nile and 1.8 for red tilapia (Rakocy et al., 2006). Mortality (10 % was allowed at UVI) reduces the harvest for the same feed.

What density is acceptable? The FAO manual recommends that beginners do not exceed 20 kg of fish per 1000 L, because "a brief interruption can kill all of the fish within an hour at high stocking densities" (Somerville et al., 2014). UVI raised tilapia to a "critical standing crop" of 0.5 lb gal⁻¹ (≈ 60 kg m⁻³): Nile tilapia were harvested at 61.5 kg m⁻³ and red tilapia at 70.7 kg m⁻³ from 7.8 m³ tanks aerated by 22 diffusers each, without pure oxygen (Rakocy et al., 2006). Goddek et al. (2015) quote 30–40 kg m⁻³ as common for outdoor tilapia systems without pure oxygen and up to 60 kg m⁻³ in greenhouses. Density is also a welfare question: its effects operate largely through water quality and behaviour, which Lesson 6.3 examines.

The critical standing crop is the largest biomass a system can carry without restricting growth. Operating close to it uses tank space efficiently and, just as importantly for aquaponics, "reduces variation in the daily feed input to the system, an important factor in sizing the hydroponic component" (Rakocy et al., 2006). There are three ways to keep biomass near the critical standing crop: sequential rearing (several age groups in one tank, with selective harvests), stock splitting (halving the population into a new tank when the limit is reached) and multiple rearing units (separate tanks for separate cohorts). UVI uses four tanks stocked six weeks apart on a 24-week cycle. Figure 2 shows why.

With a single batch, the feed input climbs from about 8.5 to 27 kg d⁻¹ over the cycle and then collapses at harvest — a factor of three. Plants sized for the mean would be starved at the start of each cycle and overloaded at the end. With four staggered cohorts the same fish volume varies only by about ±13 % around the same mean of 17.7 kg d⁻¹, and with six cohorts by about ±8 %. Staggering the plants works the same way: UVI harvests a quarter of its leaf lettuce every week, so that nutrient uptake stays nearly constant (Rakocy et al., 2006).

Flow, retention time and ammonia control

Water carries waste from the fish to the treatment units and returns treated water. Two times characterise the hydraulics. The hydraulic retention time (HRT) of a component is its volume divided by the flow through it; its inverse, multiplied by 24 h, is the number of turnovers per day. In the UVI system, 378 L min⁻¹ flows through four 7.8 m³ rearing tanks, giving a mean retention time in the rearing tanks of 82 min, and the whole 111 m³ system circulates about five times a day (Rakocy et al., 2006). What sets the flow? Usually ammonia.

Retention time and steady-state TAN in a recirculating tank(6.2.4)
\[ \begin{gathered} \tau = \frac{V}{Q}, \qquad C_T = C_{\text{in}} + \frac{P_{\text{TAN}}}{Q} \\[6pt] C_{\text{in}} = (1-\eta)\,C_T \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; C_T = \frac{P_{\text{TAN}}}{\eta\,Q} \end{gathered} \]

the retention time is volume divided by flow. In a fish tank at steady state, the ammonia concentration leaving the tank equals the incoming concentration plus the daily ammonia production divided by the flow; if the treatment loop removes a fraction eta of the ammonia on each pass, the tank settles at the ammonia production divided by eta times the flow.

Solids: remove them fast, then recycle them

Faeces, uneaten feed and sloughed biofilm are the least glamorous part of aquaponics and the most frequent cause of failure. Between 20 and 40 % of the feed mass ends up as total suspended solids in the sludge of a recirculating system (Timmons & Ebeling, 2013, cited by Goddek et al., 2019). Left in the water, solids consume oxygen as they decay and release CO₂ and ammonia; deep deposits turn anaerobic and produce methane and hydrogen sulphide, "which are very toxic to fish"; suspended solids that reach the plants coat the roots and create anaerobic zones that block nutrient uptake (Rakocy et al., 2006). Fine particles also damage gills (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Thorarinsdottir (2015) attributed more than 85 % of the failures of aquaponic systems to inadequate solids removal (as cited by Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017).

Yet solids are also where most of the phosphorus and many micronutrients sit, and their microbial breakdown — mineralisation — releases nutrients the plants need (Rakocy et al., 2006). The design principle is therefore: remove solids from the fish loop quickly, before they break into fine particles and leach, and then decide deliberately what to do with them. Engineers classify solids by size and behaviour: settleable solids (above about 100 µm) sink out of still water within an hour; suspended solids stay in the water column; fine solids below 30 µm can make up more than half of the total suspended solids in a recirculating system; and dissolved organic matter passes every filter (Losordo et al., 1998). How big must a settler be? Stokes and Hazen

Gravity separators are sized with two classic results. Stokes' law gives the terminal settling velocity of a small sphere in a viscous fluid. Hazen (1904) then showed that in an ideal settling basin, whether a particle is captured depends only on its settling velocity and the overflow rate — flow divided by the surface area of the basin — and not on the depth. Figure 4 shows the argument.

Stokes settling velocity and the overflow-rate criterion(6.2.6)
\[ \begin{gathered} v_s = \frac{g\,(\rho_p-\rho_w)\,d^{2}}{18\,\mu}, \qquad \text{captured if}\;\; v_s \ge \frac{Q}{A_s} \\[6pt] \Rightarrow\;\; d_c = \sqrt{\frac{18\,\mu\,Q}{g\,(\rho_p-\rho_w)\,A_s}} \end{gathered} \]

a small particle sinks at a speed proportional to gravity, to its excess density over water and to the square of its diameter, and inversely proportional to the water's viscosity; an ideal settler captures every particle that sinks faster than the flow divided by the settler's surface area, so the smallest particle it captures completely has the critical diameter d-c.

Recycling the sludge: the mineralisation loop

In a coupled system, the captured sludge is usually discharged — to holding ponds and farmland, compost or the municipal sewer (Rakocy et al., 2006) — and with it goes most of the phosphorus. Decoupled designs (Section 6.2.6) add a separate mineralisation loop: sludge is digested in aerobic or anaerobic bioreactors, and the nutrient-rich effluent is sent to the plants only. The benefits are measurable. Aerobic treatment of aquaculture sludge raised soluble reactive phosphorus 3.2-fold (from 9.4 to 29.7 mg L⁻¹) and also mobilised potassium, whereas unaerated treatment left phosphate unchanged (Monsees, Keitel et al., 2017). In sequential upflow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) reactors, a pH below 6 increased the recovery of phosphorus, potassium and calcium by about 25 % compared with reactors at higher pH, at the cost of less organic-matter reduction (Goddek et al., 2018); around 90 % of the nutrients in RAS sludge can be recovered at pH 4 (Jung & Lovitt, 2011, as cited by Goddek et al., 2019). Goddek et al. (2019) give three equations to size such a loop.

One loop or several? Coupled and decoupled designs

In the traditional coupled (one-loop) system, water recirculates freely between fish and plants, so pH, temperature and nutrient concentrations must be a compromise between their different optima (Goddek et al., 2019). Decoupled (two-loop) systems separate the recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) from the hydroponic (HP) unit. Water flows only one way, from the fish to the plants; the RAS is topped up with fresh water, and the hydroponic loop runs at its own pH and temperature and can be supplemented with fertiliser without exposing the fish. The concept was proposed by Goddek et al. (2015) and Kloas et al. (2015) — the latter as ASTAF-PRO, an "aquaponic system for (nearly) emission-free tomato and fish production" in which a recirculating fish unit and a hydroponic unit are joined by a one-way valve. Multi-loop systems add a sludge mineralisation loop (Eq. 6.2.7) and, in the most ambitious designs, a distillation or desalination loop that concentrates the nutrient solution for the plants and returns clean water to the fish (Goddek & Keesman, 2018). Frontier — closing the last loops

Multi-loop aquaponics treats a farm as a set of coupled mass balances that must be sized by simulation rather than by rules of thumb. Goddek and Körner (2019) built an integrated model of fish growth, greenhouse climate, crop evapotranspiration and nutrient flows to size multi-loop systems for different climates, and Goddek and Keesman (2018) showed how a desalination or distillation unit can keep the fish water clean while concentrating the plant solution — attractive where solar or geothermal heat is cheap. Decoupled designs have been adopted by commercial companies, but the sector remains fragile: UrbanFarmers in The Hague, an early adopter of decoupling, went bankrupt, and Goddek et al. (2019) could point to only a few multi-loop implementations, such as NerBreen in Spain and Kikaboni AgriVentures in Kenya.

Nitrogen and phosphorus budgets

The design checks of the previous sections rest on one principle: over a long enough period, every gram of nitrogen and phosphorus that enters with the feed must leave the system or accumulate in it. For any element X, a steady-state budget has six terms.

\[ \begin{aligned} F\,w_X &= \underbrace{r_X F w_X}_{\text{fish}} + \underbrace{c\,\varphi_X F w_X}_{\text{sludge}} + U_X + G_X + E_X + \Delta M_X \\[3pt] D_X &= F w_X\left[s_X + (1-c)\,\varphi_X\right], \qquad \varphi_X = 1 - r_X - s_X \end{aligned} \]

Filling the gaps: supplements and the pH compromise

Fish feed is a complete food for fish, "but not necessarily everything needed for plant growth": fish simply do not need the same amounts of iron, potassium and calcium as plants, so even a perfectly balanced system tends to become deficient in exactly these three (Somerville et al., 2014). The UVI experience is the same. Nitrate, phosphate and sulphate are usually sufficient for good plant growth, while potassium and calcium are generally insufficient; iron from the feed is insufficient and must be supplemented as chelated iron to about 2 mg L⁻¹; manganese, boron and molybdenum are several times lower than in hydroponic formulations but produce no deficiency symptoms; copper is similar; and zinc accumulates to 4–16 times hydroponic levels, although it usually remains within the limit safe for fish (Rakocy et al., 2006). Other systems differ with their feed and water: Delaide et al. (2016) found K, P, Fe, Cu, Zn, Mn and Mo lacking and N, Ca, B and Na accumulating, and Graber and Junge (2009) measured three times less nitrogen, ten times less phosphorus and 45 times less potassium than in a hydroponic solution, with similar yields but lower quality because of the potassium shortage (as reported by Eck et al., 2019). Fish retain only up to about 7 % of the potassium in the feed (Rafiee & Saad, 2005, as reported by Eck et al., 2019) — the problem is that feed contains too little of it.

The pH compromise

pH is the parameter on which the three organisms disagree most. Nitrification is optimal between pH 7.0 and 9.0, most efficient in the high 8s; most hydroponic plants grow best at pH 5.8–6.2 (acceptable 5.5–6.5); iron, manganese, copper, zinc and boron become less available above pH 7.0, while the solubility of phosphorus, calcium, magnesium and molybdenum drops sharply below 6.0 (Rakocy et al., 2006). Goddek et al. (2015) summarise the evidence for the bacteria: in submerged biofilters nitrification efficiency increased linearly by about 13 % per pH unit between pH 5 and 9, with the highest ammonia-oxidiser activity at pH 8.2 (Villaverde, García-Encina & Fdz-Polanco, 1997); optimal values reported for Nitrosomonas are 7.0–7.5, for Nitrobacter 7.5 and for Nitrospira 8.0–8.3. Tilapia tolerate pH 3.7–11 but grow best at 7–9. Their recommendation, "the highest possible pH value … consistent with the prevention of ammonia accumulation", is 6.8–7.0; Rakocy et al. (2006) recommend "close to 7.0" and the FAO manual 6–7 (Somerville et al., 2014). Higher pH also raises the toxic, un-ionised share of ammonia (Lesson 6.3). Root uptake of nitrate releases bicarbonate and nudges the pH up, but nitrification dominates, so a coupled system drifts slowly downwards and needs base (Goddek et al., 2015). Common misconception

"Any base will do to correct the pH — baking soda is cheap and safe for fish." Sodium bicarbonate is widely used in fish-only RAS, but it "should never be added to an aquaponic system for pH control because a high Na⁺ level in the presence of chloride is toxic to plants"; above about 50 mg Na L⁻¹ sodium interferes with K and Ca uptake and causes tip-burn in lettuce (Rakocy et al., 2006). In aquaponics the base is part of the fertiliser plan.

Energy, water, money and scale

Water

Recirculating aquaculture already reuses 95–99 % of its water and can produce fish with less than 100 L of water per kilogram (Goddek et al., 2015). Fish-only RAS usually replace 5–10 % of their volume each day to keep nitrate and dissolved organics down (Masser et al., 1999); in aquaponics the plants take over part of that job, and daily exchange falls below 2 % (Rakocy et al., 2006). The remaining losses are evaporation, transpiration and sludge — UVI runs entirely on collected rainwater. In a decoupled system the fish water becomes the plants' irrigation water, and the RAS is topped up by exactly the volume the crop transpires (Eq. 6.2.8).

Energy

Aquaponics trades land and water for electricity and heat. Water must be lifted and air blown continuously, and warm-water fish must be kept warm. Pumping power follows directly from the hydraulic head.

For the Västerås farm (29.5 m³ h⁻¹, 1.5 m head, 50 % efficiency) the pump needs about 240 W, or 5.8 kWh per day. Aeration usually costs more than pumping: the UVI unit lists a ½ hp water pump but blowers of 1½ hp for the fish and 1 hp for the plants — about 2.2 kW of motors in total, or up to ≈ 54 kWh d⁻¹ if they all ran at their rated power, for roughly 5 t of tilapia and 1400 cases of lettuce a year (Rakocy et al., 2006). In a Nordic greenhouse, heat dominates: tilapia grow best at 27–30 °C, while rainbow trout prefer 14–16 °C (Somerville et al., 2014), so the choice of fish is also a choice of heating bill (Lesson 8.1). Goddek et al. (2015) point to waste heat from combined heat-and-power plants, geothermal heat as in Iceland, and heat pumps. Housing fish and plants in the same greenhouse creates synergies — shared heat, and CO₂ from fish respiration that enriches the greenhouse air (Eck et al., 2019; Rakocy et al., 2006).

Money and scale

The economic evidence is sobering. In an international survey of 257 aquaponic operations, only 31 % reported that their operation had been profitable in the previous 12 months, aquaponics was the main source of income for only 30 %, and the average US production site covered just 0.01 ha — closer to a hobby than to commercial horticulture (Love et al., 2015, as summarised by Turnšek et al., 2019). Engle (2015) found labour to be 46 % of operating costs in the one detailed cost analysis of commercial farms (Tokunaga et al., 2015, as cited by Engle, 2015), fixed costs only 8–13 % of total costs (so economies of scale are weaker than in other aquaculture), and a consistent pattern in which the vegetables — especially basil — made money while the fish broke even or lost it. Modelled investments paid back in 7.5 years for a trout–lettuce system (Adler et al., 2000) and 6.8 years at non-organic prices (Quagrainie et al., 2018), both as reported by Turnšek et al. (2019); in Europe, an initial investment of at least 250 € per m² of growing area should be expected (Turnšek et al., 2019). A European model of three farm sizes found that only the production-scale farm covered its costs — and only if land was free — with labour, feed plus fingerlings, and energy each making up roughly a third of the costs (Morgenstern et al., 2017, as reported by Turnšek et al., 2019). For multi-loop systems, Goddek et al. (2019) conclude that profitability currently requires large scale (more than about 1 ha) and good markets. Organic certification, which could justify premium prices, is not available: EU Regulation 2018/848 prohibits hydroponic production and closed recirculation aquaculture facilities (except hatcheries and nurseries), so aquaponic products cannot be sold as organic in the EU (Kledal, König & Matulić, 2019; as of 2026).

The European and Nordic research landscape

European aquaponics research was organised by COST Action FA1305, "The EU Aquaponics Hub" (2014–2018), which produced the open-access book used throughout this module. In 2016 the Association of Commercial Aquaponics Companies grew out of it, and a survey counted about 20 commercial aquaponic enterprises in Europe; a later count found 45 commercial enterprises and 52 research organisations, although only a handful actually sold aquaponic produce (Villarroel et al., 2016; Turnšek et al., 2019). Nordic researchers took part from the start: the review of aquaponic challenges by Goddek et al. (2015) came partly from the University of Iceland, the fish-welfare review of Yavuzcan Yildiz et al. (2017) included the University of Jyväskylä, and the chapter on decoupled systems was co-written at the University of Gothenburg (Goddek et al., 2019). Simulations for the Faroe Islands at 62° N show what high latitude means for design: low and strongly seasonal evapotranspiration requires the largest plant areas and the most active nutrient control, and in Norway, vegetable cultivation without supplementary lighting "hardly takes place" (Goddek et al., 2019). For a Swedish farm at 59.6° N the lesson is clear: design for winter light and heat first, and choose fish accordingly.

Sustainability link

A design that halves water use but doubles electricity may not reduce the environmental footprint at all. Whether aquaponics is more sustainable than separate RAS and hydroponics depends on the whole life cycle — feed ingredients, energy source, nutrient losses and yields — and is assessed with the methods of Lesson 1.3 and Lesson 15.1.

Key terms

Feed-rate ratio
Daily fish-feed input per square metre of plant growing area (g m⁻² d⁻¹); the main empirical sizing rule of aquaponics.
Critical standing crop
The largest fish biomass a system can carry without restricting growth; operating near it keeps the daily feed input stable.
Stocking density
Fish biomass per unit water volume (kg m⁻³); limited by oxygen supply, water quality and welfare.
Staggered (multiple-cohort) production
Rearing several age groups of fish or plants in parallel and harvesting them in rotation, to smooth feed input and nutrient uptake.
Hydraulic retention time
Volume of a component divided by the flow through it; the mean time water spends in it.
Removal per pass
The fraction of a substance (e.g. TAN) removed each time water passes through a treatment unit.
Overflow rate
Flow divided by the surface area of a settling basin (m h⁻¹); an ideal basin captures all particles that settle faster than this.
Stokes' law
The terminal settling velocity of a small sphere: \(v_s = g\Delta\rho\,d^2/(18\mu)\).
Microscreen drum filter
A rotating cylindrical screen (typically 40–80 µm) that strains solids from water and removes them by automatic backwashing.
Sludge mineralisation
Aerobic or anaerobic microbial digestion of captured solids that converts organic nutrients into dissolved forms plants can use.
Decoupled aquaponics
A design in which the fish and plant units have separate water loops and water flows one way from fish to plants.
Multi-loop aquaponics
A decoupled design with additional loops, such as sludge mineralisation reactors and distillation units, to recycle nutrients and water more completely.
Chelated iron
Iron bound to an organic ligand (e.g. DTPA, EDTA) that keeps it soluble and plant-available at near-neutral pH.
