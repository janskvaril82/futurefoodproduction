GMOs, new genomic techniques, organics and labelling

A CRISPR-edited wheat, a hydroponic tomato and a pea-protein "sausage" have one thing in common: in the European Union, what they may be called and what their labels must say is decided by law, not by the producer. This lesson follows the rules that shape how new foods are presented to consumers — from the 0.9 % GM labelling threshold and the Court of Justice's 2018 mutagenesis ruling, through the new genomic techniques regulation finally adopted in June 2026, to the soil requirement that keeps vertical farms out of organic certification and the long fight over whether a veggie burger may be called a burger.

  • Summarise the CJEU mutagenesis ruling (C-528/16) and the new genomic techniques Regulation (EU) 2026/1388: categories NGT1 and NGT2, labelling, organic exclusion and patents, and classify example plants (LO6, LO1).
  • Apply the organic regulation (EU) 2018/848 to soilless systems and explain the demarcated-bed derogation for Sweden, Finland and Denmark (LO6, LO2).
  • Check a pre-packed novel-food label against Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, and calculate energy values, QUID percentages and eligibility for protein claims under Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 (LO6, LO3).
  • Analyse the naming disputes over plant-based and cultivated products, from TofuTown (2017) and Protéines France (2024) to the reserved meat terms of Regulation (EU) 2026/1739 (LO6).

Process, product and the right to know

Regulators can look at a new food in two ways. A product-based system asks what the final food is: its composition, its hazards, its intended use. A process-based system asks how it was made, and attaches obligations to the technique itself. The United States and Canada regulate foods from biotechnology largely product by product; the European Union regulates genetically modified organisms by process — the same maize kernel is subject to authorisation, traceability and labelling if it was produced by recombinant DNA technology, and not if it was produced by conventional breeding. Process-based regulation is often criticised by scientists as inconsistent with risk, and defended by others as a matter of consumer choice and precaution.

Labelling rules have two functions that are easy to confuse. Some protect health: allergen emphasis, warnings for vulnerable groups, use-by dates. Others protect informed choice: the name of the food, the list of ingredients, origin, "organic" and "genetically modified" labels. A GM label does not say that a food is unsafe — authorised GM foods have passed an EFSA risk assessment — it tells consumers about a production method that many of them care about. Keep this distinction in mind throughout the lesson: most battles about new foods are battles over information and names rather than over safety.

Big idea

For future-food producers, labelling law is as important as authorisation law. A product can be perfectly legal and yet unsellable if it cannot carry the name, the claim or the organic logo that its market expects.

The EU GMO framework and the 0.9 % threshold

The EU's GMO legislation rests on three pillars:

  • Directive 2001/18/EC on the deliberate release of GMOs into the environment — field trials (Part B) and placing on the market (Part C), with an environmental risk assessment. A GMO is an organism "in which the genetic material has been altered in a way that does not occur naturally by mating and/or natural recombination". Annex I B exempts some techniques, notably mutagenesis.
  • Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003 on GM food and feed — a single centralised procedure: application via a national authority, risk assessment by EFSA's GMO Panel, authorisation by the Commission after comitology, valid for ten years and renewable. It covers food that contains or consists of GMOs and food produced from GMOs, such as oil or sugar from GM crops.
  • Regulation (EC) No 1830/2003 on traceability and labelling — at every stage of the chain operators must pass on, in writing, the information that a product contains or is produced from GMOs and the unique identifier of each GMO, and keep the records for five years.

Since Directive (EU) 2015/412, Member States may also restrict or prohibit the cultivation of authorised GMOs on their territory for non-safety reasons, which many have done. Imports for food and feed — above all soybean meal for animal feed — continue on a large scale.

Labelling applies to each ingredient: the list of ingredients must say "genetically modified [name]" or "produced from genetically modified [name]". The label is required even if no GM DNA or protein can be detected in the final food, as with highly refined oil. Two things are not labelled: food produced with the help of GMOs but not from them (for instance meat or milk from animals fed GM feed, or products made with enzymes from GM micro-organisms that are processing aids), and adventitious traces below a threshold. Articles 12(2) and 24(2) of Regulation 1829/2003 exempt food or feed containing GM material "in a proportion no higher than 0.9 per cent of the food ingredients considered individually … provided that this presence is adventitious or technically unavoidable".

GM labelling rule for one ingredient(14.2.1)
\[ \text{label}_i = \begin{cases} \text{required} & \text{if } w_i > 0.9\,\% \ \text{ or the GM presence is not adventitious} \\ \text{not required} & \text{if } w_i \le 0.9\,\% \ \text{ and adventitious or technically unavoidable} \end{cases}, \qquad w_i = \frac{m_{\text{GM},i}}{m_i}\times 100\,\% \]

for each ingredient separately, compute the share of GM material; a GM label is needed if this share exceeds 0.9 per cent, or if the GM material was used deliberately, however small the share.

Why per ingredient, not per food?

If the threshold referred to the whole food, a small GM ingredient could hide in a large product: 5 % GM soy flour in a bread would be only 0.25 % of the bread. Considering ingredients "individually" prevents this dilution. The operator must also show — e.g., with segregation, certificates and testing — that the presence was adventitious or technically unavoidable; otherwise even 0.1 % must be labelled.

Assumptions and validity

Only GM events authorised in the EU benefit from the threshold. Unauthorised GM material is not permitted at all in food (for feed, a technical low-level-presence tolerance of 0.1 % exists for certain events pending authorisation). The threshold does not apply to organic products differently: organic rules prohibit the use of GMOs, but adventitious traces below 0.9 % do not in themselves cause loss of organic status.

kg/kg × 100 % → % ✓ (dimensionless ratio)
Worked example 1Soy, maize and oil in a vegetarian lasagne

A lasagne contains (1) textured soy protein in which PCR detects 0.4 % GM soy, arriving despite an identity-preserved supply chain; (2) maize starch with 1.3 % GM maize, also adventitious; (3) soybean oil pressed from a declared GM soybean variety, in which no DNA can be detected after refining; (4) cheese from cows fed GM soybean meal. Which ingredients must be labelled?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Soy protein, 0.4 %, adventitious: \(w \le 0.9\,\%\) and unavoidable → no label (Eq. 14.2.1). Keep the documentation that proves segregation.
  2. Maize starch, 1.3 %: above 0.9 % → "maize starch (produced from genetically modified maize)". Being adventitious does not help above the threshold.
  3. Soybean oil from GM soy: deliberately produced from a GMO → "soybean oil (produced from genetically modified soybeans)", even though no DNA is detectable. Labelling follows the process, not the analysis.
  4. Cheese from GM-fed cows: produced with GM feed, not from a GMO → no GM label. (Some national voluntary "GMO-free" schemes, such as Germany's "Ohne Gentechnik", go further and exclude GM feed.)
Label the maize starch and the soybean oil; not the soy protein or the cheese

Mutagenesis before the Court of Justice

Plant breeders have used mutagenesis — random mutations induced by radiation or chemicals — since the 1950s, and the resulting varieties were exempted from the GMO rules by Annex I B of Directive 2001/18/EC. When targeted techniques such as CRISPR–Cas9 appeared, the question became whether they too were "mutagenesis" and therefore exempt. In 2015 the Swedish Board of Agriculture (Jordbruksverket) took the view that certain CRISPR-edited plants without foreign DNA did not fall under the GMO definition, which allowed researchers at Umeå University to grow edited plants outside GMO rules. French farmers' and environmental organisations challenged the French implementation, and the Conseil d'État referred the question to the Court of Justice.

In its judgment of 25 July 2018 in Case C-528/16 Confédération paysanne and Others, the Court held that organisms obtained by mutagenesis are GMOs, and that the exemption covers only organisms obtained by mutagenesis techniques "which have conventionally been used in a number of applications and have a long safety record". New techniques of targeted mutagenesis, which appeared after the directive was adopted, therefore fell under the full GMO regime: authorisation, traceability and labelling. The ruling ended national interpretations such as the Swedish one. In Case C-688/21 (7 February 2023) the Court added that in vitro random mutagenesis applied to plant cells, whose effects are comparable to conventional in vivo random mutagenesis, remains exempt.

Why the ruling was so consequential

Many edited plants are indistinguishable from plants with spontaneous or radiation-induced mutations: a small deletion leaves no trace of how it arose. The 2018 ruling therefore created rules that could not be enforced by laboratory tests on imported products (Purnhagen et al., 2018; Eriksson et al., 2019). In 2021 the Commission concluded, in a study requested by the Council, that the legislation was "not fit for purpose" for NGTs, and in 2022 EFSA's GMO Panel published criteria for risk assessment of plants from targeted mutagenesis, cisgenesis and intragenesis (EFSA GMO Panel, 2022). These documents prepared the ground for a new law.

The new genomic techniques regulation

On 5 July 2023 the Commission proposed a regulation on plants obtained by certain new genomic techniques (COM(2023) 411). It covers only targeted mutagenesis and cisgenesis (including intragenesis) applied to plants; transgenic plants, and NGTs applied to animals and micro-organisms, stay under the existing GMO law. After almost three years of negotiations it became Regulation (EU) 2026/1388 of 17 June 2026 on plants obtained by certain new genomic techniques and their products. It was published in the Official Journal on 26 June 2026, entered into force on 16 July 2026 and applies two years later, from 17 July 2028.

The criteria for NGT1 are the heart of the law. The Commission's proposal defined equivalence by a list of modifications (Annex I) of which a plant could carry up to 20: substitution or insertion of no more than 20 nucleotides; deletion of any number of nucleotides; cisgenic insertion of sequences from the breeders' gene pool; targeted inversion; and other targeted modifications provided the resulting sequence already exists in the gene pool. The final text keeps this approach of listing modification types, and at Parliament's request adds the excluded traits. The Parliament's most far-reaching demands — mandatory consumer labelling of all NGT food and a complete ban on patents — were not retained.

Worked example 2Classifying three edited plants

Under Regulation (EU) 2026/1388, classify (a) a wheat line in which CRISPR–Cas9 deleted part of an asparagine synthetase gene to reduce acrylamide formation in bread; (b) an oilseed rape with a single base change conferring tolerance to a herbicide; (c) a potato into which three late-blight resistance genes from wild potato relatives were inserted with their own promoters (cisgenesis), with no other changes.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Targeted mutagenesis producing a deletion — a modification type listed for NGT1 — and not an excluded trait → NGT1 after verification. Seeds labelled "NGT1 plants"; flour and bread need no GM label; not allowed in organic production.
  2. (b) A single-nucleotide substitution would satisfy the modification criteria, but herbicide tolerance is an excluded trait → NGT2: authorisation with risk assessment, GMO labelling and traceability; Member States may ban cultivation.
  3. (c) Cisgenesis with genes from the breeders' gene pool is within the scope. Three insertions are well below the limit of modifications, so it can qualify as NGT1 if the inserted sequences meet the criteria (in the proposal: cisgenic sequences inserted without interrupting endogenous genes). Otherwise NGT2.
(a) NGT1 · (b) NGT2 (excluded trait) · (c) NGT1 if the criteria are met

Note how a trait (herbicide tolerance) changes the category even though the molecular change in (b) is the smallest. The law mixes process, product and policy criteria.

Frontier — what to watch until 2028

Controls of imports will remain difficult because many NGT1 changes cannot be distinguished from natural variation. Organic production and soilless cultivation

The EU organic regulation, Regulation (EU) 2018/848, applies since 1 January 2022 (its application was postponed by one year by Regulation (EU) 2020/1693). It defines organic plant production as soil-related. Annex II, Part I states:

  • Point 1.1: organic crops, "except those which are naturally grown in water, shall be produced in living soil, or in living soil mixed or fertilised with materials and products allowed in organic production, in connection with the subsoil and bedrock".
  • Point 1.2: "Hydroponic production, which is a method of growing plants which do not naturally grow in water with their roots in a nutrient solution only or in an inert medium to which a nutrient solution is added, is prohibited."
  • Points 1.3–1.4 (derogations): sprouts by moistening seeds and chicory heads (including dipping in clear water); ornamentals and herbs in pots sold with the pot to the final consumer; seedlings and transplants grown in containers for further transplanting.
  • Point 1.5: growing crops in demarcated beds is allowed only for surfaces certified as organic for that practice before 28 June 2017 in Finland, Sweden and Denmark; no extension of those surfaces is permitted; the derogation expires on 31 December 2031, and the Commission must report on demarcated beds by 31 December 2026 (dates as amended by Regulation (EU) 2020/1693).

For this course the consequence is clear: lettuce from a hydroponic system, a deep-water-culture raft or an aeroponic tower cannot be sold as organic in the EU, however few inputs it uses; only water plants (such as watercress or duckweed grown in water) are "naturally grown in water". The Nordic demarcated-bed derogation is a historical compromise for greenhouse growers who had been certified under the previous regulation (EC) No 834/2007. The United States takes the opposite position: the USDA's National Organic Program allows organic certification of hydroponic operations, a policy that a US federal court declined to overturn in 2022. A lettuce can therefore be "organic" in California and not in Sweden.

Common misconception

"Organic hydroponics is impossible because the nutrients are mineral." The EU prohibition is not about the fertiliser: organic-compatible nutrient solutions (e.g., from digestates or fish effluent in aquaponics) exist. It is about the principle that organic crops are fed through a living soil ecosystem connected to the subsoil. Aquaponic vegetables are therefore not organic in the EU either, even if the fish are organic.

Food information to consumers

Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on the provision of food information to consumers (often called FIC) has applied since 13 December 2014, and its mandatory nutrition declaration since 13 December 2016. It applies to every food, novel or not. Article 7 requires information to be accurate, clear and not misleading, and Article 9(1) lists twelve mandatory particulars:

  1. the name of the food;
  2. the list of ingredients;
  3. any substance or product causing allergies or intolerances listed in Annex II;
  4. the quantity of certain ingredients or categories of ingredients (QUID);
  5. the net quantity;
  6. the date of minimum durability or the "use by" date;
  7. any special storage conditions and/or conditions of use;
  8. the name or business name and address of the food business operator;
  9. the country of origin or place of provenance where required by Article 26;
  10. instructions for use where it would be difficult to make appropriate use of the food without them;
  11. the actual alcoholic strength by volume for beverages with more than 1.2 % alcohol;
  12. a nutrition declaration.

The fourteen allergens of Annex II are cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame seeds, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above 10 mg kg⁻¹ or 10 mg L⁻¹ as SO₂), lupin and molluscs. They must be emphasised in the ingredient list, for example in bold (Article 21). Mandatory information must be printed with an x-height of at least 1.2 mm, or 0.9 mm if the largest surface of the package is smaller than 80 cm² (Article 13). The name of the food (Article 17) is its legal name if one exists; otherwise its customary name or a descriptive name.

A novel food adds the specific labelling requirements of its Union list entry (Lesson 14.1). For insects this means the designation (e.g., "partially defatted powder of Acheta domesticus (house cricket)") and a statement that the ingredient may cause allergic reactions in people with known allergies to crustaceans, molluscs and dust mites; for rapeseed protein, a mustard allergy statement; for chia seeds sold pre-packed, a maximum daily intake of 15 g.

Two quantitative rules appear on every label. The energy value in the nutrition declaration is calculated from the composition with conversion factors fixed in Annex XIV, and the QUID (quantitative ingredient declaration) gives the percentage of an ingredient that is emphasised in the name or pictures — "10 % cricket powder".

Worked example 3QUID for cricket crispbread

A Swedish bakery mixes 70 kg rye flour, 12 kg house-cricket powder, 3 kg salt and seeds, and 45 kg water (130 kg dough). Baking and drying remove 40 kg water. The front of the pack says "with crickets". What QUID must be declared for the cricket powder, and how would it be expressed if the crispbread were a dried "cricket snack" made of 90 kg cricket paste that dries down to 60 kg?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. The ingredient is emphasised on the pack → QUID is mandatory (Article 22(1)(b)).
  2. Finished product: \(130 - 40 = 90\) kg. QUID \(= 12/90\times100 = 13.3\) %. (Relative to the ingredients at mixing it would be 9.2 %; because the product lost moisture, the finished-product basis applies.)
  3. Snack: \(90/60\times100 = 150\) % > 100 %. The label must instead say "150 g of cricket paste used per 100 g of product".
  4. Sanity check: a dried product can legitimately contain "more than 100 %" of a wet ingredient — that is why the regulation switches to grams per 100 g.

Nutrition and health claims

New foods are often marketed on their nutrition: "high in protein", "source of fibre", "supports muscle growth". In the EU such statements are claims governed by Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. The principle is a positive list: only authorised claims may be used, under their conditions.

  • Nutrition claims are listed with their conditions in the Annex. Examples: "source of protein" — at least 12 % of the energy value of the food comes from protein; "high protein" — at least 20 %; "low fat" — no more than 3 g fat per 100 g (1.5 g per 100 mL for liquids); "source of fibre" — at least 3 g per 100 g or 1.5 g per 100 kcal; "high fibre" — at least 6 g per 100 g or 3 g per 100 kcal.
  • Health claims require EFSA's scientific assessment. General function claims (Article 13) are listed in Regulation (EU) No 432/2012, e.g., "Protein contributes to a growth in muscle mass", which may be used only for foods that at least meet the "source of protein" condition. Claims based on new evidence (Article 13(5)) and disease risk reduction and children's development claims (Article 14) require individual applications.
  • Article 4 foresaw nutrient profiles — thresholds for salt, sugar and fat above which claims would be forbidden — by 2009. They have never been adopted, so a sugary drink with added protein may still say "source of protein".
Share of energy from protein(14.2.5)
\[ s_{\text{P}} = \frac{17\,c_{\text{P}}}{E}\times 100\,\% ; \qquad \text{"source of protein" if } s_{\text{P}} \ge 12\,\%, \quad \text{"high protein" if } s_{\text{P}} \ge 20\,\% \]

Why energy share rather than grams?

An energy basis makes the claim independent of water content: a watery soup and a dry powder with the same protein-to-energy ratio are treated equally. The consequence is that low-energy foods can reach 20 % easily, while energy-dense foods (nuts, fatty cheeses) need a lot of protein.

What may a product be called? Naming disputes and front-of-pack labels

Names carry expectations, which is why they are fought over. In Case C-422/16 TofuTown (14 June 2017) the Court of Justice ruled that terms reserved for milk products by the common market organisation regulation (Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013, Annex VII, Part III) — milk, cream, butter, cheese, yoghurt — may not be used for purely plant-based products, even with a clarifying description such as "tofu butter", except for traditional names on an exhaustive list (such as "coconut milk" or "peanut butter"). Meat names were not reserved in the same way, and national legislators stepped in.

France adopted decrees in 2022 and 2024 banning meat names such as "steak" for products containing plant proteins. In Case C-438/23 Protéines France and Others (4 October 2024) the Court held that Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 harmonises the protection of consumers against being misled about the substitution of ingredients, so a Member State may not prohibit the use of customary or descriptive names for plant-protein products — unless it adopts a legal name for a food, which then must be respected. The French decree was subsequently annulled by the Conseil d'État. Italy's Law No 172 of 1 December 2023 combined a ban on cultivated meat with a ban on meat names for processed products containing plant proteins (Lesson 14.3).

The question then moved to EU level. In a revision of the common market organisation rules intended to strengthen farmers' position in the food supply chain (Commission proposal COM(2024) 577), Parliament's first-reading amendments of 8 October 2025 sought to reserve meat terms including "burger" and "sausage" for products containing meat. The final act, adopted by Parliament on 16 June 2026 (560 votes to 75, with 25 abstentions) and published as Regulation (EU) 2026/1739 on 29 July 2026, defines "meat" and "meat products" and reserves the term meat and a list of 31 further terms for meat products (Annex VII, Part Ia, of Regulation (EU) No 1308/2013 as amended): beef, veal, pork, poultry, chicken, turkey, duck, goose, lamb, mutton, ovine, goat, drumstick, tenderloin, sirloin, flank, loin, ribs, shoulder, shank, chop, wing, breast, thigh, brisket, ribeye, T-bone, rump, bacon, steak and liver. "Burger" and "sausage" are not on the list. Crucially for this course, these terms may not be used to designate food consisting of, isolated from or produced from cell or tissue culture — so a cultivated chicken product could not be sold in the EU as "chicken" once the rules apply. Products placed on the market under the old rules may be sold for a transitional period (at most six years or until stocks run out).

Parliament's amendments to the CMO revision seek to reserve meat terms, including "burger".

16 June 2026

Final text adopted; Regulation (EU) 2026/1739 published 29 July 2026: "meat" and 31 further reserved terms, which may not be used for cell-cultured food.

Front-of-pack labels are the other open front. The EU has no harmonised interpretive scheme: the Farm to Fork Strategy (2020) promised a proposal for mandatory harmonised front-of-pack nutrition labelling, but the harmonised scheme had not been adopted by 2026. Member States use voluntary schemes: Nutri-Score (a five-colour A–E grade, introduced in France in 2017 and used in several countries including Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands and Spain; its algorithm was revised in 2023–2024), Italy's NutrInform Battery, and in the Nordic countries the Keyhole (Nyckelhålet), introduced by the Swedish Food Agency in 1989, which marks the healthier products within a food group. Ultra-processed plant-based meat alternatives often score reasonably on Nutri-Score but can struggle with the Keyhole's salt criteria — the choice of scheme influences which new foods look healthy.

A double standard?

Regulation (EU) 2026/1739 protects meat names on the argument that consumers could be misled, while the CJEU found in 2024 that the general labelling rules already protect consumers against misleading substitution. Critics argue that the reservation protects market shares rather than consumers; supporters argue that clear names reduce confusion. Key terms

Genetically modified organism (GMO)
An organism whose genetic material has been altered in a way that does not occur naturally by mating and/or natural recombination (Directive 2001/18/EC).
Adventitious presence
Unintended, technically unavoidable presence of authorised GM material; exempt from labelling up to 0.9 % per ingredient.
Unique identifier
Code assigned to each GM event that must accompany GM products through the supply chain (Regulations (EC) No 1830/2003 and 65/2004).
Targeted mutagenesis
Techniques such as CRISPR–Cas that change the genome at a chosen site without inserting foreign genetic material.
Cisgenesis
Insertion of genetic material from the breeders' gene pool (the same or a crossable species).
NGT1 / NGT2
Categories of plants under Regulation (EU) 2026/1388: NGT1 plants are considered equivalent to conventional plants after verification; NGT2 plants follow an adapted GMO authorisation.
Soil-related crop cultivation
Production in living soil in connection with the subsoil and bedrock — the basis of EU organic plant production.
Demarcated beds
Growing beds separated from the underlying soil; allowed in organic production only for areas certified before 28 June 2017 in Finland, Sweden and Denmark, until 31 December 2031.
Mandatory particulars
The twelve items of food information required by Article 9(1) of Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011.
QUID
Quantitative ingredient declaration: the percentage of an ingredient that is emphasised in the name or presentation of a food.
Nutrition claim
A claim that a food has particular beneficial nutritional properties, allowed only as listed in the Annex to Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
Health claim
A claim that a relationship exists between a food or constituent and health, allowed only after EFSA assessment and authorisation.
Legal name
A name of a food prescribed by EU or national law, which must be used for that food (Article 17 of Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011).
