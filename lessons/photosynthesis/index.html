Photosynthesis: from photons to sugars

Every lettuce leaf in a vertical farm is a solar-powered chemical factory running on electricity-made light. In about a millisecond it turns absorbed photons into a flow of electrons, pumps protons across a membrane thinner than a soap film, makes ATP with a rotary motor, and feeds carbon dioxide to the most important — and most frustrating — enzyme on Earth. This lesson follows that energy step by step, quantifies how much of it can end up in sugar, and builds the two models that engineers use to predict leaf photosynthesis: the light-response curve and the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model. By the end you will be able to explain, with numbers, why growers enrich CO₂ and raise light intensity together.

What you will be able to do

  • Trace electrons and protons through photosystem II, plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f, plastocyanin, photosystem I and ATP synthase, and explain the Z-scheme and chemiosmosis (LO2).
  • Calculate the minimum quantum requirement of CO₂ fixation from the ATP and NADPH budget, and the maximum efficiency with which photosynthesis converts light energy into biomass (LO3).
  • Explain the Calvin–Benson cycle, the carboxylase/oxygenase dilemma of Rubisco and the cost of photorespiration, and compare C3, C4 and CAM photosynthesis (LO2).
  • Fit and interpret a leaf light-response curve (non-rectangular hyperbola): quantum yield, maximum rate, curvature, dark respiration, light compensation and saturation points (LO3).
  • Use the Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model with the Bernacchi et al. (2001) temperature functions to predict CO₂, light and temperature responses and identify the limiting process (LO2, LO3).
  • Justify, quantitatively, why CO₂ enrichment and high photon flux should be combined in controlled-environment agriculture, and explain why a canopy responds differently from a single leaf (LO2, LO3).

Photosynthesis: the engine of every farm

All crop biomass — every gram of lettuce, tomato or wheat — is carbon that a leaf pulled out of the air and reduced to carbohydrate using energy from light. Nitrogen, potassium and water are indispensable, but they are raw materials; photosynthesis is the only process that adds chemical energy to the system. In controlled-environment agriculture (CEA) this has a very direct economic meaning: in a plant factory every photon that drives photosynthesis was bought as electricity, and every molecule of CO₂ may have been bought from a gas supplier. Understanding how efficiently a leaf converts those purchased inputs into sugar is therefore not an academic detail — it is the physiological core of the business model.

Photosynthesis is usually summarised by one overall reaction. Writing it per carbon atom (a "CH₂O unit", one-sixth of a glucose molecule) makes the bookkeeping easier:

Overall reaction of oxygenic photosynthesis(2.1.1)
\[ \text{CO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{light},\ \ge 8\ \text{photons}\;}\; (\text{CH}_2\text{O}) + \text{O}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}, \qquad \Delta G^{\circ\prime} \approx +478\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1} \]

one mole of carbon dioxide and two moles of water, driven by absorbed light, give one mole of carbohydrate (as CH₂O units), one mole of oxygen and one mole of water. The reaction is strongly uphill: about 478 kJ of free energy must be supplied per mole of CO₂ fixed, and that energy comes from photons.

Why are there two water molecules on the left and one on the right?
  1. Isotope experiments with H₂¹⁸O in the 1940s showed that all the O₂ released comes from water, not from CO₂. Producing one O₂ therefore requires two H₂O.
  2. The four hydrogen atoms of those two waters (as 4 electrons + 4 protons) reduce one CO₂ to CH₂O. The second oxygen atom of CO₂ ends up in a new water molecule.
  3. Cancelling one H₂O on each side gives the familiar net form CO₂ + H₂O → CH₂O + O₂, but the full form shows where the oxygen really comes from.
  4. For glucose multiply by six: 6 CO₂ + 12 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ + 6 H₂O, ΔG°′ ≈ +2870 kJ mol⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

The overall equation hides a long chain of reactions and says nothing about rates. The free-energy value refers to standard conditions; the actual energy stored depends on which products are made (starch, sucrose, proteins, lipids). Zhu, Long and Ort (2010) use 477 kJ per CH₂O unit, the value we adopt below.

Photosynthesis happens in two coupled parts (Figure 1). In the light reactions, located in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast, absorbed photons split water, release O₂ and produce two energy carriers: ATP and the reductant NADPH. In the Calvin–Benson cycle, located in the surrounding stroma, ATP and NADPH are spent to fix CO₂ into triose phosphate, the building block of sucrose and starch. The two parts must run at matched rates: if the cycle cannot use ATP and NADPH fast enough, the light reactions back up and absorbed energy must be dissipated as heat; if light is scarce, the cycle waits for ATP and NADPH. Most of this lesson is about that balance.

Big idea

Photosynthesis is a chain of energy conversions: photon → excited chlorophyll → separated charge → electron and proton gradients → ATP and NADPH → carbohydrate. Every link loses some energy. The maximum efficiency of the whole chain, and which link limits the rate under given conditions, determine how much crop you get per photon and per euro.

Inside the chloroplast: membranes, pigments and antennae

A typical mesophyll cell of a lettuce leaf contains tens of chloroplasts, each a lens-shaped organelle some 5–10 µm long, bounded by a double envelope. Inside is an aqueous matrix, the stroma, which holds the enzymes of the Calvin–Benson cycle, and an intricate system of flattened membrane sacs, the thylakoids. Thylakoids are stacked into grana connected by unstacked stroma lamellae; together they enclose a single continuous internal space, the lumen. That topology matters: the thylakoid membrane separates two compartments — stroma and lumen — and the light reactions exploit this separation to build a proton gradient, exactly as a battery separates charges.

Embedded in the thylakoid membrane are four large protein complexes that do the work (Figure 4): photosystem II (PSII), the cytochrome b₆f complex, photosystem I (PSI) and ATP synthase. They are not evenly distributed: PSII is concentrated in the appressed grana, whereas PSI and ATP synthase, which have bulky parts protruding into the stroma, sit mainly in the stroma lamellae and grana margins. Small mobile carriers shuttle electrons between them: plastoquinone (PQ) diffuses within the membrane, plastocyanin (PC, a copper protein) travels through the lumen, and ferredoxin (Fd, an iron–sulfur protein) in the stroma.

Light is captured by pigments bound to proteins. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b absorb strongly in the blue and red and weakly in the green; carotenoids absorb blue-green light and, crucially, protect the apparatus by quenching dangerous excited states. Most pigment molecules do not do chemistry at all: they form light-harvesting antennae (e.g., LHCII) that funnel excitation energy, by resonance transfer within picoseconds, to a special chlorophyll pair in a reaction centre — P680 in PSII and P700 in PSI, named after the wavelength (nm) at which their absorption bleaches. Each reaction centre is served by a few hundred antenna pigments, which is why a leaf can capture photons efficiently even though each individual reaction centre could process photons far faster than they arrive in sunlight.

Connecting to Module 4

Whatever its wavelength within 400–700 nm, an absorbed photon excites a chlorophyll; higher excited states (from blue photons) relax within femtoseconds to picoseconds to the lowest excited singlet state, which is equivalent to the energy of a red photon of ≈ 680 nm. The surplus energy of blue photons is lost as heat. This is why photosynthesis counts photons rather than joules — the principle behind PPFD in Lesson 4.1 and the McCree curve in Lesson 4.4.

The light reactions and the Z-scheme

The task of the light reactions is to move electrons from water, which holds them very tightly, to NADP⁺, which accepts them only reluctantly. Chemists measure how tightly a substance holds electrons with its redox (midpoint) potential \(E_m\), in volts: the more positive \(E_m\), the stronger the oxidant. The O₂/H₂O couple has \(E_m \approx +0.82\) V at pH 7, the NADP⁺/NADPH couple \(E_m \approx -0.32\) V. Moving electrons "uphill" from +0.82 to −0.32 V costs free energy:

Free energy of a redox reaction(2.1.2)
\[ \Delta G = -\,n\,F\,\Delta E, \qquad \Delta E = E_{m,\text{acceptor}} - E_{m,\text{donor}} \]

the free-energy change of transferring n moles of electrons equals minus n times the Faraday constant times the difference in redox potential between the electron acceptor and the electron donor. Electrons flowing to a more positive potential release energy (ΔG < 0); pushing them to a more negative potential costs energy.

Assumptions and validity

Midpoint potentials refer to equal concentrations of oxidised and reduced forms at pH 7. In a working chloroplast the ratios differ from 1 and the Nernst equation shifts the actual potentials by tens of millivolts, but the order of magnitude — about 1.1–1.2 V per electron — is robust.

\(\text{mol}\cdot\text{C mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{V} = \text{C}\cdot\text{J C}^{-1} = \text{J}\) (per mole of product) ✓

Can one photon supply that energy? A mole of 680 nm photons carries \(N_A hc/\lambda = 176\) kJ, i.e., 1.82 eV per photon — more than the 1.14 eV needed per electron. Yet no single pigment can do the job, because converting an excitation into a stable separated charge costs energy (the charge must be pulled away fast enough that it does not recombine), and because the same reaction centre would have to be both an extraordinarily strong oxidant (to take electrons from water) and an extraordinarily strong reductant (to give them to NADP⁺). Evolution solved this with two photosystems in series, each absorbing one photon per electron. Plotted on a redox scale, the path of an electron looks like a sideways "Z" — the Z-scheme of Hill and Bendall (Figure 3).

Follow one electron through Figure 3 and Figure 4:

  1. PSII and water oxidation. A photon (or its excitation energy delivered by the antenna) excites P680 to P680*, which within picoseconds donates an electron to pheophytin and then to the quinones QA and QB. The oxidised P680⁺ is the strongest biological oxidant known (\(E_m \approx +1.2\) V). It takes an electron back from a tyrosine, which in turn takes it from the oxygen-evolving complex, a Mn₄CaO₅ cluster on the lumen side. After four photochemical turnovers the cluster has accumulated four oxidising equivalents and splits two water molecules: 2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻. The four protons are released into the lumen.
  2. Plastoquinone. QB accepts two electrons and picks up two protons from the stroma, becoming plastoquinol (PQH₂), which leaves PSII and diffuses through the membrane.
  3. Cytochrome b₆f and the Q cycle. PQH₂ is oxidised at the lumen side of cytochrome b₆f: its two protons go into the lumen, one electron goes to plastocyanin, the other is recycled through cytochrome b₆ to reduce another quinone at the stroma side. This clever loop — the Q cycle — doubles the proton pumping: for every electron that continues to plastocyanin, two protons end up in the lumen.
  4. PSI. Plastocyanin reduces P700⁺. A second photon excites P700 to P700*, the most reducing species in the chain (\(E_m \approx -1.3\) V), which passes the electron through iron–sulfur centres to ferredoxin on the stroma side.
  5. NADPH. Ferredoxin–NADP⁺ reductase (FNR) combines two electrons from two ferredoxins with NADP⁺ and a stromal proton to make NADPH.

Two photons per electron and four electrons per O₂ give a minimum of eight photons per O₂ released — and per CO₂ fixed, because two NADPH (four electrons) reduce one CO₂. We return to this number in the next section.

Chemiosmosis: making ATP with a proton turbine

In 1961 Peter Mitchell proposed that the link between electron transport and ATP synthesis is not a chemical intermediate but a gradient of protons across a membrane — the chemiosmotic theory, for which he received the 1978 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In the chloroplast the lumen becomes acidic in the light, the stroma alkaline, and the lumen slightly positive. Both the concentration difference and the electrical difference push protons back to the stroma; their combined driving force is the proton-motive force:

Proton-motive force and the energy per proton(2.1.3)
\[ \text{pmf} = \Delta\psi + \frac{2.303\,R\,T}{F}\,\Delta\text{pH}, \qquad \Delta G_{\text{H}^+} = -F\cdot\text{pmf} \]

the proton-motive force equals the electrical potential difference between lumen and stroma plus 2.303 RT/F (about 59 mV at 25 °C) times the pH difference between stroma and lumen. Each mole of protons flowing back to the stroma releases F × pmf joules of free energy.

How many protons per ATP?

Making ATP from ADP and Pᵢ under stromal conditions requires roughly 40–50 kJ mol⁻¹ (the standard value is 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹; real concentration ratios make it higher). With ≈ 19 kJ per proton, at least 2.1–2.6 protons are thermodynamically needed. Structurally, the membrane rotor of spinach chloroplast ATP synthase has 14 c-subunits (Seelert et al., 2000): 14 protons turn it once and the catalytic head makes 3 ATP per turn, i.e., 14/3 ≈ 4.67 H⁺ per ATP. Many textbook calculations (e.g., Zhu et al., 2010) use a rounder 4 H⁺ per ATP.

Quantum requirement and the limits of efficiency

Two numbers summarise how well a leaf uses light. The quantum yield Φ is the number of moles of CO₂ fixed (or O₂ evolved) per mole of photons absorbed; its reciprocal is the quantum requirement. Measured at low light (where light, not the enzymes, limits the rate), they tell us the efficiency of the photochemical machinery. The energy-conversion efficiency goes one step further and compares the chemical energy stored with the radiant energy absorbed.

Quantum yield, quantum requirement and energy-conversion efficiency(2.1.4)
\[ \Phi = \frac{\text{mol CO}_2\ \text{fixed}}{\text{mol photons absorbed}} = \frac{1}{n_q}, \qquad \eta = \frac{\Phi\,\Delta G_{\text{CH}_2\text{O}}}{\bar E_{\text{ph}}} = \Phi\,\Delta G_{\text{CH}_2\text{O}}\,\kappa \]

the quantum yield is the moles of CO₂ fixed per mole of absorbed photons, the reciprocal of the number of photons needed per CO₂. The energy-conversion efficiency is the quantum yield times the energy stored per mole of CH₂O divided by the mean energy of a mole of photons — or, equivalently, times the number of moles of photons per joule of light.

Common misconception

"Plants are only 1 % efficient, so there must be a trick to make them ten times better." The theoretical maxima (4.6 % for C3 and 6.0 % for C4 of total solar radiation; 9.4 % and 12.3 % of PAR) are set by physics and stoichiometry, not by carelessness. Field crops over a whole season reach roughly a third of these values, mainly because their leaves are light-saturated at midday and the canopy is not closed for much of the season (Zhu et al., 2010). The realistic targets are therefore less light saturation, less photorespiration, faster canopy closure and faster recovery from photoprotection — precisely the variables a controlled environment can manipulate.

The Calvin–Benson cycle and Rubisco's dilemma

The stroma runs a cyclic metabolic pathway, worked out by Melvin Calvin, Andrew Benson and James Bassham with ¹⁴CO₂ in the 1950s. It has three phases (Figure 6):

  1. Carboxylation. The enzyme ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase — Rubisco — adds CO₂ to the five-carbon sugar ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). The unstable six-carbon product immediately splits into two molecules of the three-carbon acid 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Because the first stable product has three carbons, plants that fix CO₂ only this way are called C3 plants.
  2. Reduction. Using ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, 3-PGA is reduced to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (a triose phosphate).
  3. Regeneration. Five of every six triose phosphates are rearranged, at the cost of more ATP, back into three RuBP. The sixth is the net product, exported to the cytosol to make sucrose or kept in the chloroplast to make starch.

Summing over three turns: 3 CO₂ + 9 ATP + 6 NADPH → 1 triose phosphate + 9 ADP + 8 Pᵢ + 6 NADP⁺, i.e., 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂, the numbers used in Worked example 1.

Rubisco is probably the most abundant protein on Earth (Ellis, 1979), and for a good reason: it is slow, catalysing only a few reactions per second per active site, so leaves need a lot of it — a large share of leaf nitrogen is invested in Rubisco (Evans, 2013). Worse, its active site cannot fully discriminate between CO₂ and O₂. When O₂ is added to RuBP instead of CO₂ (oxygenation), the products are one 3-PGA and one 2-phosphoglycolate, a two-carbon compound that is useless to the cycle and toxic if it accumulates. Recycling it — photorespiration — costs ATP and reducing power and releases previously fixed CO₂.

The balance between the two reactions depends on the ratio of CO₂ to O₂ at Rubisco and on Rubisco's specificity. Farquhar, von Caemmerer and Berry (1980) captured it in a single parameter, the CO₂ compensation point in the absence of day respiration, Γ* (gamma-star): the CO₂ concentration at which photorespiratory CO₂ release exactly equals carboxylation.

Oxygenation-to-carboxylation ratio and Γ*(2.1.5)
\[ \phi = \frac{v_o}{v_c} = \frac{2\,\Gamma^*}{C}, \qquad \Gamma^* = \frac{0.5\,O}{S_{c/o}}, \qquad A_{\text{gross}} = v_c - 0.5\,v_o = v_c\left(1-\frac{\Gamma^*}{C}\right) \]

the ratio of oxygenation to carboxylation equals twice Γ* divided by the CO₂ concentration at Rubisco. Γ* equals half the O₂ concentration divided by Rubisco's specificity for CO₂ over O₂. Because every two oxygenations release one CO₂, the net CO₂ fixation is the carboxylation rate reduced by the fraction Γ*/C.

Follow the derivation
  1. CO₂ and O₂ compete for the same active site. For competitive substrates the ratio of reaction rates is proportional to the ratio of concentrations: \(v_c/v_o = S_{c/o}\,C/O\), which defines the specificity factor.
  2. Each oxygenation produces one 2-phosphoglycolate; two of them are needed to make one serine, and that step releases one CO₂. So photorespiratory CO₂ release is \(0.5\,v_o\).
  3. Net fixation (before mitochondrial respiration): \(A = v_c - 0.5v_o = v_c(1 - 0.5\,v_o/v_c) = v_c(1 - 0.5\,O/(S_{c/o}C))\).
  4. Define \(\Gamma^* = 0.5\,O/S_{c/o}\): then \(A = v_c(1-\Gamma^*/C)\) and \(\phi = v_o/v_c = O/(S_{c/o}C) = 2\Gamma^*/C\). At \(C=\Gamma^*\), \(A=0\).
  5. Numbers: with \(\Gamma^*\) = 42.75 and \(O\) = 210 000 µmol mol⁻¹, \(S_{c/o} = 0.5\times210\,000/42.75 = 2456\). At \(C_i\) = 280 µmol mol⁻¹, \(\phi = 0.31\): roughly one oxygenation for every three carboxylations, and 15 % of the carbon fixed is lost again.
Assumptions and validity

C is the CO₂ concentration at Rubisco. Because CO₂ must diffuse from the intercellular spaces into the chloroplast (mesophyll conductance), the chloroplast concentration is somewhat lower than \(C_i\); using \(C_i\) is the common "apparent" simplification. Γ* rises steeply with temperature because CO₂ solubility falls faster than O₂ solubility and Rubisco specificity declines (Eq. 2.1.10).

The consequences are large. Walker et al. Research frontier

Can we engineer our way around Rubisco's dilemma? South et al. (2019) installed synthetic glycolate-metabolism pathways in tobacco chloroplasts and blocked glycolate export; several lines produced more than 40 % more biomass in replicated field trials. Other groups accelerate the relaxation of photoprotection when leaves pass from sun to shade: this raised tobacco biomass by about 15 % (Kromdijk et al., 2016) and soybean seed yield by up to a third in one of two seasons (De Souza et al., 2022). In controlled environments, the simplest "engineering" solution to photorespiration is much older: raise the CO₂ concentration.

C3, C4 and CAM: three solutions to one problem

Photorespiration is worst when CO₂ inside the leaf is low — in hot, bright, dry conditions when stomata close. Several plant lineages evolved mechanisms that pump CO₂ to Rubisco (Figure 7). C4 photosynthesis evolved independently many times (Sage, 2004), a textbook example of convergent evolution.

  • C4 photosynthesis (maize, sugarcane, sorghum, millets, amaranth) first fixes bicarbonate in the mesophyll with PEP carboxylase, an enzyme with no oxygenase activity and a high affinity for its substrate. The resulting four-carbon acid (hence "C4") diffuses to a ring of bundle-sheath cells around the veins — Kranz anatomy (Figure 8) — where it is decarboxylated. CO₂ builds up to several times the ambient concentration around Rubisco, which is confined to the bundle sheath, and oxygenation is nearly suppressed. The price is about two extra ATP per CO₂ in the most common (NADP-malic enzyme) subtype (Zhu et al., 2010).
  • Crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) (pineapple, agave, Opuntia, Kalanchoe) separates the two carboxylations in time instead of space: stomata open at night, when evaporative demand is low, and CO₂ is stored as malic acid in the vacuole; during the day the stomata close and the malate is decarboxylated behind closed doors. CAM plants lose very little water but grow slowly.

The light-response curve

If you clamp a leaf in a gas-exchange instrument, set the CO₂ and temperature, and step the PPFD from high to low, you obtain a light-response curve (Figure 9). It has three regions: below the light compensation point the leaf respires more CO₂ than it fixes; at low PPFD the net rate rises linearly with a slope equal to the quantum yield; at high PPFD the curve bends over towards a plateau set by the capacity of the enzymes. The standard empirical description is the non-rectangular hyperbola (Thornley, 1976; Marshall & Biscoe, 1980), a curve with only four parameters, each with a physiological meaning.

Non-rectangular hyperbola (leaf light response)(2.1.6)
\[ \theta P^2 - (\phi I + P_{\max})\,P + \phi I P_{\max} = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; A = \frac{\phi I + P_{\max} - \sqrt{(\phi I + P_{\max})^2 - 4\theta\phi I P_{\max}}}{2\theta} - R_d \]

gross photosynthesis P is the smaller root of a quadratic that blends two limits — a light-limited line φI and a capacity limit Pmax — with the curvature θ deciding how sharply they merge. Net assimilation A is gross photosynthesis minus day respiration.

Follow the derivation
  1. Two simple limits bound photosynthesis: at low light it cannot exceed \(\phi I\); at high light it cannot exceed \(P_{\max}\). The simplest combination, \(P = \min(\phi I, P_{\max})\) (Blackman response), has an unrealistic sharp corner.
  2. Write the corner as the product of the two "shortfalls": \((\phi I - P)(P_{\max}-P) = 0\). Expanding: \(P^2 - (\phi I+P_{\max})P + \phi I P_{\max}=0\).
  3. Soften the corner by multiplying the \(P^2\) term by \(\theta\): \(\theta P^2 - (\phi I+P_{\max})P + \phi I P_{\max}=0\). With \(\theta=1\) we recover the Blackman corner; with \(\theta = 0\) the equation becomes linear, \(P = \phi IP_{\max}/(\phi I+P_{\max})\), the rectangular hyperbola (Michaelis–Menten shape).
  4. Solve the quadratic with the formula \(P = [b - \sqrt{b^2-4ac}]/(2a)\), \(a=\theta\), \(b = \phi I + P_{\max}\), \(c = \phi IP_{\max}\). The minus sign gives the physically meaningful root (\(P \to 0\) as \(I\to0\)).
  5. Check the limits: for \(I\to 0\), \(P\approx\phi I\) (slope φ); for \(I\to\infty\), \(P\to P_{\max}\). Subtract \(R_d\) for net assimilation.
Assumptions and validity

The curve is empirical: its parameters change with CO₂, temperature, leaf age and growth light. φ here is based on incident light (multiply the absorbed-basis quantum yield by leaf absorptance). \(R_d\) in the light is somewhat lower than respiration measured in darkness (the Kok effect). The FvCB model (Section 2.1.8) explains why \(P_{\max}\) and φ change with CO₂.

Each term under the square root has units (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹)², so \(A\) is in µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ ✓ (φ is mol mol⁻¹, dimensionless).

\[ I_c = \frac{R_d\,(P_{\max}-\theta R_d)}{\phi\,(P_{\max}-R_d)}, \qquad I_{f} = \frac{f\,P_{\max}\,(1-\theta f)}{\phi\,(1-f)} \]

A biochemical model: Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry

The light-response curve describes; it does not explain. Why does \(P_{\max}\) rise when CO₂ is enriched? Why does the quantum yield fall at high temperature? In 1980 Graham Farquhar, Susanne von Caemmerer and Joseph Berry published a model built from the biochemistry you have just learnt. More than four decades later, the FvCB model is still the standard leaf model in crop simulators, Earth-system models and greenhouse climate controllers. Its central idea is that net photosynthesis is the minimum of rates set by three potentially limiting processes:

  • Rubisco-limited (\(A_c\)): enough RuBP and energy, but the carboxylation capacity of Rubisco limits — typical at low CO₂ and high light.
  • RuBP-regeneration (electron-transport)-limited (\(A_j\)): Rubisco could work faster, but the light reactions cannot supply ATP and NADPH fast enough to regenerate RuBP — typical at low light or high CO₂.
  • Triose-phosphate-use-limited (\(A_p\)): the leaf cannot export and use triose phosphate fast enough to recycle phosphate — seen at very high CO₂ and light, or low temperature (Sharkey, 1985).
Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model of C3 photosynthesis(2.1.8)
\[ A_c = \frac{V_{c\max}\,(C_i-\Gamma^*)}{C_i + K_c\,(1+O/K_o)}, \qquad A_j = \frac{J\,(C_i-\Gamma^*)}{4\,C_i + 8\,\Gamma^*}, \qquad A_p = 3\,T_p \] \[ A = \min\{A_c,\; A_j,\; A_p\} - R_d \]

the Rubisco-limited rate is a Michaelis–Menten function of CO₂ with O₂ as a competitive inhibitor, multiplied by the photorespiration factor. The electron-transport-limited rate is the electron flow J divided by the electrons needed per net CO₂ fixed. The triose-phosphate-limited rate is three times the triose-phosphate utilisation rate. Net assimilation is the smallest of the three, minus day respiration.

From leaf to canopy: implications for controlled environments

A crop is not one leaf. In a lettuce canopy the top leaves may be exposed to 400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ while the lower leaves receive a few tens. Because each leaf follows a curved light response, the photosynthesis of the canopy is not the leaf response evaluated at the mean light. Two consequences matter in practice:

  1. Canopies saturate at much higher PPFD than leaves. When extra light arrives, the upper, near-saturated leaves gain little, but the shaded leaves below still operate on the steep, efficient initial slope. The canopy light response is therefore closer to linear than the leaf response. Sunlit/shaded "two-big-leaf" models (de Pury & Farquhar, 1997) capture this by splitting the canopy into a sunlit and a shaded fraction, each with its own FvCB calculation. You will build the canopy light-interception side of this — the Beer–Lambert law — in Lesson 2.4.
  2. Light use efficiency is highest at low PPFD. Per photon, a canopy under modest light is more efficient than one under intense light. In a vertical farm this creates a genuine optimisation problem: more photons mean more growth per square metre (and per unit of rent and labour), but fewer grams per kilowatt-hour. Kelly et al. (2020) found, for example, that at the same daily light integral of 15.6 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, lettuce grown under a lower PPFD with a longer photoperiod produced more biomass than lettuce under a higher PPFD and shorter photoperiod — a canopy-level expression of the curved light response.

Why CO₂ enrichment and high PPFD belong together

Put the pieces together. At ambient CO₂ and high light, a C3 leaf is Rubisco-limited (\(A_c\)), and adding photons does little; at high CO₂ the Rubisco limitation is lifted and the leaf becomes limited by electron transport (\(A_j\)), which is exactly the process that more photons relieve. Enrichment also suppresses photorespiration, which raises the quantum yield — the efficiency of every photon, including those absorbed by shaded leaves. And because Γ* and photorespiration rise with temperature, enrichment raises the temperature optimum of photosynthesis. In other words: CO₂ makes light more valuable, and light makes CO₂ more valuable.

Experiments confirm the synergy. Kimball's (1983) analysis of 430 observations found that doubling the atmospheric CO₂ concentration increased crop yields by about a third on average. Frantz et al. (2004) grew lettuce in growth chambers with CO₂ at 1200 µmol mol⁻¹ and PPFD up to 1000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (57.6 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, two to three times the usual level). Elevated CO₂ raised the temperature optimum from 25 to 30 °C, the higher temperature accelerated leaf expansion and radiation capture, and the best treatment reached 19 g m⁻² d⁻¹ of dry biomass — a four-fold increase over conventional conditions — once they had eliminated tipburn by blowing air directly onto the growing point (a calcium-transport problem you will meet in Lesson 2.2).

Engineering caveats

Enrichment is only worth paying for while the CO₂ stays in the building. In a ventilated greenhouse much of the injected CO₂ is blown out, so enrichment set-points are usually lowered when vents open; in an airtight plant factory almost all CO₂ is either fixed or remains inside, so high set-points are cheap. Stomata also partly close at high CO₂, which lowers transpiration and can aggravate calcium-related disorders such as tipburn. The economic optimum is a mass-balance question, treated in Lesson 8.3 and the CO₂ enrichment lab.

History

The sequence of discoveries behind this lesson spans three centuries: Jan Ingenhousz (1779) showed that plants "purify" air only in light; Robert Emerson and William Arnold (1932) found that about 2500 chlorophylls cooperate per O₂ released, the first hint of antennae and reaction centres; Robert Hill and Fay Bendall (1960) proposed the Z-scheme; Melvin Calvin received the 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the carbon-fixation pathway; Hal Hatch and Roger Slack (1966) described the C4 pathway; and Farquhar, von Caemmerer and Berry (1980) turned the biochemistry into the equations you used above.

Key terms

Thylakoid
Flattened membrane sac inside the chloroplast that carries the light-reaction complexes and separates the lumen from the stroma.
Photosystem II (PSII)
Light-driven water:plastoquinone oxidoreductase with reaction centre P680 and the Mn₄CaO₅ oxygen-evolving complex.
Photosystem I (PSI)
Light-driven plastocyanin:ferredoxin oxidoreductase with reaction centre P700; produces the strongest biological reductant.
Cytochrome b₆f
Membrane complex that transfers electrons from plastoquinol to plastocyanin and, through the Q cycle, pumps two protons per electron into the lumen.
Z-scheme
Diagram of linear electron flow on a redox-potential scale, showing two photosystems acting in series.
Proton-motive force
Electrochemical driving force of protons across a membrane, pmf = Δψ + (2.303RT/F)ΔpH; powers ATP synthase (chemiosmosis).
Quantum yield
Moles of CO₂ fixed (or O₂ evolved) per mole of photons absorbed; its reciprocal is the quantum requirement (≥ 8).
Calvin–Benson cycle
Stromal pathway that fixes CO₂ into triose phosphate using 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂.
Rubisco
Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase; fixes CO₂ onto RuBP but also reacts with O₂.
Photorespiration
Recycling of 2-phosphoglycolate formed by Rubisco oxygenation; releases CO₂ and NH₃ and costs ATP and reductant.
Γ* (CO₂ photocompensation point)
CO₂ concentration at which photorespiratory CO₂ release equals Rubisco carboxylation; ≈ 43 µmol mol⁻¹ at 25 °C.
C4 photosynthesis
CO₂-concentrating pathway in which PEP carboxylase fixes carbon in the mesophyll and Rubisco works in the bundle sheath (Kranz anatomy).
CAM
Crassulacean acid metabolism: nocturnal CO₂ uptake stored as malic acid, decarboxylated behind closed stomata in the day.
Light compensation point
PPFD at which gross photosynthesis balances respiration so that net CO₂ exchange is zero.
FvCB model
Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model: net assimilation as the minimum of Rubisco-, RuBP-regeneration- and TPU-limited rates minus respiration.
