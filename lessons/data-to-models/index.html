From data to models: regression and growth curves

Your grow log is a column of numbers: leaf area on day 3, day 6, day 8… A model turns those numbers into understanding and foresight — how fast the crop is growing, when it will slow down, and on which day it will reach harvest size. This lesson builds the toolkit from first principles: why least squares works, what the parameters of a growth curve mean, how to tell a good fit from an over-fitted one, and how to forecast your own harvest date with an honest uncertainty interval.

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish mechanistic, empirical and hybrid models, and choose the right kind for a question and a data set (LO5).
  • Derive the least-squares estimates of a straight line from the normal equations and calculate slope, intercept, R², RMSE and MAE by hand and in the browser (LO4, LO5).
  • Write down, interpret and evaluate the exponential, logistic, Gompertz, Richards and expolinear growth functions, and calculate relative growth rate and doubling time (LO3, LO5).
  • Explain how non-linear least squares (Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt) finds parameters, and diagnose a fit with residual plots (LO4, LO5).
  • Detect over-fitting with a train/test split, k-fold cross-validation and the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) (LO5).
  • Fit growth curves to your own grow-log data and forecast a harvest date with a bootstrap uncertainty interval (LO4, LO5).

Why model? Mechanistic, empirical and hybrid models

A model is a simplified, quantitative description of a system that lets you answer a question you could not answer from the raw data alone. In controlled-environment agriculture the questions are practical: Is my crop growing as fast as it should? Which of two nutrient recipes gives faster growth? When will the lettuce reach 150 g, so I can book the harvest crew and the buyer? Answering them requires turning a handful of noisy measurements into a smooth, interpretable relationship — and knowing how far to trust it.

Models sit on a spectrum from mechanistic (“white-box”) to empirical (“black-box”). A mechanistic model is built from physical, chemical and biological laws: photons are intercepted by leaves following the Beer–Lambert law, converted to sugars with a known quantum yield, respired at a temperature-dependent rate and allocated to new leaves. The lettuce model of Van Henten (1994), which drives the lettuce digital twin, is of this kind: two state variables (structural and non-structural dry weight) and a dozen parameters, each with a physiological meaning and a unit. An empirical model makes no claim about mechanism; it is a flexible function chosen because it describes the shape of the data. A straight line through a calibration plot, a logistic curve through a series of leaf-area measurements, or a neural network that maps climate records to yield are all empirical. Hybrid (grey-box) models combine the two — for example a mechanistic crop model whose most uncertain parameter is re-estimated every day from camera images, or a neural network that learns only the residual that the mechanistic model cannot explain.

History

George Box, a statistician who worked on industrial experiments, wrote in 1976: “Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a ‘correct’ one by excessive elaboration.” His point was not pessimism but economy: seek the simplest model that is useful for the question at hand, and worry about the errors that matter (Box, 1976). This lesson gives you the tools — residual plots, cross-validation and information criteria — to decide when a model is useful enough.

The line of best fit: least squares from first principles

Suppose you harvested five lettuce plants, photographed each from above before cutting, and weighed them. You want a rule that converts projected leaf area \(x\) (cm²) into fresh mass \(y\) (g), so that in future you can estimate mass from photos alone. A straight line \(\hat y = a + b\,x\) is the simplest candidate. But which line? Every choice of intercept \(a\) and slope \(b\) leaves a vertical miss for each plant, the residual \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\). Some residuals are positive, some negative. We need one number that measures the total misfit, and then we choose the line that makes it as small as possible.

Adding residuals directly does not work: positive and negative misses cancel, and infinitely many lines give a sum of zero. Adding absolute values works but has a kink at zero that makes the mathematics awkward. Squaring the residuals is smooth, penalises large misses heavily and — as we shall see — leads to a solution that can be written down exactly. This is the method of least squares, published by Legendre in 1805 and claimed by Gauss, who said he had used it since 1795 and who famously applied it in 1801 to predict where the dwarf planet Ceres would reappear from behind the Sun (Stigler, 1981).

Least-squares objective(10.1.1)
\[ \text{SSE}(a,b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^{2} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - a - b\,x_i\bigr)^{2} \]

the sum of squared errors is the sum, over all \(n\) observations, of the squared vertical distance between each measured value and the line. The least-squares line is the pair \((a,b)\) that makes this sum as small as possible.

Why squares? Three justifications
  1. Geometry. SSE is the squared Euclidean distance between the vector of observations and the vector of predictions. Minimising it is an orthogonal projection — the most natural notion of “closest”.
  2. Probability. If the errors are independent and normally distributed with constant variance \(\sigma^2\), the likelihood of the data is proportional to \(\exp(-\text{SSE}/2\sigma^2)\). Maximising the likelihood is therefore identical to minimising SSE: least squares is the maximum-likelihood estimator under Gaussian noise.
  3. Algebra. SSE is a smooth quadratic bowl in \((a,b)\). Its minimum is where both partial derivatives vanish, which gives two linear equations with an exact solution (Eq. 10.1.2). Absolute errors (minimising the sum of \(|e_i|\), “least absolute deviations”) are more robust to outliers but need an iterative algorithm.
Assumptions and validity

Least squares treats \(x\) as known exactly and puts all the error in \(y\). If both variables are noisy (for example two sensors compared with each other), ordinary least squares underestimates the slope (“regression dilution”). Least squares is sensitive to outliers because a residual of 10 contributes 100 to SSE while ten residuals of 1 contribute only 10.

(g − g − g cm⁻² × cm²)² summed → g². SSE carries the squared unit of the response, which is why its square root (RMSE, Eq. 10.1.3) is easier to interpret. ✓

Before we solve the minimisation analytically, try it by hand. In Figure 2 each plant's residual is drawn as a vertical segment, and its square literally as a square: the total shaded area is the SSE. Drag the two handles to move the line and make the shaded area as small as you can.

Now the analytical solution. At the minimum of a smooth function of two variables, both partial derivatives are zero. Differentiating Eq. 10.1.1 with respect to \(a\) and \(b\) and setting the results to zero gives the normal equations:

Normal equations and the least-squares line(10.1.2)
\[ \hat b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^{2}}, \qquad \hat a = \bar y - \hat b\,\bar x \]

the best slope is the sum of the cross-products of the deviations of \(x\) and \(y\) from their means, divided by the sum of squared deviations of \(x\). The best intercept makes the line pass exactly through the centre of mass of the data, the point \((\bar x, \bar y)\).

\(\bar x,\ \bar y\)sample means of \(x\) and \(y\)cm², g
\(S_{xx}\)sum of squared deviations of \(x\)cm⁴
\(S_{xy}\)sum of cross-products of deviationsg cm²

Follow the derivation
  1. Differentiate SSE with respect to \(a\), using the chain rule on each squared term: \(\dfrac{\partial\,\text{SSE}}{\partial a} = \sum_i 2\,(y_i - a - b x_i)\cdot(-1) = -2\sum_i e_i\).
  2. Setting it to zero: \(\sum_i e_i = 0\), i.e. \(\sum y_i = n a + b\sum x_i\). Dividing by \(n\): \(\bar y = a + b\bar x\). The residuals of a least-squares line with an intercept always sum to zero, and the line passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\).
  3. Differentiate with respect to \(b\): \(\dfrac{\partial\,\text{SSE}}{\partial b} = -2\sum_i x_i\,e_i = 0\), i.e. \(\sum x_i y_i = a\sum x_i + b\sum x_i^2\). The residuals are uncorrelated with the predictor.
  4. Steps 2 and 3 are the two normal equations — two linear equations in two unknowns. Substitute \(a = \bar y - b\bar x\) from step 2 into step 3: \(\sum x_i y_i - n\bar x\bar y = b\,(\sum x_i^2 - n\bar x^2)\).
  5. Recognise the two brackets: \(\sum x_i y_i - n\bar x\bar y = \sum (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y) = S_{xy}\) and \(\sum x_i^2 - n\bar x^2 = S_{xx}\). Hence \(\hat b = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(\hat a = \bar y - \hat b\bar x\).
  6. It is a minimum, not a maximum: the second derivatives \(\partial^2/\partial a^2 = 2n > 0\) and the determinant of the Hessian \(4(n\sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2) = 4nS_{xx} > 0\) whenever the \(x_i\) are not all equal.
General form: many predictors and polynomials (matrix notation)

Stack the observations in a vector \(\mathbf y\) and the predictors in a design matrix \(\mathbf X\) whose first column is all ones (for the intercept) and whose other columns hold \(x\), \(x^2\), a second sensor, a treatment indicator… The model is \(\mathbf y = \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta + \mathbf e\) and \(\text{SSE} = (\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)\). Setting the gradient \(-2\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y - \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)\) to zero gives the normal equations in matrix form,

\[ \mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y . \]

This single formula fits multiple regression and polynomial regression (\(\hat y = \beta_0+\beta_1 x+\beta_2x^2+\dots\)): a polynomial is non-linear in \(x\) but linear in its parameters, which is what matters. Software solves the system with numerically stable factorisations (QR) rather than an explicit inverse; the course's ols() function does exactly this calculation.

\([S_{xy}/S_{xx}] = \text{g cm}^2/\text{cm}^4 = \text{g cm}^{-2}\) — the unit of a slope is always (unit of \(y\))/(unit of \(x\)). \([\bar y - \hat b\bar x] = \text{g} - \text{g cm}^{-2}\cdot\text{cm}^2 = \text{g}\) ✓

Goodness of fit and residual analysis

Least squares always returns a line, even through data that are not linear at all. We therefore need numbers that summarise how well the model fits, and — more importantly — pictures that reveal how it fails. The three standard summaries are the coefficient of determination \(R^2\), the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and the mean absolute error (MAE).

Goodness-of-fit statistics(10.1.3)
\[ R^{2} = 1-\frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1-\frac{\sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2}, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}}, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \lvert y_i-\hat y_i\rvert \]

\(R^2\) is the fraction of the total variation of \(y\) around its mean that the model explains. RMSE is the typical size of a residual, in the units of \(y\), with large errors weighted more heavily. MAE is the average absolute miss, less sensitive to a single large error.

SSTtotal sum of squares: variation of \(y\) around its mean (the SSE of the “no-model” model \(\hat y = \bar y\))unit of \(y\) squared
\(R^2\)coefficient of determination– (0 to 1 for linear least squares with intercept)

Why is \(R^2\) between 0 and 1? And why it can be negative
  1. For least squares with an intercept, the total variation splits exactly into an explained and an unexplained part: \(\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE}\), with \(\text{SSR}=\sum(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2\). The cross-term vanishes because the residuals are uncorrelated with the fitted values (normal equations).
  2. Hence \(R^2 = \text{SSR}/\text{SST} \in [0,1]\). For a straight line \(R^2\) equals the square of Pearson's correlation coefficient \(r\).
  3. For a non-linear model, a model without intercept, or a model evaluated on new data, the split does not hold and \(1-\text{SSE}/\text{SST}\) can be negative: the model is then worse than predicting the mean.
Degrees of freedom: \(n\) or \(n-p\)?

RMSE as defined here divides by \(n\) and describes the fit to these data. The residual standard error \(s=\sqrt{\text{SSE}/(n-p)}\), with \(p\) fitted parameters, is an unbiased estimate of the noise standard deviation \(\sigma\) and is used for confidence intervals. With five points and a line (\(p=2\)) the difference is a factor \(\sqrt{5/3}=1.29\). Always state which one you report.

\(R^2\): g²/g² = dimensionless ✓. RMSE: \(\sqrt{\text{g}^2}\) = g ✓. MAE: g ✓.

In Worked example 1, SST = 6126.8 g² and SSE = 28.2 g², so \(R^2 = 0.995\), RMSE = \(\sqrt{28.2/5} = 2.38\) g and MAE = 2.24 g. An RMSE of 2.4 g on plants of 40–140 g means that a photo-based mass estimate is typically within about 2–3 g — useful to know before you rely on it.

Common misconception

“My \(R^2\) is 0.98, so the model is correct.” A high \(R^2\) says only that the model follows the data better than a horizontal line. Growth data that rise steadily with time give \(R^2 > 0.95\) for almost any increasing function, including clearly wrong ones. \(R^2\) also rises automatically as you add parameters, so it cannot be used to choose between models of different complexity. Look at the residuals, and use cross-validation or AICc (Sections 10.1.6–10.1.7).

A residual plot — residuals against fitted values or against time — is the single most informative diagnostic. If the model is adequate, the residuals look like random scatter around zero with constant spread. Systematic patterns tell you what is wrong: a curve (U or arch) means the functional form is wrong; a funnel means the noise grows with the mean (heteroscedasticity — typical of biological growth, where a 400 cm² plant is measured with a larger absolute error than a 20 cm² seedling); a single isolated point means an outlier or a data-entry error; and runs of positive residuals followed by runs of negative ones in time-ordered data mean autocorrelation. Anscombe (1973) famously constructed four data sets with identical means, variances, correlations and regression lines that look completely different when plotted. Figure 3 lets you play the same game.

Growth curves: shape, parameters and meaning

Plant growth is not linear. A seedling grows in proportion to its own size, because more leaf area intercepts more light and makes more new leaf area — compound interest. Later, leaves shade each other, the canopy closes, respiration of the growing mass increases and the plant approaches a size limit set by the container, the light supply or the grower's harvest. The result is the familiar S-shaped (sigmoid) growth curve. Growth analysts describe it with a small family of functions whose parameters have direct biological meaning (Hunt, 1982; Paine et al., 2012).

Exponential growth and relative growth rate

If every gram of plant produces new mass at the same rate, the growth rate is proportional to size, \(\mathrm{d}W/\mathrm{d}t = rW\). The proportionality constant is the relative growth rate (RGR), the most fundamental number in plant growth analysis.

Exponential growth and relative growth rate(10.1.4)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = r\,W \;\Rightarrow\; W(t) = W_0\,e^{r t}, \qquad \text{RGR} = \frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\ln W_2-\ln W_1}{t_2-t_1}, \qquad t_{d} = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \]

under exponential growth the biomass multiplies by the same factor in every equal time step. The relative growth rate is the growth per unit of existing biomass; between two harvests it is estimated from the difference of the natural logarithms of the masses divided by the time between them. The doubling time is ln 2 divided by the RGR.

Follow the derivation
  1. Separate the variables: \(\mathrm{d}W/W = r\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  2. Integrate from \((0, W_0)\) to \((t, W)\): \(\ln W - \ln W_0 = r\,t\), so \(W = W_0 e^{rt}\).
  3. Taking logarithms, \(\ln W = \ln W_0 + r t\): on a log scale exponential growth is a straight line with slope \(r\). You can therefore estimate RGR by linear regression of \(\ln W\) on \(t\) (Eq. 10.1.2) — the “classical” approach of Hunt et al. (2002).
  4. Between two harvests, RGR is the slope of the line through two points on the log scale: \((\ln W_2 - \ln W_1)/(t_2-t_1)\). This is exact for exponential growth and equals the mean RGR over the interval otherwise.
  5. Doubling: \(2W_0 = W_0e^{rt_d}\Rightarrow t_d = \ln 2/r \approx 0.693/r\).
Assumptions and validity

Constant RGR holds only for young, unshaded plants. In practice RGR declines steadily with size — even in seedlings — because an increasing fraction of biomass is structural (stems, veins) rather than photosynthetic leaf. Averaging RGR over long intervals therefore hides a trend; that is why sigmoid models are preferred for whole-crop data (Paine et al., 2012). Fitting \(\ln W\) by linear regression also assumes multiplicative (log-normal) errors, which is often realistic for growth data.

\([\ln W_2 - \ln W_1] = \ln(W_2/W_1)\) is dimensionless; divided by days gives d⁻¹ ✓. \(t_d\): 1/d⁻¹ = d ✓.

The logistic curve

The simplest way to stop exponential growth is to let the relative growth rate fall linearly as the plant approaches a maximum size \(K\): \(\mathrm{d}W/\mathrm{d}t = rW(1-W/K)\). The term \((1-W/K)\) is the unused fraction of the “carrying capacity” — the same equation Verhulst proposed for populations in 1838 and that you will meet again for microbial growth (Lesson 12.3).

Logistic growth(10.1.5)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = rW\Bigl(1-\frac{W}{K}\Bigr) \;\Rightarrow\; W(t) = \frac{K}{1+e^{-r\,(t-t_i)}}, \qquad \Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t}\Bigr)_{\max} = \frac{rK}{4}\ \text{at}\ t=t_i,\ W=\frac{K}{2} \]

biomass rises along a symmetric S-curve from near zero to the asymptote \(K\). The curve is steepest at the inflection time \(t_i\), when the plant has reached half its final size; the maximum absolute growth rate there is one quarter of \(r\) times \(K\).

  1. Separate: \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}W}{W(1-W/K)} = r\,\mathrm{d}t\). Partial fractions: \(\dfrac{1}{W(1-W/K)} = \dfrac1W + \dfrac{1/K}{1-W/K}\).
  2. Integrate: \(\ln W - \ln(1-W/K) = rt + C\), i.e. \(\ln\dfrac{W}{K-W} = r(t-t_i)\) where the constant is written as \(-rt_i\).
  3. Solve for \(W\): \(\dfrac{W}{K-W} = e^{r(t-t_i)} \Rightarrow W = \dfrac{K}{1+e^{-r(t-t_i)}}\). At \(t=t_i\), \(W=K/2\).
  4. Growth rate \(g(W)=rW-rW^2/K\) is a downward parabola in \(W\); its maximum is where \(\mathrm dg/\mathrm dW = r-2rW/K = 0\), i.e. \(W = K/2\), giving \(g_{\max} = rK/4\).
  5. The RGR, \(r(1-W/K)\), falls linearly with size from \(r\) to 0 — a testable prediction: plot RGR against \(W\) from your data.
Assumptions and validity

The logistic curve is symmetric around \(t_i\): it approaches \(K\) as fast as it left zero. Many crops slow down more gradually than they started, which the Gompertz and Richards curves capture better. \(K\) is only well estimated if the data include the saturation phase — with data from the exponential phase only, \(K\) is almost unconstrained (you will see this in Figure 9).

\(r\,(t-t_i)\): d⁻¹ × d = dimensionless, as required for an exponent ✓. \(rK/4\): d⁻¹ × cm² = cm² d⁻¹ ✓.

Gompertz, Richards and expolinear curves

Three further functions cover almost every growth curve you will meet. The Gompertz curve has an exponentially decaying RGR and an asymmetric shape: its inflection lies at only \(K/e \approx 0.37K\), so growth slows early and tails off slowly — typical of many leafy crops and of bacterial growth (Zwietering et al., 1990). The Richards curve adds a shape parameter \(\nu\) that moves the inflection continuously, containing the logistic (\(\nu=1\)) and the Gompertz (\(\nu\to0\)) as special cases (Richards, 1959; Tjørve & Tjørve, 2010). The expolinear curve of Goudriaan and Monteith (1990) is mechanistically motivated: growth is exponential while the canopy is open and becomes linear once it closes and intercepts nearly all light — a shape that matches crops such as lettuce grown at high density.

Gompertz, Richards and expolinear growth functions(10.1.6)
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Gompertz:}\quad & W = K\exp\!\bigl[-e^{-r(t-t_i)}\bigr] \\ \text{Richards:}\quad & W = K\bigl[1+\nu\,e^{-r(t-t_i)}\bigr]^{-1/\nu} \\ \text{Expolinear:}\quad & W = \frac{c_m}{r_m}\ln\!\bigl[1+e^{\,r_m (t-t_b)}\bigr] \end{aligned} \]

the Gompertz curve rises to \(K\) with its steepest point at \(K/e\); the Richards curve does the same with an adjustable inflection height set by \(\nu\); the expolinear curve starts exponentially with relative rate \(r_m\) and becomes a straight line with slope \(c_m\) that, extended backwards, crosses zero at \(t_b\).

\(t_b\)time at which the linear asymptote crosses zero (“lost time”)d
Follow the derivations
  1. Gompertz. Let the RGR itself decay exponentially: \(\frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = r\,e^{-r(t-t_i)}\). Integrating \(\mathrm d\ln W\) gives \(\ln W = \ln K - e^{-r(t-t_i)}\), i.e. the formula above. At \(t_i\), \(W = K e^{-1}\) and the maximum growth rate is \(rK/e\).
  2. Richards. Differentiate: \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = rK E(1+\nu E)^{-1/\nu-1}\) with \(E = e^{-r(t-t_i)}\). Setting the second derivative to zero gives \(E=1\), so \(t_i\) is exactly the inflection time and \(W(t_i) = K(1+\nu)^{-1/\nu}\). With \(\nu=1\) this is \(K/2\) (logistic); as \(\nu\to0\), \((1+\nu E)^{-1/\nu}\to e^{-E}\) (Gompertz, \(K/e\)).
  3. Zwietering form. Microbiologists write the Gompertz curve with a maximum rate \(\mu_m = rK/e\) and a lag time \(\lambda = t_i - 1/r\) (the time at which the tangent through the inflection point crosses zero): \(W = K\exp\{-\exp[\mu_m e(\lambda-t)/K + 1]\}\). It is the same curve, reparameterised so that each parameter answers a practical question (Zwietering et al., 1990).
  4. Expolinear. Goudriaan and Monteith (1990) assumed crop growth rate is proportional to intercepted light, which rises with leaf area and saturates as the canopy closes. For \(t \ll t_b\), \(\ln(1+e^x)\approx e^x\), so \(W\approx (c_m/r_m)e^{r_m(t-t_b)}\) — exponential with RGR \(r_m\). For \(t\gg t_b\), \(\ln(1+e^x)\approx x\), so \(W\approx c_m(t-t_b)\) — linear with slope \(c_m\).
Assumptions and validity

The Richards shape parameter \(\nu\) is notoriously poorly determined by typical data (strong correlation with \(r\) and \(t_i\)); fit it only when you have many dates spanning the whole curve, and compare it with the simpler curves using AICc (Eq. 10.1.10). The expolinear curve has no upper asymptote: it describes growth up to commercial harvest, not senescence.

All exponents are rates × times → dimensionless ✓. Expolinear: \([c_m/r_m] = (\text{g d}^{-1})/(\text{d}^{-1}) = \text{g}\) ✓.

Fitting non-linear models: Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt

For a straight line the least-squares problem has an exact solution. For \(W = K/(1+e^{-r(t-t_i)})\) it does not: the parameters appear inside an exponential, the SSE surface is no longer a simple bowl, and the minimum must be found by iteration — start from a guess, improve it, repeat. The standard algorithms linearise the model around the current guess and solve a small linear least-squares problem for the correction.

Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt updates(10.1.7)
\[ \bigl(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J + \lambda\,\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J)\bigr)\,\Delta\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e, \qquad \boldsymbol\theta_{k+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_k + \Delta\boldsymbol\theta, \qquad J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta)}{\partial\theta_j} \]

at each iteration, compute how sensitive every prediction is to every parameter (the Jacobian \(\mathbf J\)), then solve a linear system for the parameter change that best removes the current residuals \(\mathbf e\). With \(\lambda = 0\) this is the Gauss–Newton step; a large \(\lambda\) turns it into a short, safe step down the gradient. Levenberg–Marquardt adapts \(\lambda\) after every step.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\boldsymbol\theta\)parameter vector, e.g. \((K, r, t_i)\)mixed
\(f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta)\)model prediction at time \(t_i\)unit of \(y\)
\(\mathbf J\)Jacobian (\(n\times p\)): sensitivity of predictions to parameters(unit of \(y\))/(unit of \(\theta_j\))
\(\mathbf e\)current residual vector \(y_i - f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta_k)\)unit of \(y\)
\(\lambda\)damping parameter–
Follow the derivation
  1. Near the current guess, approximate the model by its first-order Taylor expansion: \(f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta_k+\Delta\boldsymbol\theta)\approx f(t_i;\boldsymbol\theta_k) + \sum_j J_{ij}\Delta\theta_j\).
  2. The residuals after the step are then approximately \(\mathbf e - \mathbf J\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\) — linear in the unknown step.
  3. Minimising \(\lVert\mathbf e - \mathbf J\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\rVert^2\) is an ordinary linear least-squares problem with “design matrix” \(\mathbf J\) and “data” \(\mathbf e\). Its normal equations (Eq. 10.1.2, matrix form) are \(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\,\Delta\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e\): the Gauss–Newton step.
  4. If the guess is far from the minimum, the linearisation is poor and the Gauss–Newton step can overshoot and even increase SSE. Levenberg (1944) and Marquardt (1963) added the damping term: for large \(\lambda\) the step becomes \(\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\approx \mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e/(\lambda\operatorname{diag}\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J)\) — a small step along the negative gradient of SSE (which is \(-2\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e\)), scaled per parameter.
  5. The rule: if the step reduced SSE, accept it and divide \(\lambda\) by 10 (trust the linear model more); otherwise reject it and multiply \(\lambda\) by 10. The algorithm stops when SSE no longer decreases noticeably.
  6. At convergence, \((\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J)^{-1}s^2\) approximates the covariance matrix of the estimates; the square roots of its diagonal are the standard errors reported by fitting software (the course's fitLM() does exactly this).
Practical advice: starting values and parameterisation

Non-linear fits fail mainly because of bad starting values. Read them off a plot: \(K\) a little above the largest observation, \(t_i\) where the data reach about half of \(K\), \(r \approx 4\times\)(steepest slope)/\(K\) for the logistic. Try several starting points and keep the best. Parameters with very different magnitudes (\(K\approx500\), \(r\approx0.2\)) make the problem badly scaled; the diagonal scaling in the LM step helps. Finally, remember that fitting \(\ln W\) instead of \(W\) changes the error model and therefore the estimates (Paine et al., 2012).

\(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf J\,\Delta\boldsymbol\theta\): row \(j\) has units (y/θⱼ)·Σ(y/θₖ)·θₖ = y²/θⱼ, and \(\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\mathbf e\) has units y·(y/θⱼ) = y²/θⱼ ✓ — each equation is dimensionally consistent, and \(\lambda\) is dimensionless because it multiplies the diagonal of the same matrix.

Over-fitting, the bias–variance trade-off and cross-validation

If a cubic fits better than a line and a quartic better than a cubic, why not use a polynomial of degree 10? Because a model is useful only if it predicts new data — the plants you have not measured yet, the next crop cycle, tomorrow. A very flexible model fits the noise in the training data as eagerly as the signal. It has low bias (it can bend to any shape) but high variance (a different random sample of plants would give a wildly different curve). A rigid model has the opposite problem. The expected prediction error on new data decomposes exactly into these two parts plus the irreducible noise:

Expected prediction error and its cross-validation estimate(10.1.8)
\[ \mathbb E\bigl[(y_0-\hat f(x_0))^2\bigr] = \underbrace{\bigl(f(x_0)-\mathbb E[\hat f(x_0)]\bigr)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\bigl[\hat f(x_0)\bigr]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}, \qquad \text{CV}_{(k)} = \frac1k\sum_{j=1}^{k}\text{MSE}_j \]

the average squared error when predicting a new observation equals the squared systematic error of the model, plus the scatter of the model caused by the randomness of the training sample, plus the noise in the new observation that no model can remove. k-fold cross-validation estimates this error by fitting the model \(k\) times, each time leaving out one fold, and averaging the mean squared errors on the left-out folds.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(f(x_0)\)true (unknown) mean response at \(x_0\)unit of \(y\)
\(\hat f(x_0)\)prediction of a model fitted to a random training sampleunit of \(y\)
\(\mathbb E[\cdot]\), Varaverage and variance over many possible training samples–
\(\sigma^2\)variance of the measurement noiseunit of \(y\) squared
\(\text{MSE}_j\)mean squared prediction error on fold \(j\) when the model was trained on the other \(k-1\) foldsunit of \(y\) squared
Follow the derivation of the decomposition
  1. Write \(y_0 = f(x_0)+\varepsilon\) with \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}[\varepsilon] = \sigma^2\), and \(\varepsilon\) independent of the training data (hence of \(\hat f\)).
  2. Add and subtract \(\mathbb E[\hat f]\): \(y_0-\hat f = \varepsilon + (f - \mathbb E\hat f) + (\mathbb E\hat f - \hat f)\).
  3. Square and take the expectation. The three cross-terms vanish: \(\varepsilon\) has mean zero and is independent; \((f-\mathbb E\hat f)\) is a constant multiplying \(\mathbb E[\mathbb E\hat f-\hat f]=0\).
  4. What remains is \(\sigma^2 + (f-\mathbb E\hat f)^2 + \mathbb E[(\hat f - \mathbb E\hat f)^2]\) = noise + bias² + variance ∎.
Train/test split, k-fold and leave-one-out

A train/test split (e.g., 70/30) is simple but wastes data and depends on the luck of the split. k-fold cross-validation (typically \(k=5\) or 10) uses every observation once for testing. Leave-one-out (\(k=n\)) is natural for the small data sets of a student experiment. For time series and for data grouped by plant, bench or greenhouse compartment, folds must respect the structure — leave out whole plants or whole time blocks — or the estimate will be optimistic (Roberts et al., 2017; see Lesson 10.2 on data leakage).

Every term is a squared quantity in the unit of \(y\) (e.g., cm⁴ for leaf area in cm²); \(\sqrt{\text{CV}}\) is a cross-validated RMSE in cm² ✓.
Key idea

Never judge a model by how well it fits the data it was fitted to. Judge it by how well it predicts data it has not seen: a held-out test set, cross-validation, or next week's measurements. In-sample \(R^2\) always improves with more parameters; out-of-sample error does not.

Choosing between models: the Akaike information criterion

Cross-validation needs enough data to leave some out. With the ten or fifteen measurement dates of a typical student experiment, an information criterion is a useful complement. Akaike (1974) showed that the expected out-of-sample fit of a maximum-likelihood model can be estimated from its in-sample fit plus a penalty proportional to the number of estimated parameters. For least-squares fits with Gaussian errors, the criterion takes a simple form.

Akaike information criterion for least squares(10.1.9)
\[ \text{AIC} = n\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{\text{SSE}}{n}\Bigr) + 2K, \qquad \text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2K(K+1)}{n-K-1}, \qquad w_m = \frac{e^{-\Delta_m/2}}{\sum_j e^{-\Delta_j/2}} \]

AIC rewards a small sum of squared errors (first term) and penalises every estimated parameter by 2 (second term). AICc adds a correction that grows sharply when the number of parameters approaches the number of data points. Among candidate models, the lowest AICc is preferred; \(\Delta_m\) is each model's AICc minus the lowest, and the Akaike weight \(w_m\) is the relative support for model \(m\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(n\)number of observations–
\(K\)number of estimated parameters including the residual variance: \(K = p+1\) for a model with \(p\) curve parameters–
\(\Delta_m\)\(\text{AIC}_{c,m} - \min_j \text{AIC}_{c,j}\)–
\(w_m\)Akaike weight: probability-like support for model \(m\) within the candidate set–
An intuitive derivation (why “+2 per parameter”)
  1. With Gaussian errors the maximised log-likelihood of a least-squares fit is \(\ln\hat L = -\tfrac n2\ln(\text{SSE}/n) + \text{constant}\). So \(-2\ln\hat L = n\ln(\text{SSE}/n)\) + constant measures lack of fit.
  2. This in-sample lack of fit is optimistic: the parameters were tuned to these data, including their noise. Each free parameter absorbs, on average, one “degree of freedom” of noise, lowering \(-2\ln\hat L\) by about 1 in-sample and raising it by about 1 on new data.
  3. The expected gap between in-sample and out-of-sample \(-2\ln L\) is therefore about \(2K\). Adding \(2K\) corrects the optimism: AIC estimates the out-of-sample lack of fit (Akaike, 1974; Burnham & Anderson, 2002).
  4. For small \(n\) the approximation is too lenient; Hurvich and Tsai (1989) derived the correction term in AICc. Burnham and Anderson recommend AICc whenever \(n/K < 40\) — i.e., always, for grow-log data.
  5. Only differences in AICc between models fitted to the same data are meaningful. Rules of thumb: \(\Delta<2\) substantial support; 4–7 considerably less; \(>10\) essentially none.
Assumptions and validity

All models must be fitted to exactly the same response (you cannot compare a model of \(W\) with a model of \(\ln W\) this way) and the same observations. AICc ranks the candidates you supply; it cannot tell you that all of them are poor — check residuals too. Software differs in whether \(\sigma^2\) is counted in \(K\); this lesson and the course's stats.js count it.

\(\ln(\text{SSE}/n)\) takes the logarithm of a dimensional quantity; the unit only adds a constant \(n\ln(\text{unit})\) that is the same for all models and cancels in \(\Delta\) — another reason only differences matter ✓.
Worked example 3Which growth curve does the grow log support?

Four models were fitted by least squares to \(n=12\) leaf-area measurements of one lettuce plant. SSE values (cm⁴): exponential (\(p=2\)) 5210; logistic (\(p=3\)) 812; Gompertz (\(p=3\)) 745; Richards (\(p=4\)) 731. Compute AICc, \(\Delta\) and the Akaike weights. Which model would you report?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Count parameters. \(K = p+1\): 3, 4, 4, 5.
  2. AIC for the logistic. \(12\ln(812/12) + 2\times4 = 12\times4.2146 + 8 = 58.58\). Correction: \(2\times4\times5/(12-4-1) = 40/7 = 5.71\). AICc = 64.29.
  3. All four. Exponential: AIC 78.88, correction \(24/8 = 3.00\), AICc 81.88. Gompertz: 57.54 + 5.71 = 63.26. Richards: 59.31 + \(60/6 = 10.00\) = 69.31.
  4. Differences. Minimum 63.26 (Gompertz). \(\Delta\): exponential 18.63, logistic 1.03, Gompertz 0, Richards 6.06.
  5. Weights. \(e^{-\Delta/2}\): 0.0001, 0.598, 1, 0.048; sum 1.646 → \(w\) = 0.000, 0.363, 0.608, 0.029.
  6. Interpret. Gompertz and logistic are both well supported (\(\Delta<2\)); the extra Richards parameter reduces SSE by only 14 cm⁴ — not enough to pay for itself; exponential growth is rejected. Report the Gompertz fit, mention that the logistic is almost as good, and check both residual plots.
Gompertz (w ≈ 0.61), logistic close behind (w ≈ 0.36); Richards and exponential not supported

Forecasting your harvest with uncertainty

The payoff of a fitted growth curve is a forecast: on which day will the plant reach the target size? For the logistic curve the answer follows by solving Eq. 10.1.5 for \(t\).

Time to reach a target size (logistic)(10.1.10)
\[ t^{*} = t_i - \frac{1}{r}\ln\!\Bigl(\frac{K}{W^{*}}-1\Bigr), \qquad W^{*} < K \]

the day on which the logistic curve reaches the target size \(W^*\) equals the inflection time minus one over the rate times the natural logarithm of (asymptote divided by target, minus one). Targets above half of \(K\) are reached after the inflection; targets close to \(K\) take very long — and are extremely sensitive to the estimate of \(K\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(W^*\)target (harvest) size, e.g. from the area–mass calibrationcm² or g
\(t^*\)forecast time of reaching \(W^*\)d after transplanting
Follow the derivation — and the uncertainty
  1. From Eq. 10.1.5: \(1+e^{-r(t^*-t_i)} = K/W^*\), so \(e^{-r(t^*-t_i)} = K/W^* - 1\).
  2. Take logarithms: \(-r(t^*-t_i) = \ln(K/W^*-1)\), hence \(t^* = t_i - \ln(K/W^*-1)/r\).
  3. Uncertainty. The estimates \(\hat K, \hat r, \hat t_i\) are uncertain and correlated. The delta method propagates their covariance through the derivatives of \(t^*\); a more transparent approach is the parametric bootstrap: simulate many new data sets from the fitted curve plus noise with the fitted residual standard deviation, refit each one, and look at the spread of the resulting \(t^*\). Figure 9 does this 150 times in your browser.
  4. Note that \(\partial t^*/\partial K = -\dfrac{1}{r}\cdot\dfrac{1}{K-W^*}\): when the target is close to the asymptote, a small error in \(K\) causes a very large error in \(t^*\).
\([t_i] = \text{d}\); \([1/r] = \text{d}\); the logarithm of a ratio of sizes is dimensionless → \([t^*] = \text{d}\) ✓.
Worked example 4When will the lettuce be ready?

A logistic fit to your grow log gives \(\hat K = 520\) cm², \(\hat r = 0.20\) d⁻¹ and \(\hat t_i = 18.0\) d after transplanting. Your calibration (Worked example 1, extended with larger plants) says that a marketable 150 g head corresponds to about 420 cm² of projected area. (a) On which day is the target reached? (b) What is the maximum daily increase in leaf area? (c) If the true asymptote were 10 % lower, how would the forecast change?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(t^* = 18.0 - \ln(520/420 - 1)/0.20 = 18.0 - \ln(0.2381)/0.20 = 18.0 + 1.435/0.20 = 18.0 + 7.18 = 25.2\) d.
  2. (b) \(rK/4 = 0.20\times520/4 = 26\) cm² d⁻¹, on day 18.
  3. (c) With \(K = 468\) cm²: \(t^* = 18.0 - \ln(468/420-1)/0.20 = 18.0 - \ln(0.1143)/0.20 = 18.0 + 10.85 = 28.8\) d. A 10 % error in \(K\) moves the forecast by 3.6 days — because 420 cm² is close to the asymptote.
  4. Sanity check. 420/520 = 81 % of final size; on a logistic curve 81 % is reached \(\ln(4.2)/r = 7.2\) d after the inflection ✓. Hydroponic lettuce is often harvested roughly three to five weeks after transplanting, so the forecast is plausible.
(a) day ≈ 25; (b) 26 cm² d⁻¹ at day 18; (c) day ≈ 29 — forecasts near the asymptote are very sensitive to K

A protocol for modelling your own growth data

  1. Measure consistently. Same plants, same time of day, same camera set-up (see the photography protocol in Lesson 10.3). Aim for at least 8–10 dates; more dates early and around the expected inflection are most informative.
  2. Plot first. Raw data against time, on linear and log scales. The log plot shows the exponential phase as a straight line and reveals when RGR starts to fall.
  3. Fit per plant, then summarise. Fit the curve to each plant separately and compare parameters (e.g., \(r\) or maximum growth rate) between treatments with a t-test or ANOVA (Lesson 3.3). Fitting one curve to all plants pooled hides the plant-to-plant variation that your statistics need.
  4. Compare models by AICc and residual plots, not by \(R^2\). Report the chosen model, its parameters with standard errors, RMSE and \(n\).
  5. Forecast honestly. Report a forecast interval, state which data it was based on, and later compare it with what actually happened — the most convincing validation there is.
Frontier

Research on crop modelling is moving towards hybrid approaches in which mechanistic models provide structure and machine learning corrects their systematic errors, or in which the parameters of a mechanistic model are re-estimated continuously from sensor and camera data (data assimilation). These are the building blocks of the digital twins discussed in Lesson 10.5. The statistical ideas of this lesson — least squares, residuals, over-fitting, validation on unseen data — remain exactly the same.

Key terms

Mechanistic model
A model built from physical, chemical and biological processes, whose parameters have physical meaning and units.
Empirical model
A model chosen to describe the shape of data without claiming to represent mechanisms; reliable mainly within the range of the data.
Hybrid (grey-box) model
A combination of mechanistic structure and data-driven components or continuously re-estimated parameters.
Residual
The difference between an observed value and the model prediction, \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\).
Least squares
Estimation of parameters by minimising the sum of squared residuals (SSE).
Normal equations
The linear equations obtained by setting the derivatives of SSE to zero; their solution gives the least-squares estimates.
Coefficient of determination (R²)
Fraction of the variation of the response around its mean explained by the model, \(1-\text{SSE}/\text{SST}\).
RMSE
Root-mean-square error: typical size of a prediction error, in the units of the response.
Relative growth rate (RGR)
Growth rate per unit of existing biomass, \((1/W)\,\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt\), in d⁻¹.
Logistic curve
Symmetric sigmoid growth function \(K/(1+e^{-r(t-t_i)})\) with inflection at \(K/2\).
Gompertz curve
Asymmetric sigmoid growth function with inflection at \(K/e\); its RGR decays exponentially with time.
Levenberg–Marquardt
Iterative non-linear least-squares algorithm that blends Gauss–Newton steps with damped gradient steps.
Over-fitting
Fitting noise rather than signal, giving low training error but poor predictions on new data (high variance).
Cross-validation
Estimating prediction error by repeatedly fitting on part of the data and testing on the held-out remainder.
AICc
Small-sample corrected Akaike information criterion; ranks models by estimated out-of-sample fit, penalising parameters.
