Mineral nutrition and nutrient uptake

A lettuce head is about 95 % water, and more than nine-tenths of its dry matter is carbon, oxygen and hydrogen taken from air and water. The last few per cent — nitrogen, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sulfur and eight trace elements — come from the root zone, and they decide whether the photosynthetic machinery of Lesson 2.1 can be built at all. This lesson asks which elements a plant needs and why, how a root cell pulls ions in against steep electrochemical gradients, how fast it can do so, what the "laws of the minimum" really say about yield, and how all of this becomes the recipes, pH control and diagnostic skills that hydroponic growers use every day.

What you will be able to do

  • State Arnon and Stout's criteria of essentiality, classify the 17 essential elements into macro- and micronutrients, distinguish beneficial elements and interpret typical tissue concentrations (LO2).
  • Relate the function and phloem mobility of each nutrient to where and how deficiency and toxicity symptoms appear, and use this knowledge to diagnose a sick crop (LO2).
  • Explain how the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase, the proton-motive force, symporters and channels drive ion uptake, and use the electrochemical potential and the Nernst equation to decide whether uptake is passive or active (LO2, LO3).
  • Describe uptake with Michaelis–Menten kinetics including the threshold Cmin, estimate Jmax and Km from data, and explain why the Lineweaver–Burk plot gives biased estimates (LO3).
  • Compare nitrate and ammonium nutrition, predict pH drift from the charge balance of uptake, and use Liebig's and Mitscherlich's laws to calculate economic optima and nutrient-use efficiencies (LO2, LO3, LO7).
  • Explain iron-acquisition strategies, chelates and calcium transport, and use uptake concentrations to design and manage hydroponic nutrient solutions (LO2, LO3).

What a plant is made of — and where the elements come from

Burn a dried lettuce leaf and almost everything disappears as CO₂ and water vapour: carbon, oxygen and hydrogen together make up about 96 % of plant dry matter (Epstein & Bloom, 2005). They arrive through the leaves as CO₂ and through the roots as water, and photosynthesis (Lesson 2.1) assembles them into carbohydrates. What remains in the ash — a few per cent of the dry mass — are the mineral nutrients. They are few in mass but decisive in function: nitrogen builds every enzyme, magnesium sits at the centre of every chlorophyll molecule, potassium gives cells the turgor they need to expand, and an iron atom passes on every electron that ferredoxin carries. In a field these elements come from the weathering of minerals and the decomposition of organic matter; in a hydroponic system they come from you, weighed out as fertiliser salts (Lesson 5.2).

Big idea

Essentiality has nothing to do with quantity. A plant needs a million times more nitrogen than molybdenum, but without molybdenum it cannot reduce nitrate, and without nickel it cannot break down urea. Each essential element is equally indispensable; the only difference is how much of it the plant has to find.

What makes an element essential?

Plants absorb almost every element that is dissolved around their roots — including useless and toxic ones such as cadmium, aluminium or arsenic. Finding out which elements they need was therefore impossible in soil, which contains everything. The breakthrough came from the technique that this course is about: in the 1860s the German plant physiologists Julius Sachs and Wilhelm Knop grew plants in solutions of pure salts, so that each element could be withheld on purpose (Epstein & Bloom, 2005). Hydroponics began as a research tool for plant nutrition, a century before it became a way to produce food.

In 1939 Daniel Arnon and Perry Stout, working at the University of California, formalised what "essential" should mean. An element is essential if (Arnon & Stout, 1939a):

  1. without it the plant cannot complete its vegetative or reproductive life cycle;
  2. its function cannot be replaced by another element — the deficiency is specific and is prevented or corrected only by supplying that element; and
  3. it is directly involved in the nutrition of the plant — for example as a constituent of an essential molecule or as a cofactor of an enzyme — and not merely by improving some unfavourable chemical or microbiological condition of the growth medium.

The third criterion is subtle but important: a substance that only detoxifies the solution (by precipitating a toxic metal, say) is not a nutrient. Epstein later proposed a broader, more practical definition: an element is essential if it is part of a molecule that is an intrinsic component of the plant's structure or metabolism, or if its absence causes abnormalities of growth, development or reproduction compared with plants that are less deficient (Epstein & Bloom, 2005). By either definition, 17 elements are essential for all higher plants: C, H and O; the six macronutrients N, P, K, Ca, Mg and S; and the eight micronutrients Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl and Ni.

Why did the micronutrients take almost a century to identify? Because the quantities are so small that impurities were enough. Glassware releases boron, dust contains zinc and copper, "pure" chemicals of the 1920s carried traces of everything, and a seed may contain enough molybdenum or nickel for a whole generation. Proving the essentiality of nickel required barley to be grown in carefully purified solutions for successive generations until the grain it produced was no longer viable (Brown et al., 1987). The history carries a lesson for your own experiments: what is not in the recipe may still be in the water, the substrate, the seed or the container.

Beneficial elements

Some elements stimulate growth or protect plants without meeting the strict criteria, or are essential only for some taxa. They are called beneficial elements (Pilon-Smits et al., 2009):

  • Sodium (Na) is a micronutrient for many species with the C4 and CAM pathways (Brownell & Crossland, 1972) and can partly replace potassium as an osmoticum in some crops. In excess it is the main cause of salt stress, and it accumulates in recirculating hydroponic systems (Section 2.3.9).
  • Silicon (Si), taken up as uncharged monosilicic acid Si(OH)₄, is deposited as amorphous silica in cell walls. It stiffens tissues and increases resistance to fungal pathogens and insects; rice, many grasses and cucurbits accumulate large amounts (Epstein, 1999).
  • Cobalt (Co) is required by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (as cobalamin), and is therefore beneficial for legumes that rely on symbiotic fixation.
  • Selenium (Se) is not needed by plants but is essential for humans and animals. It is taken up as selenate through sulfate transporters. Finland has added selenium to agricultural fertilisers since the mid-1980s to raise the selenium intake of its population — a nation-wide example of biofortification (Alfthan et al., 2015).
Common misconception

"Micronutrients are minor nutrients, so they matter less." A lettuce crop that lacks 0.1 mg kg⁻¹ of molybdenum is just as dead as one that lacks 15 000 mg kg⁻¹ of nitrogen. In hydroponics, where there is no soil reservoir, micronutrient mistakes (a forgotten iron chelate, a boron overdose) show up within days. The narrowest safety margins are in fact found among the micronutrients: the range between boron deficiency and boron toxicity is only a few-fold.

Functions, mobility and the geography of symptoms

Why does a nitrogen-deficient lettuce turn yellow from the outside in, while a calcium-deficient one burns from the inside out? The answer lies in how the nutrients move. Everything that reaches a leaf arrives in the xylem, carried by the transpiration stream (Lesson 2.2). What happens next depends on the element's phloem mobility — whether it can be exported again from a mature leaf to young, growing tissue:

  • Mobile elements (N, P, K, Mg, Cl and, to a lesser extent, Mo) are remobilised from old leaves when supply runs short. The plant sacrifices its older leaves to keep the young ones growing, so deficiency symptoms appear first on older leaves (the outer leaves of a lettuce head). Nitrogen deficiency, for example, begins as Rubisco and other proteins in old leaves are broken down and the amino acids exported.
  • Immobile elements (Ca, B, Fe, Mn, Cu and, in most species, S and Zn) stay where the xylem delivered them. When supply fails, the old leaves remain green but the new growth — young leaves, growing points, fruits, root tips — shows symptoms. Calcium is the extreme case: it moves almost only with the transpiration stream and hardly at all in the phloem (White & Broadley, 2003), which is why weakly transpiring organs such as the enclosed heart of a lettuce head, or a tomato fruit, suffer first.

Table 2 summarises the main functions and symptoms, and Figure 2 lets you explore them element by element. Two words recur: chlorosis (yellowing caused by a lack of chlorophyll) and necrosis (death of tissue, turning brown). Where the chlorosis appears — whole leaf or only between the veins (interveinal), old or young leaves — is the first clue in any diagnosis.

Diagnosis in hydroponics

Symptoms overlap (magnesium, potassium, iron and manganese can all cause interveinal chlorosis), and diseases, pests, cold roots and oxygen shortage produce look-alikes. Before blaming the recipe, check the four usual suspects: pH (at pH > 6.5 iron and manganese become unavailable; below 5 root damage and poor Ca and Mg uptake), EC (too high causes osmotic stress and leaf-margin burn), root health (brown, slimy roots from low dissolved oxygen or Pythium cannot take up anything; Lesson 5.3) and transport (calcium disorders are usually caused by low transpiration, not by low calcium in the solution). Then confirm with a solution or tissue analysis.

Getting ions across the membrane

Water and dissolved ions can travel through the root cortex in the cell walls (the apoplast), but at the endodermis the Casparian strip blocks this route (Lesson 2.2, Figure 4). Every nutrient that reaches the xylem has therefore crossed at least one plasma membrane — a lipid bilayer that ions cannot cross on their own. Proteins do the job, and they fall into three classes (Figure 3):

  • Pumps (primary active transport) use chemical energy directly. The master pump of the root is the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase, a P-type ATPase that hydrolyses one ATP to expel one proton from the cytosol (Palmgren, 2001).
  • Carriers bind a solute and change shape to release it on the other side. Symporters couple the uphill movement of a nutrient to the downhill movement of protons in the same direction; antiporters couple them in opposite directions. This is secondary active transport: the energy comes from the proton gradient built by the pump.
  • Channels are gated pores through which ions flow passively, downhill, at very high rates — up to millions of ions per second per channel.

The pump makes the outside of the cell (the apoplast) acidic — pH around 5.5 compared with about 7.3 in the cytosol — and positively charged relative to the inside. Plant cells therefore have strongly negative membrane potentials, typically −100 to −200 mV, much more negative than animal cells. Whether a given ion can flow in on its own, or must be pumped, depends on both of these gradients together. The quantity that combines them is the electrochemical potential.

Electrochemical potential of an ion(2.3.1)
\[ \tilde\mu_j = \mu_j^{\circ} + R\,T\ln a_j + z_j\,F\,\psi, \qquad \Delta\tilde\mu_j = \tilde\mu_{j,\text{in}}-\tilde\mu_{j,\text{out}} = R\,T\ln\frac{c_{j,\text{in}}}{c_{j,\text{out}}} + z_j\,F\,E_m \]

the electrochemical potential of ion j is its standard chemical potential plus a concentration term (RT times the logarithm of its activity) plus an electrical term (its charge number times the Faraday constant times the electrical potential of the compartment). Moving one mole of the ion from outside to inside changes the free energy by RT ln(cin/cout) plus zF times the membrane potential. If this change is negative, the ion enters spontaneously (passively); if it is positive, energy must be supplied.

  1. For an uncharged solute, the chemical potential is \(\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln a\): each tenfold increase in concentration raises the free energy by \(RT\ln 10 = 5.6\) kJ mol⁻¹ at 20 °C. This is the same logarithmic term that gave the osmotic potential of water in Lesson 2.2.

Assumptions and validity

Activities are replaced by concentrations; in the cytosol (ionic strength ≈ 0.1–0.2 mol L⁻¹) activity coefficients of monovalent ions are about 0.75 and of divalent ions lower still, which shifts the numbers by a few kJ mol⁻¹ but rarely changes the conclusion. A pressure term (\(\bar V_jP\)) is negligible for ions. The equation predicts the direction of spontaneous movement, not its rate — rates depend on how many transporters or channels are open.

Setting \(\Delta\tilde\mu = 0\) asks a sharper question: at what membrane potential would an ion be in equilibrium — no net tendency to move in or out — given its concentrations on the two sides? The answer is one of the most useful equations in physiology, and the same one that makes a pH electrode work (Lesson 9.2).

Nernst equation for an ion(2.3.2)
\[ E_{N,j} = \frac{R\,T}{z_j\,F}\ln\frac{c_{j,\text{out}}}{c_{j,\text{in}}} = \frac{2.303\,R\,T}{z_j\,F}\log_{10}\frac{c_{j,\text{out}}}{c_{j,\text{in}}}, \qquad \frac{\Delta\tilde\mu_j}{F} = z_j\,\bigl(E_m - E_{N,j}\bigr) \]

the Nernst potential of an ion is RT divided by zF times the natural logarithm of the ratio of its outside to its inside concentration — about 58 mV divided by z for each tenfold ratio at 20 °C. The driving force on the ion is its charge number times the difference between the actual membrane potential and its Nernst potential: if z(Em − EN) is negative, the ion flows in passively; if positive, uptake needs energy.

Assumptions and validity

The Nernst potential is a pure thermodynamic property of one ion; it does not depend on how permeable the membrane is. The actual membrane potential is set by the pump and by the combined leak currents of all ions (described by the Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation), which is why \(E_m\) generally differs from every \(E_N\). An uncharged solute such as boric acid, B(OH)₃, or silicic acid has no Nernst potential (\(z = 0\)): only its concentration gradient matters.

For anions such as nitrate the negative membrane potential is an obstacle rather than a help. A symporter overcomes it by letting protons "carry" the anion in: the protons are pulled inwards by both their concentration gradient (pH 5.5 outside, 7.3 inside) and the negative potential. The combined driving force of protons is the proton-motive force — the same quantity that drives ATP synthase in the chloroplast (Lesson 2.1, Eq. 2.1.3), here across the plasma membrane. How many protons must accompany each nitrate?

Proton-motive force and the energetics of H⁺-coupled symport(2.3.3)
\[ \frac{\Delta\tilde\mu_{\text{H}^+}}{F} = E_m - \frac{2.303\,R\,T}{F}\,\bigl(\text{pH}_{\text{in}}-\text{pH}_{\text{out}}\bigr), \qquad \Delta G_{\text{symport}} = \Delta\tilde\mu_{S} + n\,\Delta\tilde\mu_{\text{H}^+} < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; n > \frac{\Delta\tilde\mu_S}{-\Delta\tilde\mu_{\text{H}^+}} \]

A symporter that carries one substrate molecule S together with n protons works as long as the total free-energy change — the uphill step of the substrate plus n downhill steps of protons — is negative. This sets the minimum number of protons per substrate.

How fast? Michaelis–Menten uptake kinetics

Thermodynamics tells us whether an ion can enter; kinetics tells us how fast. In the early 1960s Emanuel Epstein and co-workers measured the uptake of potassium by excised barley roots over a wide range of concentrations. Below about 0.2 mM, uptake rose steeply and then levelled off exactly like an enzyme reaction approaching saturation; above 1 mM a second, low-affinity "mechanism" appeared (Epstein et al., 1963). Treating the transporter as an enzyme whose "substrate" is the ion outside the cell, they described the first mechanism with the equation that Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten had derived for invertase in 1913 (Johnson & Goody, 2011). Claassen and Barber (1974) then added one refinement that matters in practice: net uptake stops not at zero concentration but at a small positive threshold, \(C_{\min}\), where influx is balanced by efflux.

Michaelis–Menten uptake with a threshold concentration(2.3.4)
\[ J = \frac{J_{\max}\,(C - C_{\min})}{K_m + C - C_{\min}} \quad (C > C_{\min}), \qquad J = 0 \quad (C \le C_{\min}) \]

the net uptake rate equals the maximum rate multiplied by the concentration in excess of the threshold, divided by the Michaelis constant plus that excess. At a concentration of Km above Cmin the rate is half its maximum; far above Km it approaches Jmax and becomes independent of concentration.

Integrated form: how fast do roots strip a closed tank?

In a closed volume \(V\) with root surface \(A\), the mass balance is \(V\,\mathrm dC/\mathrm dt = -A\,J(C)\). Separating variables with \(u = C - C_{\min}\): \((K_m + u)\,\mathrm du/u = -(AJ_{\max}/V)\,\mathrm dt\). Integrating from \(u_0\) to \(u\) gives the integrated Michaelis–Menten equation

\[ t = \frac{V}{A\,J_{\max}}\left[K_m\ln\frac{C_0-C_{\min}}{C-C_{\min}} + (C_0 - C)\right]. \]

The second term (linear depletion at full speed) dominates at high concentration; the logarithmic term dominates as the solution approaches \(C_{\min}\). Fitting this equation to the concentration decline in a small tank is the "depletion method" of Claassen and Barber (1974) for measuring \(J_{\max}\), \(K_m\) and \(C_{\min}\) on intact plants.

Assumptions and validity

One saturable transport system per ion, a well-stirred solution (no depletion zone at the root surface; in soil or stagnant solution, diffusion rather than the transporter may limit uptake) and constant transporter numbers. In reality plants regulate \(J_{\max}\): starved roots make more high-affinity transporters within hours, and well-fed roots down-regulate them. Real roots also have low-affinity systems that do not saturate even at tens of millimolar (Griffiths & York, 2020), which adds a nearly linear component at high concentration (Figure 5).

Figure 5 reveals a fact with large practical consequences. At the concentrations of a standard nutrient solution (millimolar), the high-affinity systems run at more than 99 % of their maximum rate: uptake is set by the plant's demand, not by the concentration outside. Chen et al. (1997) put this to the test with lettuce in a nutrient-film system, varying nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium over a hundred-fold range. Lowering the concentrations below those used by commercial growers had no adverse effect on growth or on nutrient uptake; they concluded that N, P and K could safely be reduced to one-tenth in a recirculating system — and to one-hundredth in a non-recirculating one, provided the supply keeps pace with uptake. The Swedish plant nutritionist Torsten Ingestad turned the same idea into an experimental principle: add nutrients at a relative addition rate matched to the plant's relative growth rate and the plant grows at a steady state regardless of how low the external concentration is (Ingestad, 1982; Ingestad & Ågren, 1992). High concentrations in commercial recipes are an insurance against depletion and a buffer for imperfect control, not a physiological requirement — but they also mean that every litre discharged carries a large nutrient load.

Estimating Jmax and Km — and why not to use Lineweaver–Burk

How do you get \(J_{\max}\) and \(K_m\) from measured uptake rates? Before computers, the standard trick was to transform the curve into a straight line. The most famous transformation is the double-reciprocal plot of Lineweaver and Burk (1934), still found in many textbooks.

Linearised forms of the Michaelis–Menten equation(2.3.5)
\[ \underbrace{\frac{1}{J} = \frac{K_m}{J_{\max}}\cdot\frac{1}{C} + \frac{1}{J_{\max}}}_{\text{Lineweaver--Burk}}, \qquad \underbrace{\frac{C}{J} = \frac{1}{J_{\max}}\,C + \frac{K_m}{J_{\max}}}_{\text{Hanes--Woolf}}, \qquad \delta\!\left(\frac{1}{J}\right) \approx \frac{\delta J}{J^{2}} \]

plotting 1/J against 1/C gives a straight line whose intercept is 1/Jmax and whose slope is Km/Jmax; plotting C/J against C gives a line with slope 1/Jmax and intercept Km/Jmax. The trouble is the last expression: a measurement error δJ becomes an error δJ/J² in the reciprocal, which explodes for the small rates measured at low concentration.

Nitrate, ammonium and the pH of the root zone

Nitrogen is the nutrient taken up in the largest amounts, and it comes in two ionic forms with very different consequences. Nitrate (NO₃⁻) dominates in aerated soils, where nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium, and in almost every hydroponic recipe. Ammonium (NH₄⁺) dominates in acidic or waterlogged soils and is the nitrogen that fish excrete in aquaponics before nitrification (Lesson 6.1). Plants can use both, but nitrate must first be reduced: nitrate reductase in the cytosol reduces NO₃⁻ to nitrite with NADH, and nitrite reductase in the plastids reduces nitrite to ammonium with ferredoxin — eight electrons per nitrogen atom in total. The ammonium is then incorporated into glutamine and glutamate by the GS/GOGAT cycle. Absorbing and reducing nitrate therefore costs the plant more energy than using ammonium; barley roots, for example, spent a larger share of their respiration on nitrate than on ammonium nutrition (Bloom et al., 1992).

Why, then, do growers not feed ammonium? Because at the millimolar concentrations of a nutrient solution, ammonium as the main nitrogen source is toxic to many species, lettuce and tomato included: leaves turn chlorotic, root growth is inhibited, and the uptake of K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ is depressed by competition with NH₄⁺ (Britto & Kronzucker, 2002). The mechanisms are still debated, but the practical rule is clear: hydroponic recipes supply nitrogen mainly as nitrate, with ammonium usually below 10–15 % of total N (Lesson 5.2, Table 1) — enough to help control pH, as we shall see, but not enough to harm.

Nitrate in leafy vegetables

Nitrate taken up faster than it is reduced is stored in the vacuole, where it serves as a cheap osmoticum. Storage is greatest when light is low — little reductant and little sugar are available, so the plant uses nitrate instead of sugars to maintain turgor (Blom-Zandstra, 1989). Leafy vegetables harvested in the dark half of the year, such as winter lettuce in a Swedish greenhouse, can therefore contain several grams of nitrate per kilogram. Because nitrate can be converted into nitrite in the body, the EU sets maximum levels for the most affected crops (Table 4). The European Food Safety Authority concluded that, at these levels, the benefits of eating vegetables outweigh the risk (EFSA, 2008). For a grower the limits are a real design constraint: keep light high and avoid a large nitrate surplus towards harvest.

The charge balance of uptake — and why the pH drifts

A plant is electrically neutral, and so is its nutrient solution. Every mole of positive charge that a root takes up must be matched either by a mole of negative charge taken up or by a mole of positive charge released — in practice H⁺. Conversely, a surplus of anion uptake is balanced by releasing OH⁻ or HCO₃⁻ (or taking up H⁺). Nitrate is by far the largest single ion taken up, so a plant fed with nitrate takes up more anion than cation charge and alkalinises its root zone; a plant fed with ammonium takes up more cation charge and acidifies it (Figure 7; Hinsinger et al., 2003). In soil these root-induced pH changes, confined to a few millimetres around the root, change the availability of phosphate and micronutrients; in hydroponics they change the pH of the whole tank.

\[ H_{\text{net}} = \sum_{\text{cations}} z_i\,U_i - \sum_{\text{anions}} |z_j|\,U_j = U_{\text{NH}_4^+} + U_{\text{K}^+} + 2U_{\text{Ca}^{2+}} + 2U_{\text{Mg}^{2+}} + U_{\text{Na}^+} - U_{\text{NO}_3^-} - U_{\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-} - 2U_{\text{SO}_4^{2-}} - U_{\text{Cl}^-} \]

the net amount of H⁺ that a root releases equals the charge of all cations it takes up minus the charge of all anions it takes up. A positive value acidifies the root zone; a negative value means the root releases OH⁻ or bicarbonate and the pH rises.

Laws of the minimum, diminishing returns and nutrient-use efficiency

How does yield respond to the supply of a nutrient? Two classic answers have shaped fertiliser advice for more than a century. In 1828 the German agronomist Carl Sprengel published a long article on soil chemistry and plant nutrition that "contained in essence" the idea that the scarcest nutrient limits growth; Justus von Liebig popularised it in his influential books of 1840 and 1855. Because Liebig's books carried Sprengel's doctrine to a world audience without much acknowledgement, van der Ploeg et al. (1999) proposed calling it the Sprengel–Liebig law of the minimum: yield is controlled by the growth factor in shortest supply relative to the plant's need, and adding more of any other factor does not help until the limiting one is relieved. Its familiar illustration is a barrel whose staves are the growth factors (Figure 8).

In 1909 Eilhard Alfred Mitscherlich offered a different picture based on his pot experiments: each growth factor gives diminishing returns — every additional kilogram of fertiliser raises yield by less than the previous one, in proportion to how far the yield still is from its maximum (Mitscherlich, 1909). When several factors are combined multiplicatively (the Mitscherlich–Baule form), all of them influence yield at the same time, and improving one raises the response to the others.

Law of the minimum and Mitscherlich's law of diminishing returns(2.3.7)
\[ \underbrace{Y = \min\bigl\{Y_{\max},\; a_1 + b_1x_1,\; a_2 + b_2x_2,\;\dots\bigr\}}_{\text{Sprengel--Liebig (linear--plateau)}} \qquad \underbrace{Y = A\prod_i\Bigl[1 - e^{-c_i\,(x_i + b_i)}\Bigr]}_{\text{Mitscherlich--Baule}} \]

in the Liebig model yield is the smallest of several straight-line responses, one per nutrient, capped by a maximum; only the most limiting nutrient matters at any time. In the Mitscherlich model yield approaches a maximum A exponentially as each nutrient supply xi (plus the amount bi already available from the soil or water) increases; the factors multiply, so every nutrient matters all the time.

  2. Separate variables: \(\mathrm dY/(A - Y) = c\,\mathrm dx\). Integrate: \(-\ln(A - Y) = cx + \text{const}\), i.e. \(A - Y = K e^{-cx}\).
  3. Yield is zero when the plant has no nutrient at all, i.e. at \(x = -b\) (no fertiliser and no soil supply). Hence \(K = Ae^{-cb}\) and \(Y = A[1 - e^{-c(x+b)}]\).
  4. Economic optimum. Fertilise as long as the value of the extra yield exceeds the cost of the extra fertiliser: \(p_Y\,\mathrm dY/\mathrm dx = p_x\). With \(\mathrm dY/\mathrm dx = Ace^{-c(x+b)}\): \(x^* = \dfrac{1}{c}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{A\,c\,p_Y}{p_x}\right) - b\).
  5. For the Liebig model the marginal return is the constant slope \(b_1\) up to the kink and zero beyond, so the optimum is simply the kink — as long as \(b_1p_Y > p_x\).
Assumptions and validity

Both are empirical response functions for a given site, season and crop; their parameters must be fitted to field or greenhouse trials. Mitscherlich believed that \(c\) was a constant for each nutrient — an idea that did not survive testing. Neither model includes toxicity at very high rates, nor the time course of supply. Paris (1992) compared the two with non-nested statistical tests on maize (N and P) and cotton (N and water) experiments and found the von Liebig (plateau) specification the better model in both.

Nutrient-use efficiency

Whatever the shape of the response curve, farmers and regulators want to know how much of the applied nutrient ends up in the crop and how much is lost to water and air. Nitrogen-use efficiency is central to the nitrogen planetary boundary (Lesson 1.2), and there are many indicators, each answering a different question (Cassman et al., 2002; Congreves et al., 2021).

Indicators of nutrient-use efficiency(2.3.8)
\[ \text{PFP} = \frac{Y}{F}, \qquad \text{AE} = \frac{Y - Y_0}{F}, \qquad \text{RE} = \frac{U - U_0}{F}, \qquad \text{PE} = \frac{Y - Y_0}{U - U_0}, \qquad \text{AE} = \text{RE}\times\text{PE} \]

partial factor productivity is yield per unit of fertiliser applied. Agronomic efficiency is the extra yield per unit of fertiliser compared with an unfertilised control. Apparent recovery efficiency is the extra nutrient taken up by the crop per unit applied. Physiological efficiency is the extra yield per unit of extra nutrient taken up. Agronomic efficiency is the product of recovery and physiological efficiency.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Every indicator is a ratio of an output to an input. PFP uses totals; AE, RE and PE use the difference from an unfertilised control plot, isolating the effect of the fertiliser from the soil's own supply.
  2. The product: \(\text{RE}\times\text{PE} = \dfrac{U-U_0}{F}\cdot\dfrac{Y-Y_0}{U-U_0} = \dfrac{Y-Y_0}{F} = \text{AE}\). A poor AE can therefore be caused by poor capture (losses by leaching, volatilisation, denitrification: low RE) or by poor conversion of nutrient into yield (low PE, e.g., luxury uptake or another factor limiting).
  3. In a closed hydroponic loop almost all nutrient not taken up stays in the tank, so the system recovery efficiency can approach 100 % — the big environmental advantage of recirculation. In open, drain-to-waste systems, the nutrients in the drain water are lost unless the drain is collected.
Assumptions and validity

"Apparent" because the difference method assumes the fertiliser does not change the uptake of soil nitrogen. Isotope (¹⁵N) methods measure true recovery. Values depend strongly on the rate: near the economic optimum, AE is much lower than at low rates because of diminishing returns (Figure 9). Congreves et al. (2021) review more than a dozen further indices, including whole-system N balances.

Worked example 6The economic optimum for nitrogen — and why the model matters

A winter-wheat trial is described by a Mitscherlich curve with A = 9000 kg ha⁻¹, c = 0.018 ha kg⁻¹ and b = 50 kg N ha⁻¹ (illustrative values of realistic magnitude). Fertiliser N costs 1.20 € kg⁻¹ and grain sells for 0.20 € kg⁻¹. (a) Find the economic optimum N rate and the yield there. (b) Calculate PFP and AE at the optimum. (c) The crop took up 145 kg N ha⁻¹ at the optimum and 75 kg N ha⁻¹ without fertiliser; calculate RE and PE and check that AE = RE × PE. (d) A linear–plateau model fitted to the same trial reaches its plateau at 120 kg N ha⁻¹. What would it recommend?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Price ratio: \(p_x/p_Y = 1.20/0.20 = 6\) kg grain per kg N — the extra grain one kilogram of N must at least produce.
  2. (a) Optimum: \(x^* = \frac{1}{0.018}\ln\!\left(\frac{9000\times0.018}{6}\right) - 50 = 55.6\times\ln 27 - 50 = 55.6\times3.296 - 50 = 133\) kg N ha⁻¹. Yield: \(9000[1 - e^{-0.018\times183}] = 9000\times(1-0.037) = 8667\) kg ha⁻¹.
  3. (b) Unfertilised yield \(Y_0 = 9000[1 - e^{-0.9}] = 5341\) kg ha⁻¹. PFP = 8667/133 = 65 kg grain per kg N; AE = (8667 − 5341)/133 = 25 kg kg⁻¹.
  4. (c) RE = (145 − 75)/133 = 0.53 — about half of the fertiliser N ended up in the crop. PE = 3326/70 = 47.5 kg grain per kg of extra N taken up. Check: 0.53 × 47.5 = 25 ✓.
  5. (d) The Liebig model's marginal return is 30.5 kg grain per kg N up to the plateau — more than the price ratio of 6 — and zero beyond. Its optimum is the kink: 120 kg N ha⁻¹, 13 kg less than the Mitscherlich optimum, for a slightly higher predicted yield (9000 kg ha⁻¹).
  6. Interpretation. The recommended rate depends on the shape assumed for the response curve — exactly Paris's (1992) point. Near the optimum the Mitscherlich curve is almost flat (the last 13 kg N add only about 1 % yield), so the two models disagree mostly about nitrogen that would be largely lost to the environment.

Two special cases: iron and calcium

Iron: plenty everywhere, soluble nowhere

Iron is the fourth most abundant element in the Earth's crust, yet iron deficiency is one of the most common nutritional disorders of crops. The reason is chemistry: in aerated water at near-neutral pH, iron exists as Fe(III), which forms hydroxides and oxides of extraordinarily low solubility. The concentration of free Fe³⁺ that can coexist with ferric hydroxide falls a thousand-fold for every unit increase in pH:

Solubility of ferric iron(2.3.9)
\[ \text{Fe(OH)}_3(s) + 3\,\text{H}^+ \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe}^{3+} + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}, \qquad K = \frac{[\text{Fe}^{3+}]}{[\text{H}^+]^3} \;\Rightarrow\; \log_{10}[\text{Fe}^{3+}] = \log_{10}K - 3\,\text{pH} \]

ferric hydroxide dissolves by consuming three protons for every iron ion released. At equilibrium the free Fe³⁺ concentration is K times the cube of the proton concentration; on a logarithmic scale it falls by three units for every unit rise in pH.

Follow the derivation
  1. Write the dissolution with protons (it is an acid–base reaction: each OH⁻ released combines with H⁺ to water).
  2. The activity of the solid and of water is 1, so the equilibrium constant is \(K = \{\text{Fe}^{3+}\}/\{\text{H}^+\}^3\).
  3. Take logarithms: \(\log\{\text{Fe}^{3+}\} = \log K + 3\log\{\text{H}^+\} = \log K - 3\,\text{pH}\).
  4. Numbers with \(\log K = 2.7\): pH 5.5 → \(10^{-13.8}\) M; pH 6.0 → \(10^{-15.3} = 5\times10^{-16}\) M; pH 7.0 → \(10^{-18.3}\) M. A nutrient solution supplies 10–40 µM Fe, i.e. about \(2\times10^{-5}\) M — some ten orders of magnitude more than free Fe³⁺ allows at pH 6. That iron can only stay dissolved if it is bound in a chelate.
Assumptions and validity

Free Fe³⁺ only; hydrolysed species (FeOH²⁺, Fe(OH)₂⁺, Fe(OH)₄⁻) raise the total dissolved inorganic iron, but it remains far below plant requirements at pH above about 6 (Lindsay & Schwab, 1982). Freshly precipitated amorphous hydroxide is somewhat more soluble, crystalline oxides such as goethite much less. The pH dependence — a factor of 1000 per unit — is the robust message.

\(\log_{10}\) of a concentration (in mol L⁻¹) = \(\log_{10}K\) (dimensionless on an activity basis) − 3 × pH (dimensionless) ✓

A chelate (from the Greek for "claw") is an organic molecule that grips a metal ion with several bonds at once, keeping it in solution. Synthetic chelates such as Fe-EDTA, Fe-DTPA and Fe-EDDHA differ in how well they hold iron at high pH; which one to use, and why chelates break down in light, is treated in Lesson 5.2 (Lucena, 2003). Plants themselves have evolved two strategies to mobilise iron (Figure 10; Marschner & Römheld, 1994; Kobayashi & Nishizawa, 2012):

  • Strategy I — reduction (all dicots, including lettuce, tomato, cucumber and basil, and non-grass monocots). Iron-deficient roots pump more protons (acidifying the rhizosphere and increasing Fe(III) solubility, Eq. 2.3.9), reduce Fe(III) at the root surface with a ferric-chelate reductase (FRO2 in Arabidopsis), and take up the more soluble Fe²⁺ through the transporter IRT1. Many also secrete phenolic compounds (coumarins) that mobilise iron.
  • Strategy II — chelation (grasses: wheat, barley, maize, rice). Roots release phytosiderophores of the mugineic-acid family, which bind Fe(III) strongly; the whole Fe(III)–phytosiderophore complex is taken up by YS1/YSL transporters. Rice combines this with direct Fe²⁺ uptake.

For hydroponic lettuce, a Strategy I plant, the practical consequences are straightforward: keep the pH of the solution between about 5.5 and 6.3, use a chelate that is stable at that pH, keep tanks and pipes opaque, and suspect pH before anything else when the youngest leaves turn yellow with green veins.

Calcium: a transport problem, not a supply problem

Calcium has three jobs: it cross-links pectin chains in the cell wall (calcium pectate, the "cement" between cells), it stabilises membranes, and it is a universal intracellular messenger. For the last role the cytosol keeps free Ca²⁺ at only about 100–200 nM, ten thousand times less than in the cell wall, and releases brief pulses in response to stimuli (White & Broadley, 2003; Hepler, 2005) — the steep gradient calculated in Worked example 2. Because calcium moves almost exclusively in the xylem, its delivery to an organ is proportional to how much that organ transpires, and it is hardly redistributed afterwards. Rapidly growing, weakly transpiring tissues — the enclosed heart leaves of lettuce, tomato and pepper fruits, apple fruit — are therefore the first to suffer, producing tipburn, blossom-end rot and bitter pit. The physics of this was worked out in Lesson 2.2 (Figure 11 there): calcium disorders in controlled environments are cured by air movement onto the growing point, moderate humidity and moderate growth rates, not by pouring more calcium into the tank. Excess potassium, magnesium or ammonium can make matters worse by competing with Ca²⁺ at uptake (Table 2).

From physiology to recipe: implications for hydroponics

Everything in this lesson converges on one practical question: what should a nutrient solution contain, and how should it be managed as the crop grows? Two facts from the previous sections set the frame. Uptake is governed by the plant's demand (the transporters are saturated, Section 2.3.5), and water and nutrients enter the plant by different routes at different rates. The plant's demand for an element is its growth rate multiplied by its tissue concentration; its water uptake is its growth rate multiplied by the amount of water it transpires per gram of dry matter. The ratio of the two is the concentration at which the plant "removes" that element from the solution — the uptake concentration, a central concept in the nutrient management of greenhouse crops (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009).

Uptake concentration(2.3.10)
\[ C_{u,i} = \frac{\text{uptake of } i}{\text{uptake of water}} = \frac{x_i\,\Delta W / M_i}{\text{TR}\,\Delta W} = \frac{x_i}{M_i\,\text{TR}} \qquad\Bigl(\text{in practical units: } C_{u,i}\,[\text{mmol L}^{-1}] = \frac{10\,000\,x_i[\%]}{M_i\,\text{TR}[\text{L kg}^{-1}]}\Bigr) \]

the uptake concentration of element i is the amount of the element taken up divided by the amount of water taken up over the same period. Because both scale with the dry matter produced, ΔW cancels: it equals the element's mass fraction in the dry matter divided by its molar mass and by the transpiration ratio, the litres of water transpired per kilogram of dry matter.

Three further links close the circle. First, the tissue nitrogen concentration is not only an output but also a driver of growth: Ågren (1985), working at the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, showed that relative growth rate increases linearly with the plant's internal nitrogen concentration — the nitrogen productivity concept that connects this lesson to the growth analysis of Lesson 2.4. Second, the charge balance (Eq. 2.3.6) and the iron chemistry (Eq. 2.3.9) together explain why pH control is the most important daily task in hydroponics. Third, the saturation of uptake (Eq. 2.3.4) means that lower-concentration solutions with continuous replenishment are physiologically possible (Chen et al., 1997) and reduce the nutrient load of any water that has to be discharged.

Research frontier

Can we breed or engineer roots that take up nutrients more efficiently? Griffiths and York (2020) argue that root uptake kinetics — Jmax, Km and Cmin, and how they vary along the root system and over time — are a largely untapped target for improving nutrient-use efficiency, and that high-throughput phenotyping of these parameters is now becoming feasible. For controlled environments, the complementary engineering frontier is closed-loop nutrient control with ion-selective sensors (Lesson 9.2), which would let growers dose individual ions to match measured uptake instead of relying on EC alone.

Key terms

Essential element
Element without which a plant cannot complete its life cycle, whose function no other element can replace, and which is directly involved in plant metabolism (Arnon & Stout, 1939a).
Macronutrient
Essential mineral element needed in relatively large amounts (≈ 0.1–1.5 % of dry matter): N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S.
Micronutrient
Essential mineral element needed in trace amounts (≈ 0.1–100 mg kg⁻¹ dry matter): Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni.
Beneficial element
Element that stimulates growth or is essential only for some taxa, e.g., Na, Si, Co; Se is essential for animals, not plants.
Phloem mobility
Ability of a nutrient to be re-exported from mature leaves; mobile nutrients show deficiency first in old leaves, immobile ones in young tissue.
Electrochemical potential
Free energy of an ion including concentration and electrical terms, \(\tilde\mu = \mu^\circ + RT\ln a + zF\psi\); ions move spontaneously to lower \(\tilde\mu\).
Nernst potential
Membrane potential at which an ion is in equilibrium across a membrane, \(E_N = (RT/zF)\ln(c_{\text{out}}/c_{\text{in}})\).
Symporter
Carrier that couples the uphill movement of a solute to the downhill movement of H⁺ in the same direction (secondary active transport).
Michaelis–Menten kinetics
Saturating relationship between uptake rate and concentration, characterised by Jmax (maximum rate) and Km (concentration at half-maximal rate).
Cmin
External concentration below which net uptake of a nutrient stops because influx is balanced by efflux.
Law of the minimum
Sprengel–Liebig principle that yield is limited by the growth factor in shortest supply relative to need.
Mitscherlich equation
Yield response with diminishing returns, \(Y = A[1 - e^{-c(x+b)}]\); multiplicative for several nutrients (Mitscherlich–Baule).
Agronomic efficiency
Extra yield per unit of nutrient applied, (Y − Y₀)/F; equals recovery efficiency × physiological efficiency.
Chelate
Organic ligand that binds a metal ion (e.g., Fe³⁺) with several bonds and keeps it soluble at pH values where the free ion would precipitate.
Uptake concentration
Ratio of nutrient uptake to water uptake (mmol L⁻¹), \(x_i/(M_i\,\text{TR})\); decides whether a nutrient accumulates or depletes in a recirculating solution.
