Planetary boundaries and the food system

For ten thousand years — the Holocene — the Earth's climate and ecosystems were unusually stable, and agriculture and civilisation grew up inside that stability. The planetary boundaries framework asks a precise question: how far can we push the processes that keep the Earth in that state before we risk tipping it into something else? The answer, updated in 2023, 2025 and 2026, is sobering: seven of nine boundaries are already transgressed, and food production is the single largest driver of several of them. This lesson teaches you to read the boundaries quantitatively, to translate them into budgets for a diet or a country, and to judge them critically.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the planetary boundaries framework — control variables, boundary values, zones of uncertainty and the safe operating space — and how it evolved from 2009 to 2026 (LO1, LO7).
  • Describe quantitatively how the food system drives climate change, land-system change, freshwater change, nitrogen and phosphorus flows, biosphere integrity and novel entities (LO1).
  • Calculate normalised transgression ratios, nitrogen-use efficiency and surpluses, per-capita budgets and overshoot ratios (LO7).
  • Summarise the EAT–Lancet planetary health diet (2019 and 2025), the food-system boundaries and the modelled effects of combining dietary change, technology and waste reduction (LO1, LO7).
  • Evaluate critiques of the framework and relate it to the "doughnut" of social foundations and ecological ceilings (LO7).

A safe operating space for humanity

In 2009 a group of 29 Earth-system scientists led by Johan Rockström at the Stockholm Resilience Centre published a short paper in Nature with a provocative title: A safe operating space for humanity (Rockström et al., 2009). They identified nine processes that regulate the stability and resilience of the Earth system, proposed a measurable control variable for each, and set a boundary — a precautionary limit, placed at the "safe" end of the scientific uncertainty about where dangerous thresholds lie. Staying within all nine, they argued, gives humanity a good chance of keeping the planet in a Holocene-like state, the only state we know can support complex societies and agriculture.

The nine processes are: climate change; change in biosphere integrity (biodiversity loss); land-system change; freshwater change; biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus); ocean acidification; atmospheric aerosol loading; stratospheric ozone depletion; and the introduction of novel entities (synthetic chemicals, plastics and other new substances). The framework has been updated several times:

2009

Rockström et al. (Nature; extended version in Ecology and Society): nine boundaries proposed, seven quantified; climate change, biodiversity loss and the nitrogen cycle judged transgressed.

2015

Steffen et al. (Science): revised control variables, regional sub-boundaries ("two-tier" approach) and two core boundaries — climate change and biosphere integrity — each able on its own to push the Earth into a new state. Four boundaries transgressed.

2023

Richardson et al. (Science Advances): all nine boundaries quantified for the first time, including novel entities and green water; six of nine transgressed; ocean acidification close to its boundary.

2025

Planetary Health Check 2025 (Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research): with revised pre-industrial ocean chemistry, ocean acidification is assessed as transgressed — seven of nine (Sakschewski et al., 2025; Findlay et al., 2025). The EAT–Lancet Commission 2.0 and te Wierik et al. (2025) define "food-system boundaries" for all nine processes.

2026

Planetary Health Check 2026 (released September 2026): seven of nine boundaries remain transgressed, all at record levels of pressure; only ozone and aerosol loading have improved over the past decade.

Key idea

A planetary boundary is not a tipping point. It is a precautionary guard rail set, by scientific judgement, some distance before the region where abrupt or irreversible change becomes likely. Crossing it does not trigger immediate collapse; it means moving into a zone where the risk of large, possibly irreversible change increases.

Control variables, boundary values and transgression

To compare boundaries measured in very different units — parts per million, teragrams per year, per cent of forest cover, a dimensionless saturation state — Richardson et al. (2023) normalised each control variable so that the Holocene (pre-industrial) value maps to 0 and the boundary to 1. We use the same idea and call the result the transgression ratio.

Normalised transgression ratio(1.2.1)
\[ T = \frac{x - x_{H}}{x_{B} - x_{H}} \qquad \begin{cases} T \le 1 & \text{within the safe operating space}\\ T > 1 & \text{boundary transgressed} \end{cases} \]

the transgression ratio equals the distance the control variable has moved from its Holocene value, divided by the distance from the Holocene value to the boundary.

Why it works for "higher is worse" and "lower is worse" variables
  1. For CO₂ concentration, higher is worse: \(x_B > x_H\) and both numerator and denominator are positive once \(x\) rises above \(x_H\).
  2. For forest cover (per cent of original forest) or ocean aragonite saturation, lower is worse: \(x_B < x_H\). As \(x\) falls, the numerator \(x - x_H\) becomes negative, and so does the denominator \(x_B - x_H\): the ratio is again positive and grows as the situation worsens.
  3. Example: forest cover \(x_H = 100\,\%\), \(x_B = 75\,\%\), \(x = 60\,\%\): \(T = (60-100)/(75-100) = (-40)/(-25) = 1.6\).
  4. \(T\) is linear in the control variable, so it says "how many safe-space widths" we have travelled. It says nothing about how dangerous each unit of overshoot is — that depends on the Earth-system process.
Assumptions and validity

The ratio inherits every uncertainty of \(x_H\), \(x_B\) and \(x\). A change of reference value alone can move a boundary across the line: the Planetary Health Check 2025 raised the estimated pre-industrial aragonite saturation of the surface ocean from 3.44 to 3.57, which moved the boundary (80 % of pre-industrial) from 2.75 to 2.86 and, with a current value of 2.84, turned "almost transgressed" into "transgressed". For "novel entities" the control variable (share of synthetic chemicals released without adequate safety testing) has a boundary of 0 %, so the ratio is undefined — the boundary is simply judged transgressed (Persson et al., 2022).

How food drives the boundaries

Campbell et al. (2017) reviewed the evidence boundary by boundary and concluded that agriculture is a major driver of the Earth system exceeding planetary boundaries — the dominant driver for land-system change, freshwater use and biogeochemical flows, and a major one for biosphere integrity and climate. Eight years later, te Wierik et al. (2025) quantified food-system boundaries for all nine processes and found that food systems dominate at least four transgressed boundaries (biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change and biogeochemical flows) and strongly contribute to two more (climate change and novel entities); global food systems are currently beyond all nine food-system boundaries. The 2025 EAT–Lancet Commission summarises: food systems are the largest contributor to the transgression of five planetary boundaries (Rockström et al., 2025).

Climate change

Food systems — from land clearing and farming to processing, transport, retail, cooking and waste — emit roughly a quarter to a third of anthropogenic greenhouse gases. Poore & Nemecek (2018) estimated 13.7 Gt CO₂e per year (26 %) for the food supply chain; Crippa et al. (2021), using the EDGAR-FOOD database, estimated 18 Gt CO₂e in 2015 (34 %); the 2025 EAT–Lancet Commission uses about 30 %. The farm stage dominates: 61 % of food's GHG emissions, 81 % including deforestation (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). Unlike the energy sector, much of it is methane (from ruminants, rice paddies and manure) and nitrous oxide (from fertilised soils), which cannot be eliminated by switching to renewable electricity. The Commission warns that even if the global energy transition succeeds, food-system emissions alone would push the world past 1.5 °C.

Land-system change and biosphere integrity

Agriculture covers about 43 % of the world's ice- and desert-free land (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). Conversion of forests, savannahs and wetlands to cropland and pasture is the main cause of habitat loss, which in turn is the main driver of the extinction crisis. Richardson et al. (2023) introduced human appropriation of net primary production (HANPP) as a control variable for functional biosphere integrity: humans now appropriate about 30 % of the Holocene net primary production — through harvest, grazing and land-use change — against a boundary of 10 %.

Freshwater change

Irrigation accounts for about two thirds of human freshwater withdrawals and drives 90–95 % of global scarcity-weighted water use, because it is concentrated in dry regions and seasons (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). The 2023 update replaced the earlier global limit on consumptive blue-water use with two new control variables — the share of ice-free land where streamflow (blue water) or root-zone soil moisture (green water) deviates from its pre-industrial variability (Wang-Erlandsson et al., 2022). Both are transgressed, and agriculture, through irrigation, drainage and land conversion, is a principal cause.

Nitrogen and phosphorus

Before industrialisation, reactive nitrogen entered ecosystems only through lightning and biological fixation. Since the Haber–Bosch process made it possible to fix atmospheric N₂ into ammonia with fossil energy, humans fix about 190 Tg N per year industrially and through legume crops — three times the boundary of 62 Tg. Most of it is applied as fertiliser, but only part ends up in food; the rest cascades through the environment as ammonia (air pollution, deposition), nitrate (groundwater, eutrophication) and nitrous oxide (a greenhouse gas and the most important remaining ozone-depleting emission). Phosphorus, mined from finite rock deposits, washes from fertilised soils into lakes and seas, where it drives algal blooms and oxygen-free "dead zones" — the Baltic Sea is a textbook example.

Nitrogen-use efficiency and nitrogen surplus(1.2.2)
\[ \text{NUE} = \frac{N_{\text{out}}}{N_{\text{in}}}, \qquad N_{\text{surplus}} = N_{\text{in}} - N_{\text{out}} = N_{\text{in}}\,(1-\text{NUE}) \]

nitrogen-use efficiency is the nitrogen removed in harvested products divided by the nitrogen applied; the surplus is what is applied but not removed, and it is the nitrogen at risk of being lost to the environment.

Assumptions and validity

The surplus is an upper bound on losses in a single year: some surplus nitrogen accumulates in soil organic matter (or is drawn from it), so NUE above 1 is possible for a while when soils are mined. The 2025 food-system boundary for nitrogen is expressed as a global surplus of 57 Tg N yr⁻¹, not as fixation, precisely because surplus is closer to what causes harm (te Wierik et al., 2025).

(b) The global food-system boundary for N surplus is 57 Tg N yr⁻¹ (te Wierik et al., 2025). Spread over roughly 1.5 billion hectares of cropland, what average surplus per hectare does that allow?

Reveal the reasoning

Novel entities, ozone, aerosols and the ocean

Pesticides, veterinary antimicrobials and agricultural plastics are among the novel entities released faster than they can be assessed; the 2025 food-system boundaries propose limits of about 1 Tg of pesticide active substances per year and a halt to prophylactic antimicrobial use (te Wierik et al., 2025). Nitrous oxide from fertilised soils is now the largest emission of an ozone-depleting substance. Ammonia from manure and fertiliser forms secondary particulate matter (aerosols), and agricultural burning adds more. Ocean acidification is driven by CO₂ — including that from deforestation for agriculture.

Sweden and the Baltic

The Baltic Sea is one of the world's largest areas of oxygen-depleted bottom water, caused largely by nitrogen and phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater in the surrounding countries. It shows that biogeochemical boundaries have a strong regional dimension: global averages can hide local overshoot. Moberg et al. (2020) benchmarked the Swedish diet against global and national environmental targets and highlighted how the choice of indicators and data gaps (for example, for pesticides and biodiversity) shapes the verdict.

From planetary to personal: budgets and overshoot

A global boundary becomes actionable when it is translated into a budget for a country, a sector, a company or a person. The simplest principle is equal per-capita sharing: divide the part of the boundary allocated to food by the number of people.

Per-capita budget (equal shares)(1.2.3)
\[ b = \frac{s_{\text{food}}\;B}{P} \]

the per-capita budget equals the share of the planetary boundary allocated to the food system times the boundary, divided by the population.

Other sharing principles

Equal per-capita sharing is only one of several ethical principles. Häyhä et al. (2016) distinguish equality (equal shares), rights and needs (e.g., more for those below a social foundation), capability (more responsibility for those able to pay) and responsibility (accounting for historical overuse); they also show how boundaries can be allocated between sectors, countries and supply chains, and why consumption-based accounting (including imports) matters for countries such as Sweden that import much of their food and feed.

\[ F = 365\sum_{i} q_i\,e_i, \qquad O = \frac{F}{b} \]

Assumptions and validity

The footprint and the budget must measure the same thing. The EAT–Lancet climate boundary (5 Gt CO₂e yr⁻¹) covers the non-CO₂ gases methane and nitrous oxide from food production, because CO₂ from energy should go to zero anyway, whereas typical LCA footprints include CO₂ from energy and land-use change. Likewise, a cropland boundary must be compared with cropland use, not with cropland plus pasture. This kind of mismatch is one of the "indicator limitations" analysed by Moberg et al. (2020).

The planetary health diet and food-system boundaries

In January 2019 the EAT–Lancet Commission — 37 scientists from 16 countries — published the first global scientific targets for healthy diets from sustainable food production (Willett et al., 2019). It proposed six global food-system boundaries with uncertainty ranges: greenhouse-gas emissions (CH₄ and N₂O) of 5 Gt CO₂e yr⁻¹; cropland use of 13 million km² (11–15); freshwater use of 2,500 km³ yr⁻¹ (1,000–4,000); nitrogen application of 90 Tg N yr⁻¹ (65–90); phosphorus application of 8 Tg P yr⁻¹ (6–16, depending on recycling); and an extinction rate of 10 E/MSY. It showed that a planetary health diet of about 2,500 kcal per day — rich in whole grains, vegetables, fruits, legumes and nuts, with modest amounts of dairy, fish and poultry and very little red meat and sugar — could, combined with productivity gains and halved food loss and waste, feed about 10 billion people within those boundaries.

In October 2025 the second EAT–Lancet Commission, on healthy, sustainable, and just food systems, updated the evidence (Rockström et al., 2025). Its key messages include:

  • The updated planetary health diet (PHD) is based on health evidence alone, flexible across cultures, for a population-level energy intake of about 2,400 kcal per day (Table 2). A shift to it could prevent about 15 million deaths per year — roughly 27 % of all deaths.
  • Food systems are the largest contributor to the transgression of five planetary boundaries and account for about 30 % of greenhouse-gas emissions.
  • A transformation in line with the Commission (dietary shifts, more and more sustainable productivity, less loss and waste) would by 2050 reduce agricultural land use by 11–18 %, greenhouse-gas emissions by 55–67 %, water use by 9 % and nitrogen and phosphorus use by 26 %, compared with business as usual. Dietary change has the largest potential, but no single action is sufficient.
  • Justice matters: the richest 30 % of people drive more than 70 % of food-related environmental impacts, while nearly half of humanity lacks secure rights to adequate food, a healthy environment or decent work.

Combining levers: what the models say

Springmann et al. (2018) modelled five environmental domains — greenhouse gases, cropland, blue water, nitrogen and phosphorus — to 2050. Driven by population and income growth, the environmental effects of the food system could increase by 50–90 % between 2010 and 2050 in the absence of technological change and dedicated mitigation, reaching levels beyond the planetary boundaries. They examined dietary change towards healthier, more plant-based diets, improvements in technologies and management, and reductions in food loss and waste, and concluded that no single measure is enough to keep all effects within the boundaries simultaneously; a synergistic combination is needed. Why combinations are so powerful becomes clear from a simple multiplicative model.

Multiplicative combination of mitigation levers(1.2.5)
\[ \frac{I_{2050}}{B} = \frac{I_{2010}}{B}\;G\;\prod_{k}\left(1-r_k\right) \]

the pressure in 2050 relative to the boundary equals today's pressure relative to the boundary, times the business-as-usual growth factor, times the product of one minus the fractional reduction achieved by each lever.

SymbolMeaningUnitIllustrative value
\(I_{2010}/B\)initial pressure as a multiple of the boundary–1.0–3.0
\(G\)business-as-usual growth factor 2010–2050–1.5–1.9 (Springmann et al., 2018)
\(r_k\)fractional reduction from lever \(k\) (diet, technology, loss and waste)–0–0.7
Assumptions and validity

Each lever acts on the pressure left after the other levers, so reductions multiply rather than add: 30 % and 30 % give 51 %, not 60 %. Real levers interact — a diet with less meat needs less feed, which makes some feed-efficiency technologies less important — so integrated models such as Springmann's compute the combinations explicitly. The equation is a first-order teaching model, not a substitute for them.

  2. Single levers: diet \(2.04\times0.70 = 1.43\); technology \(2.04\times0.75 = 1.53\); waste \(2.04\times0.90 = 1.84\). None gets below 1.
  3. Combination: \(2.04\times0.70\times0.75\times0.90 = 0.96\) — just inside the boundary.
  4. Single lever needed: \(1 - 1/2.04 = 0.51\): one lever would have to cut the pressure by 51 %, far beyond what any single measure achieves in the models.
No single lever suffices (1.43–1.84); combined 0.96; a lone lever would need −51 %

Gerten et al. (2020) reached a complementary conclusion for four terrestrial boundaries (biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater and nitrogen): almost half of current food production depends on transgressing them; respecting them strictly with today's system would feed only 3.4 billion people a balanced diet; but redistributing cropland, improving water and nutrient management, reducing waste and changing diets could feed 10.2 billion.

The doughnut: a social foundation under an ecological ceiling

A safe operating space is not enough; it must also be just. The economist Kate Raworth combined the planetary boundaries (an outer ecological ceiling) with a set of social minimum standards drawn from the Sustainable Development Goals — food, water, health, education, income, housing, energy, political voice, equity (an inner social foundation). Between the two lies a doughnut-shaped "safe and just space for humanity" (Raworth, 2017a, 2017b). O'Neill et al. (2018) applied the doughnut to about 150 countries and found that no country currently meets the basic needs of its citizens at a globally sustainable level of resource use: countries that achieve the social thresholds overshoot the biophysical ones, and vice versa.

Rockström et al. (2023) formalised this as safe and just Earth system boundaries: for each domain, the stricter of a "safe" boundary (Earth-system stability) and a "just" boundary (avoiding significant harm to people) applies. Justice considerations were more constraining than safety for climate and aerosols, and seven of eight globally quantified safe and just boundaries were already exceeded.

Critiques and limits of the framework

The planetary boundaries are influential in science and policy — and contested. A good engineer uses them with an understanding of their limits.

  • Global thresholds may not exist for every process. Brook et al. (2013) argued that because drivers and responses of the terrestrial biosphere are spatially heterogeneous and weakly connected across continents, global change is likely to be smooth rather than tipping; characterising global biotic change with single aggregates may be inapt. Montoya et al. (2018) went further, calling the biodiversity boundary "implausible science" that could encourage "pernicious policies" — for example, accepting local losses as long as a global number stays below its limit.
  • Boundaries embed value judgements. Where exactly to put a precautionary limit, which control variable to use and which reference state to protect are partly normative decisions; Lewis (2012) urged that boundaries be set wisely and transparently, and Biermann & Kim (2020) review how such choices shape what counts as "safe" and for whom.
  • Control variables change. The freshwater boundary changed from a global volume of consumptive blue-water use to the share of land with deviations in streamflow and soil moisture; the ocean-acidification status changed because of a revised pre-industrial reference. Always report which version you use.
  • Scale. Phosphorus, freshwater, aerosols and land have strong regional effects that a single global number cannot capture — hence the regional sub-boundaries introduced in 2015.
  • Allocation is political. Converting global boundaries into national or personal budgets (Section 1.2.4) requires ethical choices between equality, needs, capability and historical responsibility (Häyhä et al., 2016).
Common misconception

"If a boundary is transgressed, the damage has already happened, so it no longer matters." The boundaries mark rising risk, not a cliff edge. Every tonne of nitrogen surplus or hectare of forest cleared beyond the boundary increases the probability and severity of harmful change; moving back towards the safe space always reduces risk. Conversely, being inside a global boundary does not guarantee safety locally.

Frontier · as of 2026

The Planetary Health Check — an annual assessment launched by the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research in 2024 — now tracks all nine boundaries with observational data and Earth-system models. The 2025 edition (Sakschewski et al., 2025) declared ocean acidification transgressed; the 2026 edition, released in September 2026, confirms seven of nine transgressed, focuses on the weakening land carbon sink and on novel entities, and notes that only ozone and aerosols have improved. At the same time, food-system boundaries (te Wierik et al., 2025) give the food sector its own quantitative targets for the first time — the basis for science-based targets for food companies and national food strategies.

Key terms

Planetary boundary
A precautionary limit on a control variable of an Earth-system process, set at the safe end of the zone of uncertainty about dangerous change.
Safe operating space
The region of human activity within all planetary boundaries, where the Earth system is likely to remain in a Holocene-like state.
Control variable
The measurable quantity used to track a boundary (e.g., CO₂ concentration, N fixation, forest cover).
Zone of increasing risk
The range between the boundary and the upper end of the scientific uncertainty, where the risk of large or irreversible change rises.
Transgression ratio
(x − xH)/(xB − xH): distance from the Holocene value in units of the safe-space width; above 1 means transgressed.
Biosphere integrity
The genetic diversity and functional capacity of the living world; tracked by extinction rates and by human appropriation of net primary production (HANPP).
Green and blue water
Green water: soil moisture available to plants; blue water: water in rivers, lakes and aquifers.
Nitrogen-use efficiency (NUE)
Nitrogen in harvested products divided by nitrogen inputs; its complement multiplied by the input is the N surplus.
Novel entities
Synthetic chemicals, plastics and other new substances or modified life forms that can have large, persistent effects on the Earth system.
Planetary health diet
The EAT–Lancet reference diet: plant-rich, flexible, with modest animal-source foods; about 2,400 kcal d⁻¹ in the 2025 version.
Food-system boundaries
The shares of the planetary boundaries allocated to the food system (e.g., 5 Gt CO₂e yr⁻¹, 57 Tg N surplus yr⁻¹).
Overshoot ratio
A footprint divided by its fair-share budget; values above 1 mean the fair share is exceeded.
Doughnut
Raworth's framework combining a social foundation (minimum standards of well-being) with an ecological ceiling (planetary boundaries).

  • I can name the nine planetary boundaries, give the control variable of the food-related ones and state which are transgressed.
  • I can compute a transgression ratio for a "higher-is-worse" and a "lower-is-worse" control variable.
  • I can compute a nitrogen balance and explain why the N surplus, not the input, is closest to environmental harm.
  • I can derive a per-capita budget from a food-system boundary and compare a diet footprint with it — and I can spot when the two are not comparable.
