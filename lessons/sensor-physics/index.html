How sensors work: from physical quantity to signal

Every number on a greenhouse dashboard — 21.4 °C, 72 % RH, 850 ppm CO₂, 380 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — is the last link of a long chain of physics, electronics and assumptions. When the chain is sound, the number is a faithful image of the crop's environment; when one link is weak, an algorithm will happily optimise the climate around a fiction. This lesson opens the black box: how a physical quantity changes a resistance, a capacitance, a current or a light intensity, how that change becomes a voltage and then a number, and how you judge whether the number can be trusted.

  • Design a voltage divider for a thermistor, calculate ADC resolution and convert raw ADC codes into temperature with the β and Steinhart–Hart equations (LO3, LO5).
  • Explain how RTDs, thermocouples, capacitive humidity sensors, quantum PAR sensors, NDIR CO₂ sensors, dielectric moisture sensors, load cells and cameras work and what limits their accuracy (LO5).
  • Apply pressure–temperature compensation to NDIR readings and the Topp equation to dielectric permittivity measurements (LO3, LO5).
  • Select and justify sensors for your own soilless growing project in terms of range, accuracy, response time, robustness and cost (LO4, LO5).

Why measurement comes before intelligence

Artificial intelligence in food production is usually pictured as neural networks, digital twins and autonomous greenhouses. All of them stand on a humble foundation: sensors. A climate controller cannot keep the vapour pressure deficit at 0.8 kPa if the humidity sensor reads 5 percentage points high; a model predicting lettuce harvest date from light integrals inherits every error of the quantum sensor; a nutrient-dosing robot fed by a drifting pH electrode will dose acid into a solution that did not need it. The old engineering rule "garbage in, garbage out" has a sharper modern form: a data-driven system can be no more trustworthy than its least trustworthy sensor.

This matters directly for you. In the course project you will log water temperature, electrical conductivity (EC), pH, dissolved oxygen, light (PPFD) and air temperature and humidity — possibly with a home-built logger based on an ESP32 microcontroller. Each of those numbers is produced by a different physical principle with its own quirks. This lesson treats the physical sensors; Lesson 9.2 covers the electrochemical ones (pH, EC, oxygen); Lesson 9.3 teaches you to calibrate and to put an honest uncertainty on every value; and Lesson 9.4 connects sensors to networks and the cloud.

Big idea

A sensor never measures what you want directly. It measures some physical effect — a change in resistance, capacitance, current, voltage or light — that depends on what you want and on other things. Understanding the physics tells you both how to convert the signal into the quantity of interest and which "other things" can fool you.

The measurement chain

Metrologists call the quantity you intend to measure the measurand (JCGM, 2012). Between the measurand and the number in your database lies a chain of stages, and each stage can add error, delay and noise. Figure 1 follows a water-temperature measurement through the chain that you will build in your own logger.

The stages are:

  1. Measurand — defined precisely: not "temperature" but "temperature of the nutrient solution at 10 cm depth in tank A, averaged over one minute". A vague definition is a source of uncertainty in its own right.
  2. Sensing element (transducer) — converts the measurand into another physical quantity. Modulating (passive) transducers change a property such as resistance or capacitance and need an external excitation (a thermistor, an RTD, a capacitive humidity sensor). Self-generating (active) transducers produce electrical energy themselves (a thermocouple, a photodiode in photovoltaic mode, a galvanic oxygen cell).
  3. Signal conditioning — excitation, amplification, filtering, linearisation, isolation. It turns the transducer output into a clean voltage in the range the converter accepts.
  4. Analogue-to-digital conversion — samples the voltage at discrete times and quantises it into an integer.
  5. Calibration and conversion — a mathematical model with calibrated coefficients turns the integer back into the measurand in SI units.
  6. Storage with metadata — time stamp (in UTC), unit, sensor identity, calibration version and a quality flag. Without metadata a number is not a measurement but a rumour.

Whatever the principle, the heart of the chain is the transfer function: the relationship between the measurand \(x\) and the sensor output \(y\).

Transfer function, sensitivity and linearity error(9.1.1)
\[ y = f(x) \approx y_0 + S\,x + k\,x^{2}, \qquad S = \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}, \qquad e_{\text{lin}} = \frac{|k|\,x_{\text{FS}}^{2}/4}{y_{\text{FS}}-y_0}\times100\,\% \]

the sensor output equals an offset plus the sensitivity times the measurand plus a small curvature term; the sensitivity is the slope of the output with respect to the measurand; the linearity error is the largest gap between the curved characteristic and the straight line joining its end points, expressed as a percentage of the full-scale output span.

Follow the derivation of the linearity error
  1. Any smooth characteristic can be expanded in a Taylor series around \(x=0\): \(y = y_0 + S x + k x^2 + \dots\) For a well-designed sensor the quadratic term is small, and higher terms are smaller still.
  2. The end-point line passes through \((0, y_0)\) and \((x_{\text{FS}}, y_{\text{FS}})\), where \(y_{\text{FS}} = y_0 + S x_{\text{FS}} + k x_{\text{FS}}^2\). Its slope is \(S + k x_{\text{FS}}\), so the line is \(y_{\text{line}} = y_0 + (S + k x_{\text{FS}})x\).
  3. The deviation of the true curve from the line is \(d(x) = y - y_{\text{line}} = k x^2 - k x_{\text{FS}} x = k\,x\,(x - x_{\text{FS}})\).
  4. Set \(\mathrm{d}d/\mathrm{d}x = k(2x - x_{\text{FS}}) = 0\): the largest deviation is at mid-scale, \(x = x_{\text{FS}}/2\), where \(|d| = |k|\,x_{\text{FS}}^2/4\).
  5. Dividing by the output span \(y_{\text{FS}} - y_0\) and multiplying by 100 gives the linearity error in % of full scale (% FS), the form used on data sheets.
Assumptions and validity

Data sheets may instead quote linearity relative to the best-fit straight line (least squares), which halves the number for a pure quadratic bow. Always check which definition a manufacturer uses. Offsets and sensitivities of real sensors also depend on influence quantities such as temperature and supply voltage; these are specified separately as "temperature coefficient of zero" and "temperature coefficient of span".

Static characteristics: reading a data sheet

The static characteristics describe how the output relates to a measurand that is not changing. They are the first page of every data sheet, and you should be able to translate each term into a concrete statement about your data. The definitions below follow the International Vocabulary of Metrology (JCGM, 2012) and standard texts (Fraden, 2016).

Range and span
The interval of measurand values the sensor is specified for (e.g., 0–100 %RH), and its width. Outside the range the output may saturate or the sensor may be damaged.
Sensitivity
The slope \(S\) of the transfer function (Eq. 9.1.1). A Pt100 RTD has \(S \approx 0.39\) Ω K⁻¹; a 10 kΩ thermistor around 20 °C about −550 Ω K⁻¹ — more than a thousand times larger.
Offset (zero error)
The output when the measurand is zero, or more generally a constant shift of the characteristic.
Linearity
Maximum deviation from a straight line, in % of full scale.
Hysteresis
The difference between readings at the same measurand value approached from below and from above. Polymer humidity sensors typically show about ±1 %RH hysteresis because water sorption and desorption are not perfectly reversible.
Resolution
The smallest change in the measurand that produces a detectable change in the output. For a digital sensor it is usually one least-significant bit (LSB). Resolution is not accuracy.
Repeatability
Closeness of agreement between successive readings of the same measurand under the same conditions (same operator, sensor, place, short time). Reproducibility is the same under changed conditions.
Accuracy
Closeness of agreement between a measured value and the true value of the measurand. It combines all systematic and random errors; Lesson 9.3 replaces this qualitative term by the quantitative uncertainty.
Drift
Slow change of the characteristic with time (ageing, contamination). Apogee specifies less than 2 % per year for its SQ-500 quantum sensor (Apogee Instruments, n.d.).
Cross-sensitivity
Response to a quantity other than the measurand, e.g., a CO₂ sensor responding to pressure, or a capacitance moisture probe responding to salinity.

Common misconception

"My sensor displays 21.37 °C, so it is accurate to 0.01 °C." The number of displayed digits reflects the resolution of the converter, not the accuracy of the measurement. The DS18B20 digital thermometer, a favourite of hobby loggers, reports in steps of 0.0625 °C at 12-bit resolution but is specified as accurate only to ±0.5 °C between −10 and +85 °C (Analog Devices, n.d.). Sixteen of its displayed steps fit inside its accuracy band.

Dynamic characteristics: how fast does a sensor follow?

A sensor cannot respond instantly. A temperature probe must itself warm up; a humidity sensor's polymer must absorb water vapour; an NDIR cell must fill with new air by diffusion. The simplest — and remarkably often adequate — description of this lag is the first-order system. It follows directly from an energy balance on the sensing element.

First-order sensor dynamics and step response(9.1.2)
\[ \tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} + y = x(t), \qquad \tau = \frac{m\,c}{h\,A} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad y(t) = x_1 + (x_0 - x_1)\,e^{-t/\tau} \]

the time constant times the rate of change of the reading plus the reading equals the true value; the time constant is the heat capacity of the sensing element divided by the heat-transfer conductance to its surroundings. After a sudden step from \(x_0\) to \(x_1\), the reading approaches the new value exponentially.

\(y\)sensor reading (here: temperature of the sensing element)°C
\(x(t)\)true value of the measurand (fluid temperature)°C
\(\tau\)time constants
\(m\), \(c\)mass and specific heat capacity of the sensing elementkg, J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
\(h\)convective heat-transfer coefficientW m⁻² K⁻¹
\(A\)surface area of the elementm²
\(x_0, x_1\)measurand before and after the step°C
Follow the derivation
  1. Energy balance. A probe of mass \(m\) and specific heat \(c\) sits in a fluid at temperature \(x(t)\). Heat flows into it by convection at a rate \(hA\,(x - y)\), where \(y\) is the probe temperature. The rate of change of stored heat is \(mc\,\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}t\).
  2. Equate: \(mc\,\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} = hA\,(x - y)\). Divide by \(hA\) and rearrange: \(\dfrac{mc}{hA}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} + y = x\). The group \(mc/(hA)\) has units of time: it is the time constant \(\tau\).
  3. Step input. For \(t > 0\) the fluid temperature is constant, \(x = x_1\). Substitute \(z = y - x_1\): \(\tau\,\mathrm{d}z/\mathrm{d}t = -z\), so \(\mathrm{d}z/z = -\mathrm{d}t/\tau\).
  4. Integrate from 0 to \(t\): \(\ln[z(t)/z(0)] = -t/\tau\), i.e. \(z(t) = z(0)\,e^{-t/\tau}\).
  5. With \(z(0) = x_0 - x_1\) (the probe starts at the old temperature): \(y(t) = x_1 + (x_0 - x_1)e^{-t/\tau}\).
  6. Milestones. After \(t=\tau\) the probe has covered \(1 - e^{-1} = 63.2\,\%\) of the step (this is how \(\tau\) is measured and why data sheets quote "τ₆₃"); after \(3\tau\), 95.0 %; after \(4.6\tau\), 99.0 %; after \(5\tau\), 99.3 %.
  7. Time to settle within a tolerance. Solving \(|x_0 - x_1|e^{-t/\tau} = \delta\) gives \(t = \tau\ln(|x_0 - x_1|/\delta)\).
Ramp and sinusoidal inputs

Ramp. If the true value rises at a constant rate \(r\) (e.g., a greenhouse warming at 2 °C h⁻¹ in the morning), the solution of Eq. 9.1.2 is \(y = x_0 + r\,[t - \tau(1 - e^{-t/\tau})]\). After a few time constants the reading lags the truth by a constant dynamic error \(r\,\tau\), and is delayed by exactly \(\tau\).

Sine. If \(x = \bar x + a\sin\omega t\), the steady-state reading oscillates with a reduced amplitude \(a/\sqrt{1 + (\omega\tau)^2}\) and a phase lag \(\arctan(\omega\tau)\). Fast fluctuations are smoothed out: a slow sensor is a low-pass filter. This is sometimes useful (less noise) and sometimes dangerous (a short temperature spike that damages roots is never recorded).

Assumptions and validity

The element is assumed to have a uniform temperature (small Biot number), constant \(h\), and negligible heat conduction along its leads. A sensor inside a stainless-steel sheath with potting compound behaves like two or more first-order stages in series and its response starts with an S-shaped delay; the first-order model then still describes the later part of the response. \(h\) depends strongly on the fluid and its velocity: the same probe responds many times faster in stirred water than in still air, so a time constant is meaningless unless the medium and flow are stated.

\(\tau = \dfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}}{\text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^2} = \dfrac{\text{J K}^{-1}}{\text{J s}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}} = \text{s}\) ✓

On a sunny morning the air warms at 2.0 °C h⁻¹. How far does the reading lag behind the true air temperature?

Reveal the reasoning

  2. Solve for t. \(t = \tau\ln\!\left(\dfrac{|x_0 - x_1|}{\delta}\right) = 20\ \text{s}\times\ln\!\left(\dfrac{4.0}{0.1}\right) = 20\times3.689 = 73.8\ \text{s}\).

From resistance to numbers: dividers, bridges and ADCs

Microcontrollers measure voltages, not resistances. The simplest way to turn a resistance into a voltage is the voltage divider (Figure 4): the sensor and a fixed resistor in series across a supply, with the midpoint connected to the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC).

Voltage divider (sensor to ground)(9.1.3)
\[ V_{\text{out}} = V_s\,\frac{R_T}{R_f + R_T} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad R_T = R_f\,\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_s - V_{\text{out}}} \]

the output voltage is the supply voltage multiplied by the fraction of the total resistance that the sensor represents; inverted, the sensor resistance equals the fixed resistance times the output voltage divided by the voltage across the fixed resistor.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(V_s\)supply (excitation) voltageV
\(V_{\text{out}}\)voltage across the sensor (ADC input)V
\(R_T\)sensor (thermistor) resistanceΩ
\(R_f\)fixed series resistorΩ
Follow the derivation — and the best choice of Rf
  1. The same current \(I\) flows through both resistors (the ADC input draws negligible current): \(I = V_s/(R_f + R_T)\) by Ohm's law.
  2. The voltage across the sensor is \(V_{\text{out}} = I R_T = V_s R_T/(R_f + R_T)\).
  3. Solve for \(R_T\): \(V_{\text{out}}(R_f + R_T) = V_s R_T \Rightarrow R_T(V_s - V_{\text{out}}) = V_{\text{out}} R_f\), which gives the inverse form.
  4. Sensitivity. \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}V_{\text{out}}}{\mathrm{d}R_T} = V_s\dfrac{R_f}{(R_f + R_T)^2}\). For a given \(R_T\), this is largest when \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}R_f}\dfrac{R_f}{(R_f + R_T)^2} = \dfrac{R_T - R_f}{(R_f+R_T)^3} = 0\), i.e. \(R_f = R_T\).
  5. Design rule: choose the fixed resistor equal to the sensor resistance at the centre of the temperature range you care about. For nutrient solutions around 20 °C and a 10 kΩ/β 3950 thermistor, \(R_T(20\ ^{\circ}\text{C}) = 12.5\) kΩ, so 12 kΩ is a better choice than 10 kΩ (though the difference is small).
  6. Ratiometric trick. If the ADC reference equals \(V_s\), the code is \(\text{code} = 2^N V_{\text{out}}/V_s = 2^N R_T/(R_f + R_T)\): the supply voltage cancels and \(R_T = R_f\,\text{code}/(2^N - \text{code})\).
Assumptions and validity

The ADC input impedance must be much larger than \(R_f \parallel R_T\), or the sampling capacitor of a SAR ADC must have time to charge — the 100 nF capacitor in Figure 4 acts as a charge reservoir. The current through the sensor heats it (self-heating): the power is \(P = V_{\text{out}}^2/R_T\), and the temperature rise is \(P/\delta\), where \(\delta\) is the dissipation constant (mW K⁻¹) on the thermistor data sheet. Keep \(P\) well below 0.1 δ for errors below 0.1 K, or power the divider only while measuring.

\(\text{V}\cdot\dfrac{\Omega}{\Omega} = \text{V}\) ✓ and \(\Omega\cdot\dfrac{\text{V}}{\text{V}} = \Omega\) ✓

The ADC then turns the continuous voltage into an integer. A successive-approximation (SAR) converter with \(N\) bits divides its reference voltage into \(2^N\) equal steps.

ADC resolution and quantisation error(9.1.4)
\[ \text{LSB} = \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{2^{N}}, \qquad \text{code} = \left\lfloor \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{V_{\text{ref}}}\,2^{N} \right\rfloor, \qquad u_q = \frac{\text{LSB}}{\sqrt{12}}, \qquad \Delta x_{\min} = \frac{\text{LSB}}{|S|} \]

one least-significant bit is the reference voltage divided by two to the power of the number of bits; the code is the input voltage as a fraction of the reference, scaled to the number of steps and rounded down; the quantisation error behaves like random noise with a standard deviation of one LSB divided by the square root of twelve; the smallest resolvable change of the measurand is one LSB divided by the sensor's sensitivity.

Follow the derivation of LSB/√12
  1. Within one code the true voltage can be anywhere in an interval one LSB wide, and — for a signal that varies over many codes — every position is equally likely. The quantisation error \(e\) is therefore uniformly distributed between \(-\text{LSB}/2\) and \(+\text{LSB}/2\) (after centring the code).
  2. The variance of a uniform distribution of width \(w\) is \(\sigma^2 = \dfrac{1}{w}\displaystyle\int_{-w/2}^{w/2} e^2\,\mathrm{d}e = \dfrac{1}{w}\left[\dfrac{e^3}{3}\right]_{-w/2}^{w/2} = \dfrac{w^2}{12}\).

Assumptions and validity — resolution is not accuracy

Eq. 9.1.4 describes an ideal converter. Real converters also have offset, gain error, differential nonlinearity (DNL, unequal step widths) and integral nonlinearity (INL, a bowed transfer curve). The ESP32 data sheet specifies DNL of ±7 LSB and INL of ±12 LSB for its 12-bit SAR ADCs, and warns that accuracy is worse above about 2450 mV at the highest attenuation setting (Espressif Systems, 2025). The nominally 12-bit converter is thus closer to 8–9 useful bits unless you calibrate it. A dedicated converter such as the 16-bit ADS1115 (programmable full scale from ±0.256 V to ±6.144 V, 8–860 samples per second, I²C interface) gives a 62.5 µV LSB on its ±2.048 V range and much better linearity (Texas Instruments, 2018).

LSB: V/1 = V; \(\Delta x_{\min}\): V ÷ (V K⁻¹) = K ✓

Bridges are the second classic conditioning circuit. When a sensor's resistance changes by only a fraction of a percent — a strain gauge, or a Pt100 RTD over a small temperature range — a divider would give a large constant voltage with a tiny variation on top. A Wheatstone bridge subtracts the constant part by comparing two dividers, leaving a small differential voltage centred on zero that can be amplified strongly. We return to bridges with load cells in the last section.

Temperature sensors

Temperature is the most frequently measured quantity in food production: air, leaf, nutrient solution, substrate, fish tank, cold store. Four sensor families dominate (Figure 5, Table 2).

Thermistors: large signal, strongly nonlinear

An NTC (negative temperature coefficient) thermistor is a semiconductor: the number of mobile charge carriers grows exponentially with temperature, so its resistance falls exponentially. Near room temperature a typical NTC changes by about −4 % per kelvin, which makes it easy to read with a simple divider. The price is strong nonlinearity, handled by the β equation or the Steinhart–Hart equation.

β (B-parameter) model of an NTC thermistor(9.1.5)
\[ R_T = R_{25}\exp\!\left[\beta\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{25}}\right)\right] \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{T_{25}} + \frac{1}{\beta}\ln\frac{R_T}{R_{25}} \]

the thermistor resistance equals its resistance at 25 °C multiplied by the exponential of beta times the difference between the reciprocal absolute temperature and the reciprocal of 298.15 K; inverted, the reciprocal temperature is the reciprocal reference temperature plus the natural logarithm of the resistance ratio divided by beta.

\(R_T\)resistance at absolute temperature \(T\)Ω1–100 kΩ

Follow the derivation
  1. In a semiconductor the density of charge carriers follows a Boltzmann factor, \(n \propto \exp(-E_a/k_B T)\), where \(E_a\) is an activation energy. Resistance is inversely proportional to carrier density, so \(R \propto \exp(E_a/k_B T)\).
  2. Write \(\beta = E_a/k_B\) (units: kelvin) and take the ratio of resistances at \(T\) and at \(T_{25}\): \(R_T/R_{25} = \exp[\beta(1/T - 1/T_{25})]\).
  3. Take the natural logarithm and solve for \(1/T\): \(1/T = 1/T_{25} + \ln(R_T/R_{25})/\beta\).
  4. The temperature coefficient follows by differentiation: \(\alpha = \dfrac{1}{R}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}T} = -\dfrac{\beta}{T^2}\). At 25 °C with β = 3950 K: \(\alpha = -3950/298.15^2 = -0.0444\) K⁻¹ = −4.4 % per kelvin.
Assumptions and validity

β is not truly constant: for real thermistors it varies slightly with temperature, which is why data sheets specify it between two temperatures (B₂₅/₅₀, B₂₅/₈₅). Over ±25 K around 25 °C the β model is usually good to a few tenths of a kelvin; for better accuracy use the Steinhart–Hart equation or the manufacturer's resistance table. The library function thermistorR in this course implements Eq. 9.1.5.

Steinhart–Hart equation(9.1.6)
\[ \frac{1}{T} = A + B\,\ln R_T + C\,(\ln R_T)^{3} \]

the reciprocal of the absolute temperature is a constant plus a constant times the logarithm of the resistance plus a third constant times the cube of the logarithm of the resistance.

Follow the derivation — and how to find A, B and C
  1. The β model (Eq. 9.1.5) can be rewritten as \(1/T = (1/T_{25} - \ln R_{25}/\beta) + (1/\beta)\ln R_T\): a straight line in \(\ln R_T\). So it is the special case \(A = 1/T_{25} - \ln R_{25}/\beta\), \(B = 1/\beta\), \(C = 0\).
  2. Steinhart and Hart (1968), calibrating thermistors for deep-sea oceanography, found that a polynomial in \(\ln R\) fits real thermistors much better, and that the quadratic term can be dropped with negligible loss. The cubic term absorbs the temperature dependence of β.
  3. With three calibration points \((T_i, R_i)\), write \(L_i = \ln R_i\) and \(Y_i = 1/T_i\). Each point gives one linear equation \(A + B L_i + C L_i^3 = Y_i\). Three points give a 3 × 3 linear system that can be solved exactly (e.g., by Gaussian elimination or Cramer's rule).
  4. With more than three points, fit \(A, B, C\) by least squares (multiple linear regression of \(Y\) on \(L\) and \(L^3\)), which also gives an estimate of the fit residuals.

Thermocouples

When two different metals are joined and the junction is at a different temperature from their other ends, a voltage appears: the Seebeck effect. For small temperature differences the voltage is approximately \(V \approx S_{AB}(T_{\text{hot}} - T_{\text{ref}})\), where the Seebeck coefficient \(S_{AB}\) of a type K (chromel–alumel) couple is about 41 µV K⁻¹ near room temperature (Burns et al., 1993). Thermocouples are rugged, fast (a fine-wire junction has a time constant of fractions of a second) and cover extreme ranges — ideal for furnaces, dryers and the leaf-temperature measurements of plant physiologists. For crop environments their disadvantages dominate: a 1 °C change produces only 41 µV, which needs a precise amplifier, and the reading depends on an accurate measurement of the reference-junction temperature (cold-junction compensation). Their typical accuracy of ±1–2 °C is poor for climate control.

Integrated digital sensors

Most sensors you will buy for your project are smart: the sensing element, amplifier, ADC, calibration coefficients and a digital interface are on one chip. The DS18B20 (a band-gap silicon temperature sensor with a 1-Wire bus, 9–12-bit resolution, ±0.5 °C from −10 to +85 °C) is the standard waterproof tank probe; the Sensirion SHT4x family combines a capacitive humidity sensor with a temperature sensor (SHT40: ±1.8 %RH and ±0.2 °C typical) on a 1.5 mm chip with an I²C interface (Sensirion, n.d.-a). Digital sensors remove the analogue chain from your hands — but not its errors: they are hidden behind the factory calibration, which you should still verify.

Humidity and light

Capacitive humidity sensors

Almost every electronic hygrometer in a greenhouse is a capacitive polymer sensor (Farahani et al., 2014). A thin hygroscopic polymer film (often polyimide) forms the dielectric of a small capacitor (Figure 7). Water molecules diffuse from the air through a porous top electrode into the polymer until the film's water content is in equilibrium with the relative humidity. Because water has a relative permittivity of about 80 and the dry polymer only about 3–4, each absorbed molecule raises the permittivity \(\varepsilon_r\) and hence the capacitance of the parallel-plate structure, \(C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d\). An on-chip circuit converts the few-picofarad change into a digital RH value, and an on-chip temperature sensor allows temperature compensation.

Three practical facts follow from the physics. First, the polymer responds to relative humidity at its own temperature — so a sensor warmed by sunlight or by its own electronics reports a humidity that is too low. Second, sorption takes time: the SHT40 data sheet gives a humidity response time τ₆₃ of 4 s and a temperature response time of 2 s in moving air (Sensirion, n.d.-a), longer in still air. Third, near saturation (above about 90 %RH, common in greenhouses at night) accuracy degrades and condensation on the sensor can take hours to evaporate.

Practical warning: shield and ventilate

Air temperature and humidity sensors must sit in a ventilated radiation shield at crop height, away from heating pipes and lamps. In sunlight an unshielded sensor can read several degrees too warm; its RH is then too low and the vapour pressure deficit (VPD) calculated from them (Lesson 8.2) is badly wrong. Greenhouse climate computers use an aspirated "measuring box" with a small fan for exactly this reason.

Quantum (PAR) sensors

You met the quantum sensor in Lesson 4.1: a silicon photodiode behind filters that shape its spectral response to a flat 400–700 nm photon response, under a diffuser that gives it a cosine angular response. Here we look at it as a sensor engineer. A photodiode is a self-generating transducer: each absorbed photon with enough energy creates one electron–hole pair, and in short-circuit (photovoltaic) mode the photocurrent is proportional to the number of absorbed photons per second. A shunt resistor or a transimpedance amplifier turns the current into a voltage; a calibration factor (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ per mV) turns that into PPFD.

Its main error sources are the spectral error (mismatch with the ideal 400–700 nm response, largest under narrow-band LEDs close to the band edges; Barnes et al., 1993), the cosine error (largest for low sun angles and in diffuse light), calibration uncertainty and drift, and the temperature coefficient of the photodiode. The data sheet of a current full-spectrum sensor lists a calibration uncertainty of ±5 %, repeatability better than 0.5 %, drift below 2 % per year, a response time below 1 ms and a temperature response of −0.11 % per °C (Apogee Instruments, n.d.). Ross and Sulev (2000) give a systematic analysis of these errors. Notice the response time: a photodiode is essentially instantaneous, so the "dynamics" of light measurement are set by how often you sample (Lesson 9.3), not by the sensor.

Gases and water in the root zone: NDIR CO₂ and dielectric sensors

Non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) CO₂ sensors

CO₂ molecules absorb infrared radiation strongly in a band centred near 4.26 µm, where their asymmetric stretching vibration is excited; the other major air components (N₂, O₂, Ar) do not absorb there, and water vapour absorbs only weakly in this window. An NDIR sensor (Figure 9) shines broadband infrared light from a small lamp or LED through a gas cell onto a detector behind a narrow band-pass filter at 4.26 µm. The more CO₂ molecules are in the path, the less light arrives (Hodgkinson & Tatam, 2013). "Non-dispersive" means that no prism or grating spreads the spectrum — the filter selects the band.

NDIR: Beer–Lambert law with pressure and temperature compensation(9.1.8)
\[ \frac{I}{I_0} = e^{-k\,c\,L},\qquad c = \frac{x\,P}{R\,T}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad x_{\text{true}} = x_{\text{reported}}\;\frac{P_{\text{cal}}}{P}\;\frac{T}{T_{\text{cal}}} \]

the transmitted intensity is the unattenuated intensity times the exponential of minus the absorption coefficient times the molar concentration of CO₂ times the path length; the molar concentration equals the mole fraction times the pressure divided by the gas constant times the temperature. A sensor that reports a mole fraction assuming calibration conditions must therefore be corrected by the ratio of pressures and temperatures.

Dielectric sensing of substrate and soil water

The relative permittivity of liquid water (≈ 80) is roughly 20 times that of mineral soil particles (≈ 3–5) and 80 times that of air (1). The bulk apparent permittivity \(\varepsilon_a\) of a moist porous medium is therefore dominated by its water content, and measuring it is the basis of almost every electronic soil and substrate moisture sensor (Robinson et al., 2003). Two families exist. Time-domain reflectometry (TDR) sends a fast voltage step along two or three parallel metal rods inserted in the medium and times the reflection from the rod ends (Figure 10): electromagnetic waves travel at \(c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_a}\), so wetter media slow the pulse. Capacitance or frequency-domain (FDR) sensors make the medium the dielectric of a capacitor in an oscillator circuit and measure the resulting frequency or charge time, typically at tens of MHz. They are cheaper but more affected by salinity and temperature (Blonquist et al., 2005).

TDR permittivity and the Topp equation(9.1.9)
\[ \varepsilon_a = \left(\frac{c\,t}{2L}\right)^{2}, \qquad \theta_v = -5.3\times10^{-2} + 2.92\times10^{-2}\,\varepsilon_a - 5.5\times10^{-4}\,\varepsilon_a^{2} + 4.3\times10^{-6}\,\varepsilon_a^{3} \]

the apparent permittivity equals the square of the speed of light times the two-way travel time divided by twice the rod length; the volumetric water content is an empirical third-order polynomial in the apparent permittivity.

Mass and images: load cells and cameras

Strain gauges, bridges and load cells

Weighing is one of the most powerful and underrated measurements in plant science. A substrate slab or gutter standing on a load cell tells you, minute by minute, how much water the crop transpires and when the irrigation drains — the principle of a weighing lysimeter. A load cell is a machined metal beam carrying four metal-foil strain gauges. Stretching a gauge makes its conductor longer and thinner and raises its resistance: \(\Delta R/R = GF\cdot\varepsilon\), where the gauge factor \(GF\approx2\) for metal foil and \(\varepsilon\) is the strain. At the rated load the strain is only about 10⁻³, so \(\Delta R/R\approx0.2\,\%\): a job for the Wheatstone bridge (Figure 11).

\[ V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{ex}}\left(\frac{R_3}{R_2 + R_3} - \frac{R_4}{R_1 + R_4}\right)\;\approx\; V_{\text{ex}}\,GF\,\varepsilon \ \ \text{(full bridge)},\qquad V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{ex}}\;\text{RO}\;\frac{F}{F_{\text{cap}}} \]

the bridge output is the difference between the voltages of its two dividers; with four active gauges it is the excitation voltage times the gauge factor times the strain. For a commercial load cell it equals the excitation voltage times the rated output (in millivolts per volt) times the fraction of rated capacity applied.

The real challenges are elsewhere: temperature drift of the zero over a day, creep, plants growing (their own mass increases), splashing water and people leaning on the gutter. Log the weight at high frequency and interpret changes with care.

Cameras as sensors

A digital camera is an array of millions of photodiodes, each covered by a red, green or blue filter in a Bayer mosaic (Figure 12). It is therefore a spatially resolved light sensor — and, with the right processing, a sensor of leaf area, colour, disease and growth (Li et al., 2014; Lesson 10.3). To use it quantitatively you must respect the same chain as for any sensor. The raw pixel value is approximately proportional to the number of photons collected during the exposure; a standard JPEG is not: the camera has already interpolated the mosaic, applied an automatic white balance, a colour matrix and a strongly nonlinear "gamma" curve. Changing light (sun, clouds, LED recipe) changes the colours of a healthy plant. For measurements, fix the exposure and white balance, include a colour reference card in the image, and prefer raw files or at least fixed camera settings.

Frontier — sensors in 2026

Three developments are changing crop sensing. Low-cost smart sensors with digital interfaces and factory calibration (photoacoustic CO₂, MEMS humidity, 16-bit ADCs) have made a research-grade climate logger affordable for students. Soft sensors — quantities estimated by a model from other measurements, such as crop transpiration from load-cell and climate data — are increasingly used in commercial greenhouses. And imaging is moving from RGB to multispectral, thermal and chlorophyll-fluorescence cameras on rails and robots. All three make calibration, metadata and uncertainty (Lesson 9.3) more, not less, important.

Key terms

Measurand
The quantity intended to be measured, defined with its conditions (place, medium, averaging time).
Transducer
A device that converts one physical quantity into another, usually into an electrical signal; modulating (needs excitation) or self-generating.
Sensitivity
The slope of the transfer function, output change per unit change of the measurand.
Hysteresis
Difference between readings at the same input approached from below and from above.
Resolution
The smallest change of the measurand that produces a detectable output change; for an ADC one LSB. Not the same as accuracy.
Time constant (τ)
For a first-order sensor, the time to cover 63.2 % of a step change; depends on the medium and flow.
Voltage divider
Two resistances in series across a supply; converts a resistance into a voltage, Eq. 9.1.3.
ADC / LSB
Analogue-to-digital converter; the least-significant bit is the voltage step of one code, \(V_{\text{ref}}/2^N\).
NTC thermistor
A semiconductor resistor whose resistance falls exponentially with temperature; described by the β or Steinhart–Hart equation.
RTD (Pt100)
Platinum resistance thermometer with 100 Ω at 0 °C, described by the Callendar–Van Dusen equation.
Seebeck effect
Generation of a voltage in a circuit of two different conductors whose junctions are at different temperatures — the thermocouple principle.
NDIR
Non-dispersive infrared gas sensing: concentration from the absorption of a filtered IR band (4.26 µm for CO₂) via the Beer–Lambert law.
Apparent permittivity
The bulk dielectric constant of a moist porous medium; dominated by water content and measured by TDR or capacitance sensors.
Wheatstone bridge
Two voltage dividers compared differentially to measure small resistance changes such as those of strain gauges.
