Machine learning for food production

A greenhouse produces millions of numbers a year — temperatures, humidities, light sums, irrigation volumes, images, yields. Machine learning is the craft of letting an algorithm discover patterns in such data that are too complex to write down by hand: which combination of conditions precedes a disease outbreak, how much the crop will yield next week, which berry is ripe. This lesson opens the black box. You will derive how a decision tree chooses its questions, how a neural network learns by gradient descent, and — most importantly — how to tell whether a model will work on a farm it has never seen.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify a food-production problem as supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement learning, and identify its features and labels (LO5).
  • Explain and calculate how decision trees choose splits (Gini impurity, entropy, information gain), and why random forests and gradient boosting outperform single trees (LO5).
  • Apply k-means clustering and principal component analysis to agricultural data and interpret the results (LO5).
  • Compute the forward pass of a small neural network, derive backpropagation with the chain rule, and explain learning rate and regularisation (LO5).
  • Evaluate classifiers and regressors with confusion matrices, precision, recall, F1, ROC/AUC and RMSE, and recognise data leakage and the need for spatial and temporal cross-validation (LO4, LO5).
  • Critically assess published applications of machine learning, foundation models and large language models in agriculture (LO5, LO1).

What machine learning is — and the three ways machines learn

In Lesson 10.1 you chose a model (a logistic curve) and let least squares find its three parameters. Machine learning pushes the same idea further: the model family is so flexible — thousands or millions of parameters — that it can represent almost any relationship, and the data decide its shape. A useful working definition: machine learning is the estimation of a function from examples, with the explicit goal of performing well on new examples. Everything else — trees, forests, neural networks — is a choice of function family and fitting algorithm.

Three settings are distinguished by what the data contain:

  • Supervised learning. Each example has inputs (features, \(\mathbf x\)) and a known answer (label or target, \(y\)). The algorithm learns \(\hat y = f(\mathbf x)\). If \(y\) is a number (yield in kg m⁻², days to harvest) the task is regression; if it is a category (healthy/diseased, ripe/unripe, grade A/B/C) it is classification.
  • Unsupervised learning. There are no labels; the algorithm looks for structure — groups of similar greenhouse compartments (clustering), a few combined variables that summarise a 200-band spectrum (dimensionality reduction), or sensor readings that do not fit the usual pattern (anomaly detection, see the sensor anomaly lab).
  • Reinforcement learning. An agent takes actions (open vents, switch on lamps) in an environment, receives a reward (profit, penalty for leaving the temperature band) and learns a policy that maximises long-term reward. It is the machine-learning route to autonomous control, covered in Lesson 10.4.

Whatever the algorithm, a machine-learning project follows the same workflow (Figure 2). The steps that decide success are rarely the algorithm itself: they are the definition of the question, the quality and representativeness of the data, the choice of features, and above all an honest validation on data the model has never seen.

Decision trees, random forests and boosting

A decision tree predicts by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about the features: Was the leaf wet for at least 6 hours? If so, was the mean temperature between 11 and 22 °C? Each question splits the data into two groups; each final group (a leaf of the tree) predicts the majority class or the mean value of its training examples. Trees are popular in agriculture because they handle mixed feature types, need no scaling, capture interactions and thresholds (biology is full of thresholds) and can be read by a grower.

How does the algorithm choose a question? It tries every feature and every possible threshold, and picks the split that makes the two resulting groups as pure as possible. Purity is measured by an impurity function.

Impurity and information gain(10.2.1)
\[ G = 1-\sum_{c=1}^{C} p_c^{2}, \qquad H = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} p_c\log_2 p_c, \qquad \Delta I = I_{\text{parent}} - \frac{n_L}{n}I_L - \frac{n_R}{n}I_R \]

the Gini impurity of a group is one minus the sum of the squared class proportions; the entropy is minus the sum of each proportion times its base-2 logarithm. A split is scored by its gain: the impurity of the parent minus the size-weighted impurities of the left and right children. The tree chooses the split with the largest gain.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(p_c\)fraction of examples in the group that belong to class \(c\)–
\(G\)Gini impurity: probability that two randomly drawn examples (with replacement) belong to different classes– (0 to 0.5 for two classes)
\(H\)entropy: average number of yes/no questions needed to identify the classbit (0 to 1 for two classes)
\(n, n_L, n_R\)number of examples in parent, left and right child–
\(\Delta I\)information gain (impurity decrease) of the split, with \(I = G\) or \(H\)– or bit
Where do these formulas come from?
  1. Gini. Draw two examples at random with replacement. The probability both are class \(c\) is \(p_c^2\); the probability they are the same class is \(\sum p_c^2\); the probability they differ is \(1-\sum p_c^2\). A pure group has \(G=0\); two equally mixed classes give \(G = 1-0.25-0.25 = 0.5\).
  2. Entropy comes from Shannon's information theory: an event of probability \(p\) carries \(-\log_2 p\) bits of information; entropy is the expected information. A 50/50 group has \(H = 1\) bit.
  3. Weighting. The children are weighted by their sizes so that splitting off a single pure example (which trivially has zero impurity) is not rewarded.
  4. Regression trees use the variance of \(y\) within each group as impurity; the “gain” is then the reduction in sum of squared errors — least squares again.
Assumptions and validity

The greedy search finds the best split at each step, not the best tree overall. A tree allowed to grow until every leaf is pure will memorise the training data (over-fitting); depth limits, a minimum number of examples per leaf, or pruning are needed. Small changes in the data can change the first split and hence the whole tree — trees are high-variance models.

Proportions are dimensionless, so \(G\) is dimensionless; \(H\) is in bits because the logarithm is base 2 (use natural logarithms for nats) ✓.
Worked example 1Scoring a split for downy-mildew risk

On 20 days a lettuce greenhouse recorded whether downy mildew (Bremia lactucae) infections occurred: 8 days yes, 12 days no. A candidate split “leaf wetness ≥ 6 h” sends 9 days left (7 infection, 2 no infection) and 11 days right (1 infection, 10 no infection). Compute the Gini impurities, the gain, and the gain measured with entropy.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Parent. \(p = 8/20 = 0.4\): \(G = 1-0.4^2-0.6^2 = 0.480\).
  2. Left child. \(p = 7/9\): \(G_L = 1-(7/9)^2-(2/9)^2 = 1-0.605-0.049 = 0.346\).
  3. Right child. \(p = 1/11\): \(G_R = 1-(1/11)^2-(10/11)^2 = 0.165\).
  4. Weighted children. \(\frac{9}{20}0.346+\frac{11}{20}0.165 = 0.156+0.091 = 0.246\). Gain = 0.480 − 0.246 = 0.234.
  5. Entropy version. \(H = 0.971\) bit (parent), 0.764 (left), 0.439 (right); weighted 0.586; gain 0.385 bit. Both criteria rank splits almost identically in practice; Gini is slightly cheaper to compute.
Gini gain ≈ 0.23 (0.480 → 0.246); entropy gain ≈ 0.39 bit

Random forests: the wisdom of many noisy trees

Because a single deep tree has low bias but high variance, Breiman (2001) proposed averaging many of them. A random forest grows hundreds of trees, each on a bootstrap sample of the data (drawn with replacement) and each allowed to consider only a random subset of features at every split. The randomness makes the trees different from each other; averaging their predictions (regression) or taking a majority vote (classification) cancels much of their individual variance.

Variance of an average of correlated trees(10.2.2)
\[ \operatorname{Var}\Bigl[\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat f_b(\mathbf x)\Bigr] = \rho\,\sigma^{2} + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^{2} \]

the variance of the forest's prediction equals the correlation between trees times the variance of a single tree, plus the remaining uncorrelated part divided by the number of trees. Adding trees removes the second term; only making the trees less correlated reduces the first.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(B\)number of trees–
\(\sigma^2\)variance of one tree's prediction (over random training samples)unit of \(y\) squared
\(\rho\)pairwise correlation between the predictions of two trees–
Follow the derivation
  1. \(\operatorname{Var}[\frac1B\sum_b \hat f_b] = \frac{1}{B^2}\bigl[\sum_b \operatorname{Var}\hat f_b + \sum_{b\neq c}\operatorname{Cov}(\hat f_b,\hat f_c)\bigr]\).
  2. With identical variances \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise covariances \(\rho\sigma^2\): \(\frac{1}{B^2}[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2] = \frac{\sigma^2}{B} + \frac{B-1}{B}\rho\sigma^2\).
  3. Rearranging: \(\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2\) ∎. This is why random forests restrict the features available at each split: it lowers \(\rho\) (Hastie et al., 2009, ch. 15).
Gradient boosting: trees in series instead of in parallel

Boosting builds trees one after another. Each new shallow tree \(h_m\) is fitted to the residuals (more generally the negative gradient of the loss) of the current ensemble, and added with a small learning rate \(\nu\): \(F_m(\mathbf x) = F_{m-1}(\mathbf x) + \nu\,h_m(\mathbf x)\) (Friedman, 2001). Where forests reduce variance, boosting reduces bias step by step. Implementations such as XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) and LightGBM are often the most accurate methods for tabular sensor and climate data — and they need careful early stopping to avoid over-fitting.

Both terms are \(\sigma^2\) multiplied by dimensionless factors → unit of \(y\) squared ✓.

Unsupervised learning: k-means clustering and PCA

Imagine you have extracted the colour of 150 lettuce leaves from photos, expressed in the CIELAB colour space (Lesson 10.3): \(a^*\) runs from green (negative) to red (positive), \(b^*\) from blue to yellow. Nobody has labelled the leaves. Are there natural groups — healthy green, chlorotic (yellowish, e.g. nitrogen deficiency), necrotic (brown)? k-means finds \(k\) groups by placing \(k\) centres so that every point is as close as possible to its nearest centre.

k-means objective and Lloyd's algorithm(10.2.3)
\[ J = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl\lVert \mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_{c(i)}\bigr\rVert^{2}, \qquad c(i) = \arg\min_k \lVert\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert, \qquad \boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i:\,c(i)=k}\mathbf x_i \]

the within-cluster sum of squares \(J\) (“inertia”) adds the squared distance of every point to the centre of its cluster. Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps: assign each point to its nearest centre; move each centre to the mean of its points. Each step can only decrease \(J\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathbf x_i\)feature vector of point \(i\) (e.g., \(a^*, b^*\))feature units
\(\boldsymbol\mu_k\)centre (centroid) of cluster \(k\)feature units
\(c(i)\)cluster assigned to point \(i\)–
\(J\)inertia (within-cluster sum of squares)feature units squared
Why the algorithm converges
  1. Assignment step: for fixed centres, giving each point its nearest centre minimises each term of \(J\) separately.
  2. Update step: for fixed assignments, the point that minimises \(\sum\lVert\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu\rVert^2\) is the mean (set the gradient \(-2\sum(\mathbf x_i-\boldsymbol\mu)\) to zero — least squares once more).
  3. \(J\) never increases and there are finitely many assignments, so the algorithm stops — but possibly in a local minimum. Run it from several random starts and keep the lowest \(J\) (Lloyd, 1982).
  4. \(J\) always decreases as \(k\) grows (with \(k=n\), \(J=0\)); choose \(k\) where the decrease flattens (the “elbow”) or with domain knowledge.
Assumptions and validity

k-means assumes roughly spherical clusters of similar size and uses Euclidean distance, so features must be on comparable scales — standardise them first (temperature in °C and CO₂ in ppm would otherwise let CO₂ dominate). It is sensitive to outliers.

Squared distances in feature units; if features have different units they must be standardised to be dimensionless before adding ✓.

Principal component analysis (PCA) addresses a different problem: too many correlated features. A hyperspectral camera records reflectance in 200 or more bands; neighbouring bands are almost perfectly correlated. PCA rotates the coordinate system so that the first new axis (principal component, PC1) points in the direction of greatest variance, PC2 in the direction of greatest remaining variance at right angles to PC1, and so on (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016). Often two or three components capture most of the information, which can then be plotted, clustered or fed to a regression.

Principal components(10.2.4)
\[ \mathbf S\,\mathbf v_k = \lambda_k\,\mathbf v_k, \qquad z_{ik} = \mathbf v_k^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf x_i - \bar{\mathbf x}), \qquad \text{fraction explained by PC}_k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_j\lambda_j} \]

the principal directions are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the (standardised) data; each eigenvalue is the variance of the data along its direction. The score of a sample on a component is the projection of its centred feature vector onto that direction.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathbf S\)covariance (or, for standardised data, correlation) matrix of the features–
\(\mathbf v_k\)\(k\)-th principal direction (unit-length eigenvector, “loadings”)–
\(\lambda_k\)variance along direction \(k\)–
\(z_{ik}\)score of sample \(i\) on component \(k\)–
Follow the derivation
  1. The variance of the projections onto a unit vector \(\mathbf v\) is \(\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf S\mathbf v\). We want to maximise it subject to \(\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v = 1\).
  2. Lagrangian: \(\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf S\mathbf v - \lambda(\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v - 1)\). Setting the gradient to zero: \(2\mathbf S\mathbf v - 2\lambda\mathbf v = 0\), i.e. \(\mathbf S\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\) — an eigenvalue problem.
  3. At the solution the variance is \(\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf S\mathbf v = \lambda\): pick the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue as PC1. Later components are the remaining eigenvectors, automatically orthogonal because \(\mathbf S\) is symmetric.
  4. Two standardised variables with correlation \(\rho\) have correlation matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\) with eigenvalues \(1\pm\rho\): PC1 explains \((1+|\rho|)/2\) of the variance.
For standardised features every variable has unit variance and the eigenvalues are dimensionless; the fractions sum to 1 ✓.

Neural networks: neurons, layers and the forward pass

An artificial neuron is a very small model: it multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias and passes the sum through a non-linear activation function. Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) used a step function and could separate two classes with a straight line. Stacking neurons in layers, each feeding the next, gives a multilayer network that can approximate practically any smooth function — provided it has enough neurons and data.

Artificial neuron and activation functions(10.2.5)
\[ a = \varphi\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j x_j + b\Bigr) = \varphi(z), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}, \quad \tanh(z) = \frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}}, \quad \text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0,z) \]

a neuron's output is an activation function applied to the weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias. The sigmoid squashes any number into the range 0 to 1 (useful for probabilities), tanh into −1 to 1, and the rectified linear unit passes positive sums unchanged and sets negative sums to zero.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x_j\)input \(j\) (a standardised feature or the output of a previous neuron)– (standardised)
\(w_j\)weight of input \(j\)–
\(b\)bias (shifts the threshold)–
\(z\)pre-activation (weighted sum)–
\(\varphi\)activation function–
Why a non-linear activation is essential
  1. Without \(\varphi\), a layer computes \(\mathbf W\mathbf x+\mathbf b\), a linear function; two layers compute \(\mathbf W_2(\mathbf W_1\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1)+\mathbf b_2 = (\mathbf W_2\mathbf W_1)\mathbf x + \dots\) — still linear. Depth would add nothing.
  2. With a non-linear \(\varphi\), each hidden neuron contributes a “bent” piece (a smoothed step for sigmoid/tanh, a hinge for ReLU); a weighted sum of many such pieces can approximate any continuous curve — the universal approximation property.
  3. Derivatives, needed for learning: \(\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\) (at most 0.25), \(\tanh'(z) = 1-\tanh^2 z\), \(\text{ReLU}'(z) = 1\) for \(z>0\) and 0 otherwise. The small maximum slope of the sigmoid makes gradients shrink layer after layer (“vanishing gradients”), one reason ReLU dominates deep networks (Goodfellow et al., 2016).
Inputs are standardised (dimensionless), so weights and biases are dimensionless and so is \(z\); the activation of a dimensionless number is dimensionless ✓.

In matrix form the forward pass of a network with one hidden layer is \(\mathbf h = \varphi(\mathbf W_1\mathbf x + \mathbf b_1)\), \(\hat y = \mathbf w_2^{\mathsf T}\mathbf h + b_2\). A network with 3 inputs, 4 hidden neurons and 1 output has \(3\times4+4+4+1 = 21\) parameters; image networks (Lesson 10.3) have millions. Training means finding the parameter values that minimise a loss function on the training data.

Loss functions for regression and classification(10.2.6)
\[ L_{\text{MSE}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2, \qquad L_{\text{CE}} = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[y_i\ln\hat p_i + (1-y_i)\ln(1-\hat p_i)\bigr] \]

for regression the loss is the mean squared error — the SSE of Lesson 10.1 divided by \(n\). For binary classification, where the network outputs a probability \(\hat p_i\) that example \(i\) is positive, the loss is the binary cross-entropy: minus the average log-probability that the model assigned to the correct answer.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(y_i\)true value (regression) or class 0/1 (classification)unit of \(y\) / –
\(\hat p_i\)predicted probability of class 1 (sigmoid output)–
\(L\)loss averaged over the training examplesunit² / nat
Why cross-entropy for probabilities?

Cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of Bernoulli (yes/no) observations, just as MSE is the negative log-likelihood of Gaussian noise (Lesson 10.1, Eq. 10.1.1). It punishes confident mistakes severely: predicting \(\hat p=0.01\) for a diseased plant costs \(-\ln 0.01 = 4.6\), while \(\hat p=0.4\) costs 0.92. Combined with a sigmoid output, its gradient with respect to the pre-activation is simply \(\hat p-y\) — as simple as for MSE.

MSE: (unit of \(y\))². Cross-entropy: logarithms of dimensionless probabilities → dimensionless (nats) ✓.

Learning: gradient descent, backpropagation and regularisation

A loss function of thousands of parameters cannot be minimised with normal equations. Instead, networks are trained by gradient descent: compute the slope of the loss with respect to every parameter, and take a small step downhill. Repeat many times.

Gradient descent (with momentum)(10.2.7)
\[ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}L(\boldsymbol\theta_t), \qquad \text{momentum: } \mathbf v_{t+1} = \mu\mathbf v_t - \eta\nabla L,\ \ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t+\mathbf v_{t+1} \]

the new parameters equal the old ones minus the learning rate times the gradient of the loss. With momentum, the step accumulates a fraction \(\mu\) of the previous step, like a ball rolling downhill, which speeds progress along long shallow valleys.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\boldsymbol\theta\)all parameters (weights and biases)–
\(\eta\)learning rate (step size)(unit of θ)²/(unit of L)
\(\nabla L\)gradient: vector of partial derivatives \(\partial L/\partial\theta_j\)(unit of L)/(unit of θ)
\(\mu\)momentum coefficient, typically 0.9–
How large may the learning rate be? A one-parameter analysis
  1. Take a quadratic loss \(L = a(\theta-\theta^*)^2\) with curvature \(2a\). Its gradient is \(2a(\theta-\theta^*)\).
  2. One step: \(\theta_{t+1}-\theta^* = (\theta_t-\theta^*)(1-2a\eta)\). The error is multiplied by \(1-2a\eta\) each iteration.
  3. Convergence requires \(|1-2a\eta|<1\), i.e. \(0<\eta<1/a\). The fastest convergence is at \(\eta = 1/(2a)\) (one step!). For \(\eta>1/a\) the iterates oscillate with growing amplitude — divergence.
  4. With many parameters the loss has a different curvature in every direction. The learning rate must suit the steepest direction, so progress along flat directions is slow. The ratio of the largest to the smallest curvature (condition number) determines how slowly plain gradient descent converges — which is why inputs are standardised and why adaptive methods such as Adam scale each parameter's step individually.
Stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent

Computing the exact gradient over millions of images is expensive. Stochastic gradient descent estimates it from a random mini-batch of, say, 32 examples. The noisy steps are cheap and, surprisingly, the noise helps to escape poor local minima. One pass through the whole training set is an epoch.

\(\eta\,\partial L/\partial\theta\) must have the unit of \(\theta\): [η] = [θ]²/[L]. For standardised networks everything is dimensionless ✓.

For a network, the gradient with respect to every weight is computed by backpropagation (Rumelhart et al., 1986): the chain rule of calculus applied systematically from the output backwards, reusing intermediate results so that the cost of the whole gradient is only about twice the cost of one forward pass.

Backpropagation for a one-hidden-layer network(10.2.8)
\[ \delta_{\text{out}} = \hat y - y, \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{2,j}} = \delta_{\text{out}}\,h_j, \quad \delta_j = \delta_{\text{out}}\,w_{2,j}\,\varphi'(z_j), \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{1,jk}} = \delta_j\,x_k, \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_{1,j}} = \delta_j \]

for the loss \(L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\), the error signal at the output is the prediction error. Each output weight's gradient is that error times the activity of the hidden neuron it connects. The error is then sent backwards: each hidden neuron receives the output error times its outgoing weight times the slope of its activation function; its incoming weights' gradients are that hidden error times the inputs.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(z_j,\ h_j\)pre-activation and activation of hidden neuron \(j\): \(h_j=\varphi(z_j)\), \(z_j=\sum_k w_{1,jk}x_k+b_{1,j}\)–
\(w_{2,j}\)weight from hidden neuron \(j\) to the output–
\(\delta\)“error signal”: derivative of the loss with respect to a pre-activation–
Follow the derivation with the chain rule
  1. Output: \(\hat y = \sum_j w_{2,j}h_j + b_2\) and \(L = \tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\). So \(\partial L/\partial\hat y = \hat y - y \equiv \delta_{\text{out}}\).
  2. Output weights: \(\partial L/\partial w_{2,j} = (\partial L/\partial\hat y)(\partial\hat y/\partial w_{2,j}) = \delta_{\text{out}}h_j\); and \(\partial L/\partial b_2 = \delta_{\text{out}}\).
  3. Hidden activation: \(\partial L/\partial h_j = \delta_{\text{out}}w_{2,j}\). Through the activation: \(\partial L/\partial z_j = \delta_{\text{out}}w_{2,j}\varphi'(z_j) \equiv \delta_j\).
  4. Input weights: \(z_j\) depends on \(w_{1,jk}\) with slope \(x_k\), so \(\partial L/\partial w_{1,jk} = \delta_j x_k\), and \(\partial L/\partial b_{1,j} = \delta_j\).
  5. Deeper networks repeat step 3 layer by layer: each layer's \(\delta\) is the next layer's \(\delta\) multiplied by the connecting weights and the local activation slope. Products of many slopes below 1 shrink — the vanishing-gradient problem.
With standardised variables all quantities are dimensionless; in general \([\partial L/\partial w] = [L]/[w]\), which the chain rule preserves at every step ✓.
Worked example 2One step of backpropagation by hand

A tiny network has one input, one sigmoid hidden neuron and a linear output: \(h = \sigma(w_1x + b_1)\), \(\hat y = w_2h + b_2\). Parameters: \(w_1 = 0.5\), \(b_1 = 0\), \(w_2 = 1.2\), \(b_2 = 0.1\). A training example has standardised DLI \(x = 1.0\) and standardised yield \(y = 0.6\). Using \(L = \tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2\) and learning rate \(\eta = 0.5\), perform one forward pass, compute all four gradients and update the parameters. Does the loss fall?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Forward. \(z = 0.5\times1.0 + 0 = 0.5\); \(h = 1/(1+e^{-0.5}) = 0.6225\); \(\hat y = 1.2\times0.6225 + 0.1 = 0.8470\); \(L = \tfrac12(0.2470)^2 = 0.0305\).
  2. Output error. \(\delta_{\text{out}} = 0.8470 - 0.6 = 0.2470\). Gradients: \(\partial L/\partial w_2 = 0.2470\times0.6225 = 0.1537\); \(\partial L/\partial b_2 = 0.2470\).
  3. Back through the hidden neuron. \(\sigma'(z) = h(1-h) = 0.6225\times0.3775 = 0.2350\). \(\delta_1 = 0.2470\times1.2\times0.2350 = 0.0696\). Gradients: \(\partial L/\partial w_1 = 0.0696\times1.0 = 0.0696\); \(\partial L/\partial b_1 = 0.0696\).
  4. Update. \(w_2 = 1.2 - 0.5\times0.1537 = 1.1231\); \(b_2 = 0.1 - 0.5\times0.2470 = -0.0235\); \(w_1 = 0.5 - 0.5\times0.0696 = 0.4652\); \(b_1 = -0.0348\).
  5. Check. New forward pass: \(h = \sigma(0.4652-0.0348) = \sigma(0.4304) = 0.6060\), \(\hat y = 1.1231\times0.6060 - 0.0235 = 0.6571\); \(L = \tfrac12(0.0571)^2 = 0.0016\). The loss fell from 0.0305 to 0.0016 in one step ✓ (a learning rate this large is fine for one example, but would be too aggressive for a real data set).
Gradients: ∂L/∂w₂ = 0.154, ∂L/∂b₂ = 0.247, ∂L/∂w₁ = ∂L/∂b₁ = 0.070; loss 0.031 → 0.0016

A network with many parameters and few data will memorise noise. Regularisation restrains it. The most common form adds a penalty on large weights to the loss.

L2 regularisation (weight decay)(10.2.9)
\[ L_{\text{reg}} = L + \lambda\sum_j w_j^{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad w_j \leftarrow (1-2\eta\lambda)\,w_j - \eta\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} \]

the regularised loss adds lambda times the sum of squared weights. In the gradient step this shrinks every weight by a constant factor before the usual update — hence “weight decay”. Large weights, which make sharp, wiggly functions, become expensive.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\lambda\)regularisation strength (tuned by cross-validation)(unit of L)/(unit of w)²
\(1-2\eta\lambda\)shrinkage factor per step–
Other ways to fight over-fitting
  • Early stopping: monitor the loss on a validation set and stop when it starts to rise (Figure 8 shows this moment).
  • Dropout: randomly switch off a fraction of neurons in each training step so that no neuron can rely on a particular partner.
  • Data augmentation: for images, add rotated, flipped, re-coloured copies (Lesson 10.3).
  • More data and simpler models — often the most effective of all.
\(\lambda w^2\) must have the unit of \(L\); \(2\eta\lambda\) is dimensionless because \([\eta]=[w]^2/[L]\) ✓.

Evaluating models: confusion matrices, precision, recall and ROC

For regression you already know RMSE, MAE and \(R^2\) (Lesson 10.1). For classification, the starting point is the confusion matrix, which counts the four possible outcomes when a model decides “diseased” or “healthy”: true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN) and false negatives (FN). All common metrics are ratios of these counts.

Classification metrics(10.2.10)
\[ \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad \text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN+FP},\quad F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}},\quad \text{precision} = \frac{\text{se}\,\pi}{\text{se}\,\pi+(1-\text{sp})(1-\pi)} \]

precision is the fraction of plants flagged as diseased that really are; recall (sensitivity) is the fraction of diseased plants that were flagged; specificity is the fraction of healthy plants correctly passed; F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The last formula (Bayes' theorem) shows that precision depends on the prevalence \(\pi\) of disease, not only on the classifier.

SymbolMeaningUnit
TP, FP, TN, FNcounts of true/false positives/negatives–
se, spsensitivity (= recall) and specificity–
\(\pi\)prevalence: fraction of positives in the population–
ROC, AUCreceiver operating characteristic curve (recall against 1 − specificity for all thresholds) and the area under it–
Why accuracy misleads, and what the ROC curve shows
  1. Accuracy \((TP+TN)/N\) rewards predicting the majority class. If 2 % of plants are diseased, a model that always says “healthy” is 98 % accurate — and useless.
  2. Most classifiers output a score (e.g., a probability) and call a plant positive above a threshold. Lowering the threshold raises recall and lowers specificity. The ROC curve plots recall against 1 − specificity for every threshold; a random guess gives the diagonal, a perfect classifier the top-left corner (Fawcett, 2006).
  3. AUC equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative. It is threshold-free and prevalence-free — which is both its strength and its weakness: the grower still has to choose a threshold for the actual prevalence and the actual costs of each error.
  4. Derivation of the Bayes form: \(TP = \text{se}\,\pi N\) and \(FP = (1-\text{sp})(1-\pi)N\); substitute into precision = TP/(TP + FP) and cancel \(N\).
All metrics are ratios of counts → dimensionless, between 0 and 1 (often quoted in %) ✓.
Worked example 3A 95 % accurate disease detector that is mostly wrong

A camera system in a lettuce greenhouse flags plants with early downy-mildew symptoms. On a validation set it has recall 0.95 and specificity 0.95. In the greenhouse, 2 % of plants are infected. For 10 000 plants, construct the confusion matrix and calculate accuracy, precision and F1. What should the grower do with a flagged plant?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Split the population. 2 % of 10 000 = 200 infected, 9 800 healthy.
  2. Infected plants. TP = 0.95 × 200 = 190; FN = 10.
  3. Healthy plants. TN = 0.95 × 9 800 = 9 310; FP = 490.
  4. Metrics. Accuracy = (190 + 9 310)/10 000 = 0.95. Precision = 190/(190 + 490) = 0.279. F1 = 2 × 0.279 × 0.95/(0.279 + 0.95) = 0.432.
  5. Interpretation. Of 680 flagged plants only 190 (28 %) are really infected. The system is excellent as a screening tool — it finds 95 % of infections and reduces the plants to inspect from 10 000 to 680 — but a flagged plant should be confirmed by a person before being discarded.
Accuracy 0.95, precision 0.28, recall 0.95, F1 0.43 — screen, then confirm

Data leakage and spatial and temporal cross-validation

The most common reason a published agricultural model fails in practice is not the algorithm but the validation. Data leakage occurs when information about the test data sneaks into training, making the estimated performance too optimistic (Kaufman et al., 2012). Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) found leakage in 294 papers across 17 scientific fields and classified it into eight types — from obvious errors (tuning on the test set) to subtle ones (the test set is not independent of the training set). Typical forms in food production:

  • Temporal leakage: random splitting of a time series. Yesterday's and tomorrow's measurements are in the training set, so predicting today is easy interpolation — unlike forecasting next week.
  • Spatial leakage: neighbouring field plots, benches or pixels are similar (spatial autocorrelation). A random split puts near-duplicates in training and test. Ploton et al. (2020) showed that a random-forest biomass map that appeared to explain more than half of the variation under random cross-validation had almost no predictive power when validated with spatially separate data.
  • Group leakage: several images of the same plant, leaf or greenhouse compartment appear in both training and test sets. The model learns to recognise the plant (or the background, or the camera), not the disease.
  • Preprocessing leakage: standardising, imputing or selecting features using the whole data set before splitting.
Checklist before you trust a reported accuracy

Ask: (1) Was the test set collected independently — different plants, dates, greenhouses or fields from the training set? (2) Were all preprocessing and tuning steps done inside the training folds? (3) Is the metric appropriate for the class balance and the decision (precision/recall rather than accuracy)? (4) Is there a simple baseline (yesterday's value, a linear model) to compare with? Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) showed that, once leakage was corrected, complex models in a well-known prediction task were no better than decades-old logistic regression.

Applications in food production — and the age of foundation models

Machine learning in agriculture has grown from a niche to a large literature. Liakos et al. (2018) organised applications into crop management (yield prediction, disease and weed detection, crop quality, species recognition), livestock management, water management and soil management. Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Boldú (2018) surveyed 40 deep-learning studies and found that deep learning generally outperformed the image-processing techniques used before. For yield prediction, a systematic review of 50 machine-learning studies found temperature, rainfall and soil type to be the most used features and artificial neural networks the most applied algorithm; among 30 deep-learning studies, convolutional neural networks were the most common (van Klompenburg et al., 2020).

Foundation models and large language models (2024–2026)

A foundation model is a very large network pre-trained on broad data (text, images or both) that can be adapted to many tasks with little task-specific data. Large language models (LLMs) such as the GPT family are the best-known examples; vision foundation models such as Segment Anything (Kirillov et al., 2023) segment objects — including leaves, fruits and weeds — without task-specific training. Li et al. (2024) review the opportunities in smart agriculture: fewer labelled examples, multimodal inputs (images, text, sensor data) and natural-language interfaces for farmers.

The evidence so far is mixed and calls for critical reading:

  • Knowledge tests. Silva et al. (2023) found that GPT-4 answered 93 % of questions correctly on exams used to renew agronomist certifications, outperforming earlier models. Passing an exam, however, is not the same as giving correct, local, timely advice for a specific field.
  • Extension services. Tzachor et al. (2023) examined GPT for agricultural extension, tested it on technical advice for Nigerian cassava farmers, documented shortcomings and proposed a design process with human experts in the loop. Digital Green's Farmer.Chat, a generative-AI advisory chatbot, reported engaging more than 15 000 farmers and answering over 300 000 queries in four countries (Singh et al., 2024) — a self-reported deployment study rather than an independent impact evaluation.
  • Known failure modes. LLMs can produce fluent but wrong answers (“hallucinations”), are weaker in local languages and for local crop varieties that are rare in their training text, may give outdated pesticide advice, and blur responsibility when advice causes harm. Their energy use and the concentration of models in a few companies raise the governance questions discussed in Lesson 10.5.
Frontier

Research directions in 2025–2026 include retrieval-augmented LLM advisors that quote verified extension documents, multimodal models that combine a photo of a leaf with a farmer's question, and vision foundation models fine-tuned for plant phenotyping. The evaluation principles of this lesson apply unchanged: independent test data, appropriate metrics, comparison with simple baselines and with human experts, and monitoring after deployment.

Common misconception

“Deep learning always beats simpler methods.” For images, sound and text — data with spatial or sequential structure and millions of examples — deep networks dominate (LeCun et al., 2015). For tabular data such as climate records and management logs, gradient-boosted trees are frequently as good or better, need less data and tuning, and are easier to explain. Always compare with a simple baseline.

Key terms

Feature
An input variable used by a model (e.g., DLI, temperature, a pixel value).
Label
The known answer for a training example (e.g., yield, disease class).
Supervised learning
Learning a mapping from features to labels from labelled examples (regression or classification).
Unsupervised learning
Finding structure (clusters, components, anomalies) in data without labels.
Reinforcement learning
Learning a policy for sequential decisions from rewards received by an acting agent.
Gini impurity
\(1-\sum p_c^2\): probability that two random examples from a node belong to different classes.
Random forest
An ensemble of decision trees trained on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets, averaged to reduce variance.
Gradient boosting
An ensemble that adds shallow trees sequentially, each fitted to the residuals of the current model.
k-means
Clustering that minimises within-cluster squared distances by alternating assignment and centroid updates.
Principal component analysis
Rotation of standardised data onto orthogonal directions of maximum variance (eigenvectors of the covariance matrix).
Activation function
The non-linear function (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU) applied to a neuron's weighted input sum.
Backpropagation
Efficient computation of loss gradients in a network by applying the chain rule from the output layer backwards.
Learning rate
Step size of gradient descent; too small is slow, too large diverges.
Precision / recall
Fraction of predicted positives that are correct / fraction of actual positives that are found.
Data leakage
Information from the test data influencing training, producing over-optimistic performance estimates.
