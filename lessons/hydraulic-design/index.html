Hydraulic design and water management

Every hydroponic system is also a small waterworks. A pump lifts solution against gravity and friction; pipes and valves share it among channels, benches or drippers; a thin film or a trickle carries water, nutrients and oxygen past the roots; and a reservoir buffers everything the crop — and the operator — do wrong. This lesson turns that plumbing into engineering. You will derive how deep and how fast a nutrient film flows, follow oxygen and nutrients along a channel, size a pump from its curve and the pipe friction, check that every plant gets its fair share, and close the books on water with a salt balance and a water account: the calculations you need to build, commission and defend the hydraulics of your own system.

What you will be able to do

  • Calculate the flow a channel, bench or plant needs from a steady-state mass balance and compare it with practical design ranges (LO2, LO3).
  • Derive the laminar falling-film (Nusselt) equation and use it to predict film depth, velocity, Reynolds number and residence time in an NFT channel from its slope and flow (LO3).
  • Predict oxygen and nutrient depletion along a channel with a steady-state mass balance, and choose channel length and flow accordingly (LO2, LO3).
  • Size pipes and pumps: compute friction losses with Darcy–Weisbach (Swamee–Jain) and Hazen–Williams, minor losses, the total dynamic head, the operating point and the pump's power and energy use (LO3).
  • Evaluate emitter and channel uniformity, size a reservoir, and apply the salt balance, leaching fraction and a closed-loop water account to calculate water use per kilogram (LO3, LO7).
  • Plan and commission the hydraulics of your project system and document them with measurements (LO4).

A small waterworks: how much flow does a crop need?

Water in a soilless system does several jobs at once. It replaces what the crop transpires; it carries dissolved nutrients to the root surface; in nutrient film technique (NFT) and deep-water culture it carries dissolved oxygen; it carries heat — and, unfortunately, pathogens (Lesson 5.3). A hydraulic design must deliver enough flow to every plant, lift the solution with a pump against gravity and friction, share it evenly, and keep enough volume in reserve that a failure is survivable (Lesson 5.1). All of this can be calculated before a single pipe is cut, and every result can be checked afterwards with a measuring jug, a stopwatch and a spirit level.

Figure 1 shows the hydraulic layout of an NFT system of the kind many project groups build. Follow the water round the loop: the pump lifts it from the reservoir to the top of the channels; a manifold and valves share it between the channels; it runs down each channel as a thin film past the roots; and gravity returns it to the reservoir. Each part of the loop is one section of this lesson.

How much flow is "enough"? An engineer answers with a mass balance. Take a whole channel at steady state: whatever the plants remove from the passing solution per hour must be carried in by the flow, and the solution leaving the last plant must still be good enough. Whichever substance runs short first — oxygen, a nutrient, or water itself — sets the minimum flow.

Minimum flow from a steady-state mass balance(5.4.1)
\[ Q_{\min} = \frac{N_p\,r}{C_{\text{in}} - C_{\min}}, \qquad q_{\text{plant}} = \frac{Q}{N_p} \]

the smallest flow a channel can run at equals the number of plants it feeds times the rate at which each plant removes the limiting substance, divided by how far the concentration may fall between the inlet and the outlet. The flow per plant is the channel flow divided by the number of plants.

Follow the derivation
  1. Control volume: the solution in one channel, from inlet to outlet, at steady state (nothing accumulates).
  2. Mass balance for the limiting substance: in = out + uptake, \(Q\,C_{\text{in}} = Q\,C_{\text{out}} + N_p\,r\), where \(Q\) is the channel flow (L h⁻¹), \(C\) in mg L⁻¹ and \(r\) in mg h⁻¹.
  3. Solve for the outlet: \(C_{\text{out}} = C_{\text{in}} - N_p r/Q\). The drop along the channel is inversely proportional to the flow.
  4. Impose the design limit \(C_{\text{out}}\ge C_{\min}\) and solve for \(Q\): Eq. 5.4.1.
  5. Check each substance and keep the largest \(Q_{\min}\). For oxygen this is conservative, because oxygen also re-dissolves through the film surface on the way (Eq. 5.4.3).
Assumptions and validity

Steady state, uniform plants, and an uptake rate that does not depend on concentration (true for oxygen above a few mg L⁻¹ and for nutrients far above their Michaelis–Menten constants, Lesson 2.3). The equation says nothing about wetting: below some flow, a film no longer covers the channel floor evenly and roots at the edges dry out — which is why practical flows (Table 1) are several times larger than \(Q_{\min}\).

The nutrient film: slope, depth and velocity

An NFT channel is a shallow, sloping open channel in which gravity pulls a thin sheet of solution downhill while the channel floor holds it back by friction. The balance between the two forces sets the depth and the speed of the film. The problem was solved in 1916 by the German engineer Wilhelm Nusselt, who needed the thickness of the film of condensate running down a cold, vertical wall (Nusselt, 1916); it is now the standard first example of viscous flow in chemical-engineering textbooks (Bird et al., 2002). Figure 2 shows the geometry and the resulting velocity profile.

Film thickness of a laminar falling film (Nusselt)(5.4.2)
\[ \begin{gathered} h = \left(\frac{3\,\mu\,q}{\rho\,g\,\sin\theta}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad q = \frac{Q}{W} \\[8pt] \bar{u} = \frac{q}{h} = \frac{\rho\,g\,\sin\theta\;h^{2}}{3\,\mu}, \qquad Re_f = \frac{4\,q}{\nu} \end{gathered} \]

the depth of the film equals the cube root of three times the viscosity times the flow per unit channel width, divided by the density times gravity times the sine of the slope angle. The mean velocity is the flow per width divided by the depth, and the film Reynolds number is four times the flow per width divided by the kinematic viscosity.

Follow the derivation
  1. Set up. Take \(x\) down the slope and \(y\) perpendicular to the channel floor, with \(y = 0\) at the floor and \(y = h\) at the free surface. Assume steady, laminar, fully developed flow of constant depth, in a channel much wider than the film is deep, so that the velocity \(u(y)\) depends only on \(y\).
  2. Forces on a slab. Consider the fluid between height \(y\) and the surface, per unit length and width. Its weight is \(\rho g(h-y)\); the component along the slope is \(\rho g\sin\theta\,(h-y)\). Nothing pushes on the free surface (air exerts negligible shear), and the pressure is the same at both ends of a fully developed film. The only force that can hold the slab back is the shear stress \(\tau(y)\) exerted by the slower fluid beneath it.
  3. Balance (no acceleration): \(\tau(y) = \rho g\sin\theta\,(h-y)\). The shear is largest at the wall and zero at the surface.
  4. Newton's law of viscosity links shear to the velocity gradient: \(\tau = \mu\,\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}y\). Hence \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}y = (\rho g\sin\theta/\mu)(h-y)\).
  5. Integrate with the no-slip condition \(u(0) = 0\): \(u(y) = \dfrac{\rho g\sin\theta}{\mu}\left(hy - \dfrac{y^2}{2}\right)\) — a parabola with its maximum \(u_s = \rho g\sin\theta\,h^2/(2\mu)\) at the surface.
  6. Flow per unit width: \(q = \int_0^h u\,\mathrm{d}y = \dfrac{\rho g\sin\theta}{\mu}\left(\dfrac{h^3}{2} - \dfrac{h^3}{6}\right) = \dfrac{\rho g\sin\theta\,h^3}{3\mu}\).
  7. Invert for the depth: \(h = \big(3\mu q/(\rho g\sin\theta)\big)^{1/3}\). The mean velocity follows as \(\bar{u} = q/h = \rho g\sin\theta\,h^2/(3\mu) = \tfrac{2}{3}u_s\).
  8. Small slopes. For a slope of 1:\(n\), \(\tan\theta = 1/n\) and \(\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt{1+n^2}\approx1/n\) — for 1:50 the approximation errs by 0.02 %.
  9. Regime. The film Reynolds number \(Re_f = 4q/\nu\) (four times the flow per width over the kinematic viscosity) decides the regime: smooth laminar below about 20, laminar with surface ripples up to about 1500, turbulent above (Bird et al., 2002). NFT films are typically rippled-laminar; Eq. 5.4.2 remains a good estimate of the mean depth.
Assumptions and validity

The equation describes an empty or young channel with a flat floor. A mature root mat is a porous dam: the solution flows through and under it, deeper and slower than Eq. 5.4.2 predicts, and ponds behind any low spot in the channel. Many commercial channels also have ribbed floors that spread the film. Surface tension can make a very thin film break up into rivulets that leave parts of the floor dry — another reason not to run channels at very low flows. Viscosity falls by about 2 % per °C, so warm solution runs slightly thinner and faster.

\(\left(\dfrac{\text{Pa s}\cdot\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-3}\cdot\text{m s}^{-2}}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}\cdot\text{s}\cdot\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-2}}\right)^{1/3} = (\text{m}^3)^{1/3} = \text{m}\) ✓; \(Re_f\): (m² s⁻¹)/(m² s⁻¹), dimensionless ✓

Three consequences are worth remembering. First, the depth depends on the cube root of the flow: doubling the flow deepens the film by only 26 % (\(2^{1/3}\)) while the mean velocity rises by 59 % (\(2^{2/3}\)). Second, the depth depends on the cube root of the inverse slope: going from 1:100 to 1:30 thins the film by a third. Third, every NFT film is a matter of millimetres — which is exactly why the roots in its upper layers can breathe (Lesson 5.3) and why a few millimetres of sag in a channel create a pond.

Common misconception

"If the roots look dry at the top of the channel, double the flow and the film will be twice as deep." The depth grows with the cube root of the flow: doubling it adds only a quarter to the depth (1.37 → 1.72 mm in Worked example 2), while the pump's head and energy use grow. Dry patches are usually caused by an uneven floor, a twisted channel or a root mat that diverts the flow — fix the geometry first, then check the flow.

History

Wilhelm Nusselt (1882–1957) published the falling-film solution in 1916 to predict how fast steam condenses on a cold wall: the condensate film is the main resistance to heat transfer, and its thickness follows exactly Eq. 5.4.2 (Nusselt, 1916). The same result now sizes falling-film evaporators in sugar and dairy factories — and NFT channels. Allen Cooper, who developed NFT in the 1960s–70s, relied on the practical insight behind it: keep the film shallow enough for the roots to stay in air (Cooper, 1979).

Residence time and depletion along the channel

As the film flows past plant after plant, the roots remove oxygen and nutrients and the leaves remove water, so the last plant in a long channel sees a different solution from the first — the physical reason behind the channel-length limit of Table 1. A mass balance on a thin slice of the channel (Figure 4) turns this into an equation, and also shows how oxygen re-dissolving through the film surface fights the depletion.

Residence time and oxygen profile along an NFT channel(5.4.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} t_{\text{res}} = \frac{L}{\bar{u}} = \frac{L\,W\,h}{Q} \\[6pt] C(x) = C_{\text{eq}} + \big(C_{\text{in}} - C_{\text{eq}}\big)\,e^{-x/\lambda} \\[6pt] \lambda = \frac{Q}{K_L\,W}, \qquad C_{\text{eq}} = C^{*} - \frac{r'}{K_L\,W} \end{gathered} \]

the residence time is the channel length divided by the mean velocity, which equals the volume of the film divided by the flow. Along the channel, the dissolved-oxygen concentration relaxes exponentially from its inlet value towards an equilibrium value, over a characteristic length equal to the flow divided by the surface-transfer coefficient times the channel width. The equilibrium lies below saturation by the root uptake per metre divided by the same product.

Follow the derivation
  1. Residence time. A parcel of solution moves at the mean velocity \(\bar{u}\), so it needs \(L/\bar{u}\) to pass. Since \(\bar{u} = Q/(Wh)\) (Eq. 5.4.2), \(t_{\text{res}} = LWh/Q\): film volume over flow.
  2. Slice balance (Figure 4), steady state, per unit time: \(Q\,C(x) - Q\,C(x+\mathrm{d}x) + K_LW\,\mathrm{d}x\,(C^{*}-C) - r'\,\mathrm{d}x = 0\).
  3. Divide by dx and let it shrink: \(Q\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}x = K_LW\,(C^{*}-C) - r'\).
  4. Equilibrium. Far down a long channel the concentration stops changing when surface transfer equals uptake: \(K_LW(C^{*}-C_{\text{eq}}) = r'\), i.e. \(C_{\text{eq}} = C^{*} - r'/(K_LW)\).
  5. Solve. With \(y = C - C_{\text{eq}}\): \(\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}x = -y/\lambda\), \(\lambda = Q/(K_LW)\), hence the exponential profile — the spatial twin of the time behaviour in Lesson 5.3 (Eq. 5.3.5).
  6. No surface transfer (\(K_L\to0\)): expanding the exponential gives \(C(L) = C_{\text{in}} - r'L/Q\) — the whole-channel balance of Eq. 5.4.1. The same linear form applies to nutrients, which have no surface source.
  7. The size of \(K_L\). Penetration theory (Higbie, 1935) gives \(K_L\approx2\sqrt{D\,u_s/(\pi\ell)}\), where \(\ell\) is the distance over which the surface is renewed. With \(D = 2\times10^{-9}\) m² s⁻¹ and \(u_s\approx0.18\) m s⁻¹, a surface renewed every metre gives \(2\times10^{-5}\) m s⁻¹ and ripples every 5 cm about \(10^{-4}\) m s⁻¹ — hence the order-of-magnitude range in the table.
Assumptions and validity

Plug flow (no mixing along the channel), uniform plants and a constant uptake rate. The surface-transfer coefficient is the least certain number: ripples, falling water at inlets and gaps between plants increase it; a dense root mat that covers the surface decreases it. The oxygen supply through the humid air above the root mat — the main oxygen route in mature NFT — is not part of this model, so it is pessimistic for well-aerated root mats and optimistic for waterlogged ones. Treat it as a design tool for comparing flows and lengths, not as a precise prediction.

Moving water through pipes: head, friction and fittings

To push the flow of Eq. 5.4.1 up to the channel inlets, the pump must give the water energy. Hydraulic engineers express energy per unit weight of water as a height — the head, in metres — because a head is easy to picture: lifting 1 kg of water by 1 m needs 9.81 J, and a pump that delivers "1.5 m of head" could lift water 1.5 m up an open tube. Part of the head the pump supplies is stored as height (the static lift); the rest is lost to friction along the pipe walls and to turbulence in fittings, and ends up as a small amount of heat in the water.

Energy equation: total dynamic head and minor losses(5.4.4)
\[ H = \Delta z + \frac{p_2 - p_1}{\rho g} + \frac{v_2^{2} - v_1^{2}}{2g} + h_f + h_m, \qquad h_m = \Big(\sum_i K_i\Big)\,\frac{v^{2}}{2g} \]

the head the pump must supply equals the rise in elevation, plus the rise in pressure head, plus the rise in velocity head, plus the friction loss along the pipes and the minor losses in the fittings. Each fitting loses a multiple K of the velocity head.

Follow the derivation
  1. Energy per unit weight. A kilogram of water at height \(z\), pressure \(p\) and speed \(v\) carries potential energy \(gz\), "flow work" \(p/\rho\) and kinetic energy \(v^2/2\) (J kg⁻¹). Dividing by \(g\) gives heads in metres: \(z + p/(\rho g) + v^2/(2g)\) — Bernoulli's sum.
  2. Losses. For a real, viscous flow between points 1 and 2 the sum falls by the losses: \((z + p/\rho g + v^2/2g)_1 + H = (z + p/\rho g + v^2/2g)_2 + h_f + h_m\), where \(H\) is the head added by a pump in between.
  3. Rearrange for \(H\): the first form of Eq. 5.4.4.
  4. Reservoir to open channel. Point 1 on the reservoir surface (\(p_1\) atmospheric, \(v_1\approx0\)), point 2 in the jet leaving the pipe (\(p_2\) atmospheric, \(v_2 = v\)): \(H = \Delta z + v^2/2g + h_f + h_m\). The kinetic energy of the jet is lost in the channel, so it is conveniently counted as an "exit loss" with \(K = 1\) inside \(\sum K\).
  5. Minor losses. Bends, tees, valves, entrances and exits create eddies whose dissipation scales with the kinetic energy of the flow, hence \(h_m = K\,v^2/2g\) with an experimentally determined \(K\) for each fitting (Çengel & Cimbala, 2018).

Pumps: curves, operating point and power

Small hydroponic systems use centrifugal pumps: a spinning impeller flings water outwards and raises its pressure. The head such a pump produces is highest when no water flows (the shut-off head) and falls as the flow increases, until at zero head it delivers its maximum flow. This relation is the pump curve, printed on every datasheet. The pipework has its own curve: the system curve \(H_{\text{sys}}(Q)\) of Eq. 5.4.4 — the static lift plus losses that grow roughly with \(Q^2\). The pump cannot choose its flow: it settles at the operating point, where the head it can produce equals the head the system demands.

Pump power and the operating point(5.4.7)
\[ \begin{gathered} P_h = \rho\,g\,Q\,H, \qquad P_{\text{el}} = \frac{P_h}{\eta} \\[6pt] H_{\text{pump}}(Q) \approx H_0\left[1 - \left(\frac{Q}{Q_{\max}}\right)^{2}\right], \qquad H_{\text{sys}}(Q) \approx \Delta z + k_s\,Q^{2} \\[6pt] H_{\text{pump}}(Q_{\text{op}}) = H_{\text{sys}}(Q_{\text{op}}) \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; Q_{\text{op}} = \sqrt{\frac{H_0 - \Delta z}{H_0/Q_{\max}^{2} + k_s}} \end{gathered} \]

the hydraulic power given to the water is density times gravity times flow times head, and the electrical power is that divided by the pump's overall (wire-to-water) efficiency. If the pump curve falls parabolically from its shut-off head and the system curve rises parabolically from the static lift, the flow at which they meet follows from equating the two.

  1. Power = force × velocity. A pump raising the pressure of the flow by \(\Delta p\) pushes a volume \(Q\) per second through that pressure difference: \(P = \Delta p\,Q\). With \(\Delta p = \rho gH\): \(P_h = \rho gQH\).
  2. Efficiency. Motor and impeller losses mean the electricity drawn is larger: \(P_{\text{el}} = P_h/\eta\). All of \(P_{\text{el}}\) ends up as heat — in the motor, in the water by friction, and in the channels — which is why a submersible pump warms the reservoir (Lesson 5.3, Eq. 5.3.8).
  3. Pump curve. The ideal (Euler) head of a centrifugal pump falls linearly with flow; internal losses bend it downward. A parabola through the shut-off head \(H_0\) and the maximum flow \(Q_{\max}\) is a common approximation of a datasheet curve.
  4. System curve. From Eq. 5.4.4 with \(v = Q/A\): \(H_{\text{sys}} = \Delta z + (fL/D+\sum K)\,Q^2/(2gA^2) = \Delta z + k_sQ^2\) (treating \(f\) as constant).
  5. Operating point. Equate: \(H_0 - (H_0/Q_{\max}^2)Q^2 = \Delta z + k_sQ^2\) and solve for \(Q\). If \(\Delta z\ge H_0\) there is no flow at all.
  6. Energy. Energy per period = \(P_{\text{el}}\times t\): a 20 W pump running continuously uses 0.48 kWh per day, 14 kWh per month.

Note · the affinity laws

Variable-speed pumps change the whole pump curve instead of throttling against a valve. For a given pump, flow scales with rotational speed \(N\), head with \(N^2\) and power with \(N^3\) (the affinity laws; Çengel & Cimbala, 2018). In a system dominated by friction, running at 80 % speed delivers 80 % of the flow for about half the power. In a small hydroponic system dominated by static lift, however, the head cannot fall much before the flow stops — one more reason why the static lift, not friction, is the key number of small systems.

Uniform distribution: manifolds and drip emitters

A system with a perfect pump can still fail its plants if the water is shared unevenly. In an NFT system, three channels fed from one supply line receive equal flows only if the path to each offers the same resistance. The practical rule is to make the resistance of each branch — its valve, inlet tube and outlet — large compared with the head differences along the manifold: then small differences in manifold pressure hardly matter. Fit a valve to every channel and measure each channel's flow with a jug and a stopwatch until they agree within about 10 %.

In drip irrigation the same problem appears at every plant. Each emitter delivers a flow that depends on the pressure at its position, and pressure falls along a lateral because of friction and changes with elevation (Figure 7). How strongly the flow reacts depends on the emitter design, summarised by its emitter exponent (Keller & Karmeli, 1974).

Emitter discharge and irrigation uniformity(5.4.8)
\[ \begin{gathered} q = k\,H^{x}, \qquad \frac{\Delta q}{q} \approx x\,\frac{\Delta H}{H} \\[8pt] \text{CU} = 100\left(1 - \frac{\sum_i |q_i - \bar{q}|}{n\,\bar{q}}\right), \qquad \text{DU}_{lq} = 100\,\frac{\bar{q}_{lq}}{\bar{q}} \end{gathered} \]

Reservoirs: sizing and buffering

The reservoir is the system's memory. It must hold (1) the drain-back volume: everything in the channels, pipes and root mats that runs back when the pump stops — if the reservoir is full to the brim, a power cut floods the floor; (2) a minimum volume that keeps the pump submerged and prevents it from sucking air; and (3) a buffer volume that absorbs the crop's water uptake between top-ups without letting concentration, pH and temperature drift too far. A larger buffer also buys time after a failure (Eq. 5.1.8 of Lesson 5.1).

Salt balance, leaching fraction and the closed-loop water account

Water leaves a soilless system in only a few ways: as vapour through the leaves (transpiration, which carries no salt), inside the harvested product, as drain or bleed, as solution thrown away at the end of a crop, and through leaks. Salts, on the other hand, leave only with liquid water. That asymmetry is the root of every salinity problem in irrigation, from desert fields to greenhouse slabs, and it leads to one of the most useful equations in water management (Figure 8).

Salt balance, leaching fraction and the water account(5.4.10)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{LF} = \frac{V_d}{V_i} = \frac{c_i}{c_d} \;\;\big(=\tfrac{\text{EC}_i}{\text{EC}_d}\ \text{for non-absorbed salts}\big), \qquad V_i = \frac{V_{ET}}{1-\text{LF}} \\[6pt] \text{WU} = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{Y}, \qquad V_{\text{in}} = V_{T} + V_{P} + V_{D} + V_{R} + V_{L} \end{gathered} \]

at steady state the leaching (drain) fraction — the drained volume over the applied volume — equals the concentration of a non-absorbed salt in the irrigation water divided by its concentration in the drain; the water applied equals the crop's water use divided by one minus the leaching fraction. Water use per kilogram is the total water input divided by the yield, and the input is the sum of everything that leaves: transpired, contained in the product, drained or bled, discarded and lost.

Follow the derivation
  1. Salt balance of the root zone over a period long enough that its salt content does not change: salt in = salt out. Salt enters only with the irrigation, \(V_ic_i\), and leaves only with the drain, \(V_dc_d\) (transpiration carries none; the crop takes up none of this salt; nothing precipitates).
  2. Therefore \(V_ic_i = V_dc_d\), and \(\text{LF} = V_d/V_i = c_i/c_d\): the drain is concentrated by the factor 1/LF.
  3. Water balance: \(V_i = V_{ET} + V_d = V_{ET} + \text{LF}\,V_i\), so \(V_i = V_{ET}/(1-\text{LF})\).
  4. Design: to keep the root-zone (drain) concentration at or below a limit \(c_{d,\max}\), the leaching fraction must be at least \(c_i/c_{d,\max}\). For field irrigation, where most salts are not absorbed, FAO expresses the same idea with EC and a crop's salinity tolerance: the leaching requirement \(\text{LR} = \text{EC}_w/(5\,\text{EC}_e-\text{EC}_w)\) and the applied water \(\text{ET}/(1-\text{LR})\) (Ayers & Westcot, 1985).
  5. Hydroponic twist. In a nutrient solution most of the EC is taken up, so apply the balance to a conservative ion — Na⁺ or Cl⁻. In a closed system the drain is reused and only a bleed leaves: the bleed fraction plays the role of LF, which is exactly Eq. 5.2.9 (\(c_{ss} = c_w/b\)) of Lesson 5.2.
  6. Water account. Conservation of water over a crop cycle (storage change zero): input = sum of all outputs, and WU = input/yield — the definition of Eq. 5.1.7 in Lesson 5.1, now with every term named so that none is forgotten.
Assumptions and validity

Steady state (constant root-zone salt content), no uptake or precipitation of the tracer salt, perfect mixing. Real crops take up some Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (the true requirement is then a little lower), and gypsum or calcium phosphate can precipitate in slabs, removing salt without drainage. In practice, Dutch and Scandinavian growers monitor drain EC and Na⁺ and adjust the drain or bleed accordingly (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009).

Worked example 8How much must drain — and what better water is worth

A tomato grower on stone wool uses well water and fertilisers that together bring 1.5 mmol L⁻¹ of Na⁺ into the supply solution. Suppose the grower wants to keep Na⁺ in the root zone (measured in the drain) at or below 6 mmol L⁻¹. The crop uses 3.2 L m⁻² d⁻¹. (a) What leaching fraction and supply are needed in an open system? (b) How much water and sodium leave per hectare per day? (c) What changes with rain water containing 0.1 mmol L⁻¹ Na⁺?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Leaching fraction (Eq. 5.4.10): LF ≥ 1.5/6 = 0.25. Supply \(V_i = 3.2/(1-0.25) = 4.27\) L m⁻² d⁻¹, of which 1.07 L m⁻² d⁻¹ drains.
  2. (b) Per hectare (10 000 m²): drain 10.7 m³ d⁻¹, carrying \(10\,700\ \text{L}\times6\ \text{mmol L}^{-1} = 64\) mol Na⁺ = 1.5 kg of sodium — and all the nutrients dissolved in that drain (Lesson 5.1, Worked example 3).
  3. (c) Rain water: LF ≥ 0.1/6 = 0.017 — the drain could shrink to about 2 % of the supply, or the system could be closed with a tiny bleed. This is why greenhouse regions collect rain water from roofs and why reverse osmosis is common where only saline water is available.

Safety

Mains-powered pumps sit in water: use a residual-current device (RCD), give every cable a drip loop below the socket, and never reach into a tank with the pump plugged in. Keep floors dry — drain-back and leaks make them slippery — and secure reservoirs and channels so that a full tank cannot tip.

Key idea

Hydraulic design is a chain of mass and energy balances. Mass: the flow must carry what the plants remove (Eq. 5.4.1), a film has a predictable depth (Eq. 5.4.2), concentrations change predictably along a channel (Eq. 5.4.3), and salts leave only with liquid water (Eq. 5.4.10). Energy: the pump must supply lift plus losses (Eqs. 5.4.4–5.4.6) at an operating point it cannot choose (Eq. 5.4.7). Get these right and a hydroponic system is quiet, cheap and forgiving.

Key terms

Falling (Nusselt) film
Laminar liquid film flowing down an inclined surface; depth \(h = (3\mu q/(\rho g\sin\theta))^{1/3}\).
Film Reynolds number
\(4q/\nu\): smooth laminar below about 20, rippled laminar up to about 1500, turbulent above.
Residence time
Time the solution spends in a channel, length over mean velocity (film volume over flow).
Head
Energy per unit weight of liquid expressed as a height in metres (1 m = 9.81 J kg⁻¹).
Total dynamic head
Head a pump must supply: static lift plus pressure and velocity head changes plus friction and minor losses.
Darcy friction factor
Dimensionless wall shear in pipe flow; 64/Re in laminar flow, from Colebrook or Swamee–Jain in turbulent flow.
Minor loss coefficient (K)
Loss of a fitting in multiples of the velocity head, \(h_m = K v^2/2g\).
Hazen–Williams formula
Empirical pipe-friction formula for water with roughness coefficient C; reliable for larger pipes in turbulent flow.
Pump curve and system curve
Head a pump produces versus flow; head a pipe system requires versus flow.
Operating point
Flow and head at which the pump curve meets the system curve.
Wire-to-water efficiency
Hydraulic power delivered to the water divided by electrical power drawn.
Emitter exponent
Exponent \(x\) in \(q = kH^x\): about 1 laminar, 0.5 orifice, near 0 pressure-compensating.
Christiansen uniformity coefficient
100 × (1 − mean absolute deviation / mean) of measured emitter or channel flows.
Leaching fraction
Fraction of applied water that drains; at steady state equal to \(c_i/c_d\) for salts the crop does not take up.
Water use (L kg⁻¹)
Total water input of a crop divided by its marketable yield; transpiration, product water, drain, discards and leaks.
