Water quality, fish welfare and system health

For a fish, water is air, dining room, toilet and medicine cabinet at once. Its gills — a barrier only one or two cells thick between blood and water, spread over a surface that is huge for the size of the animal — exchange oxygen, carbon dioxide, ammonia and ions with the water every second. Whatever the plants, bacteria and engineers do to that water, the fish feels immediately. This lesson looks at an aquaponic system from the fish's side of the gill: the chemistry that decides whether ammonia and nitrite are harmless or lethal, the oxygen budget that can collapse in minutes, the carbonate system that links pH, alkalinity and CO₂, the Five Domains of animal welfare and the European rules that apply to farmed fish, and the biosecurity and food-safety practices that keep fish, plants and consumers healthy.

  • Assess fish welfare with the Five Domains model and operational welfare indicators, and explain biosecurity, disease prevention, food safety and the EU legal framework for aquaponic fish (LO6, LO7).

Water quality from the fish's point of view

Fish blood comes within one or two cells of the water as it passes through the gills and skin (Wurts & Durborow, 1992). That thin barrier makes gas exchange efficient — and makes fish exquisitely sensitive to what is dissolved around them. Water supplies the oxygen fish breathe, dilutes and removes their toxic metabolites and supports them against gravity; inappropriate water quality impairs physiology, growth and feed efficiency, triggers the release of stress hormones and, if severe or prolonged, suppresses immune function or kills (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Fish pathogens are present in almost any water, and fish normally resist them — unless water quality, handling and crowding overload their capacity to cope (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). Good water quality is therefore the foundation of fish welfare, fish health and production in aquaponics.

The difficulty is that the water-quality variables are not independent. Feeding drives respiration and excretion; respiration consumes oxygen and releases CO₂; CO₂ and nitrification lower the pH; the pH decides how much of the ammonia is in its toxic form; temperature changes almost everything at once. Ammonia: the equilibrium that decides toxicity

Test kits and laboratory methods measure total ammonia nitrogen (TAN): the sum of ammonium ions (NH₄⁺) and un-ionised ammonia (NH₃), expressed as nitrogen. Only a fraction of TAN is present as NH₃, but that fraction is what matters, because biological membranes are permeable to NH₃ and relatively impermeable to NH₄⁺ (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Ammonia normally leaves the fish across the gills by diffusion down its concentration gradient; high external NH₃ reverses the gradient, so ammonia is retained or even taken up. Acute ammonia toxicity acts on the central nervous system; chronic exposure damages gills and kidneys, slows growth, reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood and raises stress hormones (Randall & Tsui, 2002; Durborow, Crosby & Brunson, 1997a; Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). The balance between the two forms is an acid–base equilibrium.

Common misconception

"The test kit says 1 mg L⁻¹ ammonia, so the fish are being poisoned." Test kits measure TAN, not the toxic NH₃. Whether 1 mg L⁻¹ TAN is harmless or dangerous depends on the pH and temperature: at pH 7.0 and 20 °C only 0.4 % of it is NH₃; at pH 8.6 and 30 °C, 24 % is. Always read TAN together with pH and temperature (Durborow et al., 1997a).

Nitrite and the chloride shield

Nitrite is the intermediate of nitrification (Lesson 6.1). In a mature biofilter it is oxidised as fast as it forms, but it accumulates when nitrite oxidisers lag behind — in a new system, after a temperature drop, after vigorous cleaning or a chemical treatment, or when the feed input suddenly rises (Durborow, Crosby & Brunson, 1997b; Masser et al., 1999; Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). For freshwater fish, "nitrite is actively taken up across the gills in competition with chloride" and disrupts ion regulation, respiration, circulation, hormones and excretion (Kroupova et al., 2005). Its best-known effect is in the blood: nitrite oxidises the iron of haemoglobin from Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺, producing methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen. Blood plasma can concentrate nitrite to about ten times the level in the water, and methaemoglobin levels above 50 % are considered threatening (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Affected blood turns chocolate-brown — "brown blood disease" — and the fish gasp at the surface as if short of oxygen, even when the water is well oxygenated (Durborow et al., 1997b).

Sensitivity differs widely between species. Largemouth bass and sunfish resist high nitrite; catfish are sensitive; trout and other cool-water fish are sensitive to "extremely small amounts" (Durborow et al., 1997b). Masser et al. (1999) report that 0.5 mg L⁻¹ nitrite-N stresses catfish, while tilapia show little stress below 5 mg L⁻¹; in Nile tilapia, 48-hour exposure to 0.5–1.4 mg L⁻¹ nitrite-N already raised methaemoglobin (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Toxicity also depends on pH, temperature, oxygen, exposure time, fish size and — above all — on the chloride concentration of the water (Kroupova et al., 2005). Low oxygen makes nitrite more dangerous, because both attack the same oxygen supply (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017).

Units trap

Nitrite is reported either as the nitrite ion (NO₂⁻) or as nitrite-nitrogen (NO₂⁻-N); the first number is 3.28 times the second (46.01/14.01). The SRAC rule of thumb that "every 1 ppm TAN can convert to 3 ppm nitrite" (Durborow et al., 1997b) counts nitrite as the ion. The chloride ratios below use nitrite-N unless stated otherwise.

Chloride is the cheap and effective antidote. Because chloride and nitrite compete for the same uptake route in the gill (Figure 2), raising the chloride concentration keeps nitrite out. Six to ten parts of chloride protect fish from one part of nitrite-N (Masser et al., 1999); a chloride to nitrite-nitrogen ratio of 10:1 "generally will protect against methemoglobin build-up" (Losordo et al., 1998); catfish farmers keep at least 100 mg L⁻¹ chloride as permanent insurance (Durborow et al., 1997b). In aquaponics, however, the salt comes with a price for the plants.

Dissolved oxygen: supply, demand and the failure clock

Oxygen is the water-quality variable with "the most immediate and drastic effect on aquaponics": fish may die within hours at low dissolved oxygen (Somerville et al., 2014), and in an intensively loaded recirculating system a failure of aeration or oxygenation "can lead to a total loss of the fish crop in 1/2 hour or less" (Losordo et al., 1998). In most systems the capacity to add oxygen is the first factor that limits how many fish can be carried (Losordo et al., 1998). Guidelines differ with species and purpose: below about 3 mg L⁻¹ is too low for most fish, 3–5 mg L⁻¹ is tolerable for 12–24 hours but stressful, and about 7 mg L⁻¹ supports growth and activity (Somerville et al., 2014, Figure 3.3); warm-water systems should stay above 5 mg L⁻¹ or 60 % of saturation, and nitrifiers become inefficient below 2 mg L⁻¹ (Masser et al., 1999). Plant roots need oxygen as well: the UVI system keeps its raft tanks near 80 % saturation (6–7 mg L⁻¹) with a diffuser every 4 feet (Rakocy et al., 2006; see also the root-zone lesson).

The upper limit is set by physics. Water in contact with air holds a saturation concentration of oxygen that falls with temperature and altitude and, much less, with salinity. Benson and Krause (1984) measured it precisely; their equation is the standard in limnology and in this course.

Carbon dioxide, pH and alkalinity

Every gram of oxygen a fish consumes returns as CO₂. Carbon dioxide rarely kills fish directly, but it acidifies the blood as it accumulates (hypercarbia), which lowers the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity (the Bohr effect) — so at a given dissolved oxygen fish may suffocate when CO₂ is high and appear unaffected when it is low (Wurts & Durborow, 1992; Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Fish begin to be stressed above about 20 mg L⁻¹ CO₂ (Masser et al., 1999); catfish tolerate 20–30 mg L⁻¹ if it accumulates slowly and oxygen is above 5 mg L⁻¹ (Wurts & Durborow, 1992); and in rainbow trout, CO₂ in combination with water hardness has been linked to nephrocalcinosis, calcium deposits in the kidney (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). The CO₂ concentration cannot be understood without the carbonate system, which also sets the pH.

Fish welfare: from Five Freedoms to Five Domains

Are fish the kind of animal whose welfare matters? A review of fish cognition by Brown (2015) found that "fish perception and cognitive abilities often match or exceed other vertebrates" and that the evidence "strongly suggests that fish experience pain in a manner similar to the rest of the vertebrates"; its conclusion is that "best practice would be to lend fish the same level of protection as any other vertebrate". European law treats animals as sentient beings whose welfare must be taken into account in EU policies (Article 13 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union), and the welfare of farmed fish is increasingly a criterion for consumers and retailers (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017).

Welfare science has moved from lists of freedoms — freedom from hunger, discomfort, pain, fear and the freedom to express normal behaviour — to models that ask what an animal experiences. The widely used Five Domains model assesses four domains that give rise to experiences — nutrition, physical environment, health and behavioural interactions — and a fifth, the animal's mental state, where those experiences are integrated; the 2020 update explicitly includes interactions with humans (Mellor et al., 2020). Figure 7 applies it to aquaponic fish.

Welfare must be judged from indicators. Environment-based indicators — oxygen, CO₂, ammonia, nitrite, temperature and solids — can be measured continuously and are the backbone of aquaponic monitoring (Lesson 9.2 explains the sensors). Animal-based indicators show how the fish actually cope. The first sign of trouble is usually a weaker feeding response; food-anticipatory activity is itself a promising welfare indicator (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). Healthy fish swim energetically in species-typical patterns and have intact skin; diseased or stressed fish swim near the surface or along the sides, crowd at the inflow, gasp, flash and scratch against the tank, lose balance, hang listlessly or lie on the bottom, and show fin damage, lesions, discoloration or abnormal gills (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). When most fish in a tank behave abnormally, check the water first; when only a few do, remove and examine them (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019).

Stocking density is the most debated welfare parameter. Reviewing 43 studies on rainbow trout, Ellis et al. (2002) found that higher density does not appear to cause prolonged crowding stress, but commonly reduces feed conversion efficiency, nutritional condition and growth and increases fin erosion. There is direct experimental evidence that deteriorating water quality causes these effects and only inferential evidence for aggression; very low densities can also harm welfare through aggression and poor feeding. Their conclusion is directly relevant to aquaponic design: legislation that simply caps density "is likely to be unworkable", and "a more practical option might be to prescribe acceptable levels of water quality, health, nutritional condition and behavioural indicators" (Ellis et al., 2002). Many factors that are important for welfare are also important for production, but some routine procedures — crowding before transport, handling out of water — harm welfare and should be minimised even when they conflict with production priorities (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017).

What EU law requires (as of 2026)

EU animal-welfare law protects farmed fish only in general terms. Council Directive 98/58/EC on animals kept for farming purposes defines "animal" to include fish and requires owners to "take all reasonable steps to ensure the welfare of animals under their care and to ensure that those animals are not caused any unnecessary pain, suffering or injury" (Article 3) — but its detailed Annex applies to animals "other than fish, reptiles or amphibians" (Article 4). Regulation (EC) No 1099/2009 on the protection of animals at the time of killing likewise applies to fish only through its key principle that animals "shall be spared any avoidable pain, distress or suffering" (Articles 1 and 3(1)); it has no explicit obligation to stun fish, but it allows member states to keep or adopt stricter national rules (Reinhardt, Hoevenaars & Joyce, 2019). Several do: Germany requires stunning of all fish except flatfish and eels and a certificate of competence for those who kill them (Reinhardt et al., 2019), and Sweden's Animal Welfare Act (2018:1192), which covers all animals kept by humans, requires any animal slaughtered or killed by bleeding to be stunned (chapter 5, section 1). Voluntary guidance exists at EU level: in 2021 the EU Platform on Animal Welfare endorsed Guidelines on water quality and handling for the welfare of farmed vertebrate fish. The Commission's ongoing revision of EU animal-welfare legislation has so far produced a transport proposal (2023), a regulation on dogs and cats adopted in 2026 and planned revisions for laying hens, broilers and pigs — none specific to farmed fish (European Commission, 2026).

Welfare in practice

In a coupled aquaponic system, fish welfare and plant yield are tied together through the water. A farmer tempted to raise the pH for the nitrifiers, add salt for the fish or push the feed for the plants is making a welfare decision as well as an agronomic one. Decoupled systems (Lesson 6.2) reduce these conflicts by giving the fish their own water.

Biosecurity, disease prevention and food safety

Treating a sick fish in a coupled aquaponic system is extraordinarily difficult. Pesticides that are safe for plants are toxic to fish and to the nitrifying biofilm; antibiotics and fungicides harm the biofilm and other beneficial microorganisms, and antibiotics are not allowed for plant application (Goddek et al., 2015). Therapeutants for fish should not be used because "vegetables may absorb and concentrate them", and the common practice of adding salt to treat fish diseases harms the plants (Rakocy et al., 2006). Formalin, used against parasites in fish-only systems, damages plants (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2017). If medication is unavoidable, diseased fish must be moved to a separate hospital or quarantine tank, and the medicated water must not return to the system; decoupled systems allow treatments of the fish loop that are impossible in coupled ones (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). EU rules point in the same direction: the Veterinary Medicinal Products Regulation (EU) 2019/6 restricts the preventive (prophylactic) use of antimicrobials to exceptional cases.

Prevention is therefore everything. Aquaponic health management follows the principles of aquaculture biosecurity: avoid introducing pathogens, stop them spreading, and avoid conditions that make fish susceptible (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). The three main measures are a pathogen-free water supply, certified pathogen-free stock and strict sanitation. In practice: quarantine all new fish — for example for 45 days in an isolation tank before they join the system (Somerville et al., 2014); disinfect nets and tools and use footbaths and hand washing; remove dead or dying fish promptly; separate age groups and species; avoid high densities that cause skin lesions; and use vaccines, probiotics and immunostimulant feeds where available (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). More than half (54.9 %) of infectious disease outbreaks in aquaculture are caused by bacteria, followed by viruses, parasites and fungi (McLoughlin & Graham, 2007, as cited by Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). Biological control is promising: cleaner fish against sea lice in Norwegian salmon farming, leopard plecos grazing the cysts of the white-spot parasite Ichthyophthirius in tilapia tanks, and probiotic bacteria against several fish pathogens (Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). On the plant side, integrated pest management replaces chemistry: Rakocy et al. (2006) recommend parasitic wasps and ladybirds against whiteflies and aphids; at UVI, caterpillars were controlled by twice-weekly sprays of Bacillus thuringiensis, and the root pathogen Pythium, a problem in summer, dissipated when the water cooled in winter.

Food safety: what the evidence shows

Because aquaponic vegetables grow in water that has passed through fish tanks, consumers and regulators ask whether they carry human pathogens. The European Food Safety Authority has identified aquaponics as a new food-production practice that deserves attention for emerging risks (Afonso et al., 2017, as cited by Yavuzcan Yildiz et al., 2019). The direct evidence is still limited. In a greenhouse study with Nile tilapia, lettuce, basil and tomato, Wang, Deering and Kim (2020) found Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) in fish faeces, in the recirculating water of both aquaponic and hydroponic systems and on the surface of the roots — but not inside roots, leaves or fruit; Listeria monocytogenes and Salmonella were not found in any sample. The fish were probably contaminated before they arrived, which underlines the value of pathogen-free stock; the authors concluded that the main production risk is water splashing onto edible parts at harvest. (A comment on the study was published in 2021 — a reminder that single studies are not the last word.) Figure 8 summarises the pathways and the barriers.

Aquaponic farms that sell food are food businesses. They must follow the general hygiene rules of Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, keep records of feed, pesticide and veterinary-medicine use, and operate procedures based on HACCP principles; fish as food of animal origin also fall under Regulation (EC) No 853/2004, whose registration requirements depend on volumes and markets (Reinhardt et al., 2019). Fish farms must notify the veterinary authorities of suspected notifiable diseases under EU animal-health law, now unified in Regulation (EU) 2016/429 (Reinhardt et al., 2019). And as discussed in Lesson 6.2, the products cannot be sold as organic in the EU, because Regulation (EU) 2018/848 prohibits both hydroponic crop production and closed recirculation aquaculture facilities (except hatcheries and nurseries) (Kledal, König & Matulić, 2019).

The ratio of chloride to nitrite-N (typically 6–10:1) that protects freshwater fish from nitrite uptake.
Oxygen saturation
The dissolved-oxygen concentration in equilibrium with air at a given temperature and pressure.
Volumetric transfer coefficient (kLa)
The rate constant of gas transfer by an aerator; transfer equals kLa times the saturation deficit.
Specific dynamic action
The rise in oxygen consumption after a meal, caused by digestion and assimilation.
Hypercarbia
Elevated CO₂ in water and blood; it acidifies the blood and reduces its oxygen-carrying capacity.
Alkalinity
The acid-neutralising capacity of water, mostly bicarbonate at pH 6–8.3, expressed as mg L⁻¹ CaCO₃.
Q₁₀
The factor by which a biological rate changes for a 10 °C change in temperature.
Five Domains model
A welfare-assessment framework: nutrition, physical environment, health and behavioural interactions shape the animal's mental state.
Operational welfare indicator
A measurable environment-based or animal-based sign of welfare that can be used routinely on a farm.
Biosecurity
Measures that reduce the introduction and spread of pathogens and the conditions that make animals susceptible to infection.
