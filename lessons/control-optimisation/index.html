Control, optimisation and autonomous growing

At three o'clock on a January night in Västerås nobody is awake in the greenhouse, yet every few seconds a controller compares the air temperature with its set-point and moves the valve that sends hot water through the heating pipes. Control engineering is the invisible grower: it decides how much energy a greenhouse uses, how steady its climate is and how well the crop grows. This lesson starts with the thermostat, derives the dynamics of a greenhouse from its energy balance, and builds up the PID controller that runs most of the world's climate computers. It then climbs to model predictive control, economic optimal control and reinforcement learning — the methods with which, in the Autonomous Greenhouse Challenges, algorithms have grown cucumbers, tomatoes and lettuce without a human grower at the controls.

  • Explain cascade control, temperature integration and DIF, and formulate a model predictive or economic optimal control problem with a cost function, a prediction horizon and constraints (LO3, LO5).
  • Describe reinforcement learning as a Markov decision process, perform a Q-learning update, and critically evaluate the results of the Autonomous Greenhouse Challenges (LO5, LO1).

Open loop, closed loop and the language of control

Every control problem has the same ingredients. There is a process — the greenhouse air, the nutrient tank, the growth chamber — whose output we want to keep at a desired value: the controlled or process variable \(y\) (air temperature, EC, CO₂ concentration), with its desired value, the set-point \(r\). We act on the process through an actuator — a heating valve, a vent motor, a dosing pump — whose setting is the manipulated variable \(u\). And the process is pushed around by disturbances \(d\) that we do not control: the sun, the outside temperature and wind, the crop's transpiration, a door left open. The error \(e = r - y\) is what a controller tries to keep small.

In open-loop control the actuator follows a fixed plan and never looks at the result: lights on from 06:00 to 22:00, irrigation every two hours, the heating valve at 40 %. Open loops are simple, cheap and cannot become unstable, but they cannot correct anything the plan did not foresee. In closed-loop (feedback) control a sensor measures the output and the controller adjusts the actuator according to the error. Feedback copes with unknown disturbances and with an imperfect knowledge of the process — the reason it is everywhere — but it can only react after an error has appeared, it depends entirely on the quality of its sensor (Lesson 9.1), and an over-eager feedback loop oscillates. Feedforward control measures a disturbance and acts before it affects the output: a climate computer that reduces heating as soon as the solar radiation sensor on the roof reports the sun, or that starts an irrigation cycle when the accumulated radiation sum passes a threshold. Practical systems combine feedforward with feedback.

History

James Watt's centrifugal governor (1788) was a mechanical feedback controller that kept a steam engine's speed constant; when such governors began to "hunt" (oscillate), James Clerk Maxwell wrote the first mathematical stability analysis of a feedback system (Maxwell, 1868). Nicolas Minorsky analysed three-term — proportional, integral and derivative — control for steering ships in 1922 (Minorsky, 1922), and in 1942 Ziegler and Nichols published tuning rules that are still taught (Ziegler & Nichols, 1942). Much of the modern theory of practical PID control was developed at Lund University by Karl Johan Åström and Tore Hägglund (Åström & Hägglund, 2006).

Process dynamics: from the energy balance to first order plus dead time

To control a process you must know how it responds. In Lesson 8.1 you wrote the steady-state energy balance of a greenhouse (Eq. 8.1.6): heat in equals heat out. Control is about the moments when the balance does not hold — the heat that goes into warming the air, the structure and the crop. Adding that storage term turns the balance into a differential equation.

Dynamic energy balance → a first-order process(10.4.1)
\[ C\,\frac{\mathrm dT}{\mathrm dt} = Q_h + S - K\,(T-T_o)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau\,\frac{\mathrm dT}{\mathrm dt} + T = T_o + \frac{Q_h+S}{K},\qquad \tau = \frac{C}{K},\qquad K_p = \frac{1}{K} \]

the heat stored per second in the greenhouse (heat capacity times the rate of temperature change) equals the heating power plus the useful solar gain minus the heat lost to the outside, which is proportional to the temperature difference. Dividing by the loss coefficient gives the standard first-order form: the temperature relaxes towards a steady-state value with a time constant equal to heat capacity divided by the loss coefficient, and each extra watt per square metre of heating raises that steady state by \(1/K\) kelvin.

  1. Draw a control volume around 1 m² of greenhouse floor (air plus whatever thermal mass exchanges heat quickly with it). Conservation of energy: rate of change of stored heat = inflows − outflows.
  2. Stored heat \(= C\,T\) (per m²), so its rate of change is \(C\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dt\). For the air alone, \(C = \rho c_p \bar h\), the air density times its specific heat times the mean greenhouse height.
  3. Inflows: heating \(Q_h\) and absorbed solar radiation \(S\); outflow: \(K(T-T_o)\), with \(K\) collecting conduction through the cover and leakage (Eqs. 8.1.5 and 8.1.7). Latent heat and soil flux are neglected here or lumped into \(K\) and \(S\).
  4. Divide by \(K\): \((C/K)\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm dt + T = T_o + (Q_h+S)/K\). The group \(C/K\) has units J K⁻¹ ÷ W K⁻¹ = s: the time constant \(\tau\).
  5. For constant inputs the solution is \(T(t) = T_{ss} + (T_0 - T_{ss})\,e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(T_{ss} = T_o + (Q_h+S)/K\): after one time constant 63.2 % of the change has happened, after three 95 % — exactly like the sensor of Lesson 9.1 (Eq. 9.1.2), which obeys the same mathematics.
Assumptions and validity

A single, well-mixed temperature and a constant \(K\). Real greenhouses have several heat stores with different time constants — air (minutes), crop and structure (tens of minutes), heating pipes (minutes), soil (hours to days) — and \(K\) changes with wind, screens and vent opening. For control design, a first-order model with an effective heat capacity, identified from a step test (Eq. 10.4.2), is usually good enough; detailed simulation models such as GreenLight (Katzin et al., 2020) resolve the separate stores.

(b) Crop, structure and heating pipes add an estimated 50 kJ m⁻² K⁻¹ of fast thermal mass; recalculate. Reveal the reasoning

Real processes rarely behave as a pure first-order system. Hot water must first travel from the boiler to the pipes, heat must be conducted through the pipe wall, the air must mix, and the sensor itself has a time constant. Such chains of small lags make the response start with a delay and an S-shaped foot. Control engineers capture all of this with one extra parameter: the dead time \(\theta\), during which the output does not yet respond at all.

First-order-plus-dead-time (FOPDT) model and two-point identification(10.4.2)
\[ y(t) = \begin{cases} y_0 & t<\theta\\ y_0 + K\,\Delta u\,\left[1-e^{-(t-\theta)/\tau}\right] & t\ge\theta\end{cases},\qquad \tau = 1.5\,(t_{63}-t_{28}),\qquad \theta = t_{63}-\tau \]

after the input is stepped by \(\Delta u\) at time zero, the output stays at its initial value for the dead time and then approaches its new steady state \(y_0 + K\Delta u\) exponentially with time constant \(\tau\). From a recorded step response, read the times at which 28.3 % and 63.2 % of the total change are reached: one and a half times their difference is the time constant, and the dead time is the 63.2 % time minus the time constant.

Follow the derivation of the two-point method
  1. For \(t\ge\theta\), the fraction of the final change reached is \(F(t) = 1-e^{-(t-\theta)/\tau}\).
  2. Choose two convenient fractions: \(F = 1-e^{-1/3} = 0.283\) is reached when \(t-\theta = \tau/3\); \(F = 1-e^{-1} = 0.632\) when \(t-\theta = \tau\). Hence \(t_{28} = \theta+\tau/3\) and \(t_{63} = \theta+\tau\).
  3. Subtract: \(t_{63}-t_{28} = 2\tau/3\), so \(\tau = 1.5\,(t_{63}-t_{28})\); then \(\theta = t_{63}-\tau\). The gain is simply \(K = \Delta y_\infty/\Delta u\). (This is Smith's method, found in process-control textbooks such as Seborg et al., 2016.)
  4. Using two points in the middle of the response makes the estimate robust against the curved foot of higher-order processes; alternatively, fit all three parameters by non-linear least squares (Lesson 10.1, Eq. 10.1.7) — Figure 3 does both.
  5. In Laplace-transform language the model is the transfer function \(G(s) = K e^{-\theta s}/(\tau s+1)\) — the starting point of the tuning rules in Section 10.4.5.
Assumptions and validity

The process must start at steady state, the step must be large compared with the noise but small enough to stay in the linear range, and no other disturbance may change during the test (a cloud passing over a greenhouse spoils a temperature step test). The ratio \(\theta/\tau\) measures how hard the process is to control: as a rule of thumb, below about 0.3 tight feedback control is easy, while dead-time-dominated processes (\(\theta/\tau > 1\)) limit how fast any feedback controller can respond.

On/off control with hysteresis

The simplest feedback controller switches an actuator fully on or fully off — a thermostat on an electric heater, a solenoid valve for CO₂, a fan. If it switched exactly at the set-point, measurement noise would make it chatter on and off many times a second, wearing out relays and contactors. A hysteresis band (dead band) prevents this: switch on when the temperature falls below \(r-h\) and off when it rises above \(r+h\), and leave the actuator alone in between.

On/off control with hysteresis and its limit cycle(10.4.3)
\[ u_k = \begin{cases} 1 & T_k < r-h\\ 0 & T_k > r+h\\ u_{k-1} & \text{otherwise}\end{cases},\qquad t_{\text{on}} = \tau\ln\frac{T_\infty-(r-h)}{T_\infty-(r+h)},\qquad t_{\text{off}} = \tau\ln\frac{(r+h)-T_o}{(r-h)-T_o} \]

the heater is switched on when the temperature is below the lower edge of the band and off when it is above the upper edge; inside the band it keeps its previous state. For a first-order process without dead time the temperature then oscillates between the edges of the band: the heating phase lasts \(t_{\text{on}}\), the time to climb from \(r-h\) to \(r+h\) towards the temperature \(T_\infty\) the heater would eventually reach, and the cooling phase lasts \(t_{\text{off}}\), the time to fall back towards the outside temperature \(T_o\).

Follow the derivation
  1. While the heater is on, Eq. 10.4.1 gives \(T(t) = T_\infty + (T_{\text{start}}-T_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}\). Starting at \(r-h\), the time to reach \(r+h\) solves \(r+h = T_\infty + (r-h-T_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}\), i.e. \(e^{-t/\tau} = \dfrac{T_\infty-(r+h)}{T_\infty-(r-h)}\), which gives \(t_{\text{on}}\).
  2. While the heater is off, the temperature decays towards \(T_o\): from \(r+h\) to \(r-h\) takes \(t_{\text{off}} = \tau\ln\dfrac{(r+h)-T_o}{(r-h)-T_o}\).
  3. For a narrow band, \(\ln(1+x)\approx x\) gives \(t_{\text{on}}\approx 2h\tau/(T_\infty-r)\) and \(t_{\text{off}}\approx 2h\tau/(r-T_o)\): the period is proportional to the band width and to the time constant, and the duty cycle \(t_{\text{on}}/P \approx (r-T_o)/(T_\infty-T_o)\) is exactly the fraction of full power needed to hold the set-point.
  4. With dead time \(\theta\), the heater keeps heating for \(\theta\) after the temperature crosses \(r+h\) (and the process keeps cooling for \(\theta\) after crossing \(r-h\)). The swing grows by about \((T_\infty-r-h)(1-e^{-\theta/\tau})\) above the band and \((r-h-T_o)(1-e^{-\theta/\tau})\) below it — independent of how narrow you make the band.
Assumptions and validity

On/off control always produces a sustained oscillation (a limit cycle); only its amplitude and period can be chosen. It is appropriate for on/off actuators, slow processes and wide tolerances. Where the actuator allows, time-proportioning ("slow pulse-width modulation") or a continuous controller gives a steadier result.

Arguments of the logarithms are ratios of temperature differences (dimensionless); \(\tau\) in s → \(t_{\text{on}}\), \(t_{\text{off}}\) in s ✓.

PID control

A continuous actuator — a mixing valve, a variable-speed fan, a dimmable LED — allows a smoother strategy: make the correction proportional to the error. The PID controller adds two refinements: a term proportional to the accumulated error (integral action) and a term proportional to its rate of change (derivative action). It is by far the most widely used control algorithm in industry and in greenhouse climate computers.

The PID control law(10.4.4)
\[ u(t) = u_0 + K_c\left[\,e(t) + \frac{1}{T_i}\int_0^{t} e(t')\,\mathrm dt' + T_d\,\frac{\mathrm de(t)}{\mathrm dt}\right],\qquad e(t) = r(t)-y(t) \]

the actuator setting equals a bias plus the controller gain times the sum of three terms: the present error, the accumulated past error divided by the integral time, and the predicted future error — its current rate of change times the derivative time.

Follow the reasoning: what each term does
  1. P — proportional. \(u = u_0 + K_ce\). Bigger errors get bigger corrections. But consider a process with gain \(K\) and a load disturbance \(d\): at steady state \(y = K u + d\) and \(u = u_0 + K_c(r-y)\). Solving, \(e = r - y = \dfrac{r - Ku_0 - d}{1+KK_c}\): a steady-state offset remains unless the bias happens to be exactly right. Raising \(K_c\) shrinks the offset but makes the loop oscillatory.
  2. I — integral. The integral keeps growing as long as any error remains. At steady state every term must be constant, which is only possible for the integral if \(e = 0\): integral action removes the offset. In effect it finds the correct bias automatically. Too much integral action (small \(T_i\)) causes overshoot and slow oscillation.
  3. D — derivative. \(T_d\,\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\) extrapolates the error \(T_d\) seconds ahead, so the controller brakes before the temperature overshoots. It adds damping and allows a higher gain, but it amplifies measurement noise (the derivative of noise is large), so it is always filtered and often left out: most greenhouse loops are PI.
  4. Why "time" parameters? For a constant error \(e\), the P term gives \(K_ce\) and the I term grows by \(K_ce\) every \(T_i\) seconds — hence "reset time". A ramp error \(e = at\) makes the D term equal to \(K_cT_da\), the P term's value \(T_d\) seconds later — hence "derivative time".
Assumptions and validity

This is the "ideal" or standard form; controllers also exist in parallel form (\(K_p e + K_i\int e + K_d\dot e\), with \(K_i = K_c/T_i\) and \(K_d = K_cT_d\)) and series form — check which one your controller uses before copying gains. The law assumes a continuous, unconstrained actuator; real valves saturate at 0 and 100 %, which leads to integral windup (Eq. 10.4.5).

\(K_c e\): (% °C⁻¹)(°C) = % ✓; \(\frac{1}{T_i}\int e\,\mathrm dt\): (s⁻¹)(°C s) = °C ✓; \(T_d\,\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\): (s)(°C s⁻¹) = °C ✓ — the bracket is in °C, multiplied by \(K_c\) gives %.

A climate computer does not compute integrals and derivatives continuously: it samples the sensor every \(\Delta t\) seconds (typically 1–60 s) and updates the output. Three practical problems must be handled in that discrete implementation.

  • Integral windup. When the actuator saturates — the heating valve is fully open on a very cold night, or fully closed on a sunny afternoon — the error persists and the integral keeps growing to a huge value. When conditions change, the controller must first "unwind" this integral before the actuator moves, causing a large overshoot. The cure is anti-windup: stop integrating while the output is saturated in the direction of the error (conditional integration), or feed the difference between the saturated and the requested output back into the integrator (back-calculation) (Åström & Rundqwist, 1989).
  • Derivative kick. A step in the set-point is an infinitely fast change of the error; the derivative term responds with a spike that slams the actuator. Taking the derivative of the measurement instead of the error (\(-\mathrm dy/\mathrm dt\) instead of \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt\)) gives the same braking action against real changes but ignores set-point steps.
  • Noise. The derivative of a noisy signal is noisier still; a first-order filter on the derivative (typically with time constant \(T_d/N\), \(N\approx 5\)–20) keeps it usable.
Discrete PID with derivative on measurement and anti-windup(10.4.5)
\[ u_k = \operatorname{sat}\Bigl(u_0 + K_c e_k + I_k - K_c T_d\,\frac{y_k-y_{k-1}}{\Delta t}\Bigr),\qquad I_{k+1} = \begin{cases} I_k + \dfrac{K_c\,\Delta t}{T_i}\,e_k & \text{if the output is not saturated in the direction of } e_k\\ I_k & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]

at every sampling instant the controller computes the proportional term from the current error, adds the stored integral, subtracts a derivative term based on how fast the measurement is changing, and clips the result to the actuator's range. The integral is updated by the error times the sampling interval scaled by \(K_c/T_i\) — unless the actuator is already at its limit and the error would push it further, in which case the integral is frozen.

  1. Replace the integral by a running sum of rectangles: \(\int_0^{t_k}e\,\mathrm dt \approx \sum_{j<k} e_j\Delta t\). Multiplying by \(K_c/T_i\) and storing the running total gives the recursion \(I_{k+1} = I_k + (K_c\Delta t/T_i)e_k\).
  2. Replace the derivative by a backward difference: \(\mathrm de/\mathrm dt \approx (e_k-e_{k-1})/\Delta t\). With a constant set-point, \(e_k-e_{k-1} = -(y_k-y_{k-1})\); using the measurement form for all times removes the kick when \(r\) jumps.
  3. Anti-windup by conditional integration: if \(u_k\) was clipped at \(u_{\max}\) and \(e_k > 0\) (the error asks for even more), or clipped at \(u_{\min}\) and \(e_k < 0\), skip the integral update.
  4. An equivalent "velocity" form computes the change \(\Delta u_k = K_c(e_k-e_{k-1}) + (K_c\Delta t/T_i)e_k - K_cT_d(y_k-2y_{k-1}+y_{k-2})/\Delta t\) and adds it to the previous output; clipping the output then prevents windup automatically.
  5. Choose \(\Delta t\) small compared with the closed-loop response — as a rule of thumb at most a tenth of the time constant and less than about a quarter of the dead time.

Integral windup in a greenhouse

On a sunny spring afternoon the greenhouse is warmer than the heating set-point, so the heating controller closes its valve completely — and, without anti-windup, its integral keeps accumulating negative error for hours. At sunset the air cools below the set-point, but the valve stays shut until the integral has “unwound”, and the crop gets a cold evening. Every commercial climate computer therefore limits or freezes its integrators; if you program your own controller for the course project (for example on an Arduino), you must do the same.

Tuning: Ziegler–Nichols, Cohen–Coon and SIMC

Tuning means choosing \(K_c\), \(T_i\) and \(T_d\). Trial and error on a real greenhouse is slow and risky, so engineers start from rules that map the three FOPDT parameters of a step test onto controller settings. The rules differ in what they aim for. Ziegler and Nichols (1942) aimed at a fast response in which each oscillation is a quarter of the previous one — aggressive by modern standards. Cohen and Coon (1953) refined the formulas for processes with larger dead time. Skogestad's SIMC rules (Skogestad, 2003), developed at NTNU in Trondheim, aim for a smooth first-order closed-loop response with one tuning knob, the desired closed-loop time constant \(\tau_c\).

Tuning rules for an FOPDT process(10.4.6)
\[ \text{Ziegler--Nichols PI:}\quad K_c = \frac{0.9}{K}\,\frac{\tau}{\theta},\quad T_i = 3.33\,\theta \qquad\qquad \text{PID:}\quad K_c = \frac{1.2}{K}\,\frac{\tau}{\theta},\quad T_i = 2\theta,\quad T_d = 0.5\,\theta \] \[ \text{SIMC PI:}\quad K_c = \frac{1}{K}\,\frac{\tau}{\tau_c+\theta},\qquad T_i = \min\{\tau,\ 4(\tau_c+\theta)\},\qquad \tau_c = \theta\ \text{for tight but robust control} \]

both families set the controller gain inversely proportional to the process gain and proportional to the ratio of time constant to dead time: a sluggish process with little delay can be pushed hard, a process with a long delay must be handled gently. Ziegler–Nichols sets the integral time as a multiple of the dead time; SIMC sets it equal to the process time constant, unless that is very long, and lets you choose how fast the closed loop should be.

Robust (conservative) settings, gain scheduling or adaptive and model-based control address this. A controller for your own system

In the course project you may want to control something yourself: the temperature of a nutrient tank with an aquarium heater, the photoperiod and dimming of an LED panel, or the pump of an aeroponic chamber. The workflow of this lesson applies directly. First, identify the process: switch the actuator from one constant level to another, log the response every few seconds, and fit \(K\), \(\tau\) and \(\theta\) in Figure 3. A 25 W aquarium heater in 20 L of water, for example, heats slowly — a time constant of hours — with a dead time set mostly by mixing and by where the sensor sits, so place the sensor close to the plant roots but not next to the heater. Second, choose the controller: an on/off actuator (a relay, a heater without power control) calls for a thermostat with a band chosen from Eq. 10.4.3 so that the relay switches at most a few times per hour; a continuously adjustable actuator (a dimmable LED driver, a variable-speed pump, a heater driven with slow pulse-width modulation) calls for a PI controller tuned with SIMC. Derivative action is rarely worth its noise in such slow systems.

Third, implement it safely. Microcontroller libraries for PID control exist for every platform, but check three things in the code: that the integral is limited (anti-windup), that the output is clipped to the actuator's range, and that the controller runs at a fixed sampling interval. Add independent safety limits that do not depend on the controller at all — a thermal fuse on the heater, a float switch that stops the pump when the tank is empty, a maximum on-time for any relay — because sensors fail, and a feedback loop with a broken sensor will happily drive its actuator to the limit (the sensor-anomaly detectors of Lesson 10.5 address exactly this). Finally, evaluate the loop as in Figure 5: log set-point, measurement and actuator output, compute the IAE, the overshoot and the energy use, and report them with the tuning you used. A control experiment reported this way is reproducible — and a good piece of engineering in your project report.

Key idea

A controller is judged on several criteria at once: the integrated absolute error (IAE = \(\int|e|\,\mathrm dt\)), the overshoot and settling time after set-point changes, the rejection of disturbances, the robustness to changes in the process, the energy used and the wear of the actuator. No tuning is best for all of them; choose according to what matters for the crop and the installation.

Beyond single loops: cascade control and set-point strategies

Cascade control nests one loop inside another. In a hot-water heated greenhouse, an outer (master) controller compares the air temperature with its set-point and computes the desired pipe-water temperature; an inner (slave) controller moves the mixing valve to achieve that water temperature. The inner loop is fast — minutes — and corrects disturbances in the supply water before they reach the air; the outer loop deals with the slow air dynamics. The interface is also convenient for constraints: minimum and maximum pipe temperatures, used by growers to steer humidity and crop activity, are simply limits on the outer controller's output.

Controllers keep the climate at its set-points; which set-points to use is a horticultural and economic decision. Two classic strategies exploit the physiology of the crop. Temperature integration (TI) is based on the finding that, within limits, growth and development of many greenhouse crops respond to the average temperature over one or several days rather than to the exact temperature at each moment — for tomato, de Koning (1988) found that yield depended on the 24-hour mean over a wide range of day/night regimes. A TI strategy lets the temperature rise on sunny days (when the heat is free) and compensates with cooler nights (when heat is expensive), keeping the average on target (Körner & Challa, 2003).

Temperature integration: the night temperature that keeps the daily mean(10.4.7)
\[ \bar T_{24} = \frac{n_d\,\bar T_{\text{day}} + (24-n_d)\,\bar T_{\text{night}}}{24}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar T_{\text{night}} = \frac{24\,\bar T_{\text{target}}-n_d\,\bar T_{\text{day}}}{24-n_d} \]

the 24-hour mean temperature is the time-weighted average of the day and night means. To reach a target daily mean after a warm day, the night must be as much cooler as the day was warmer, weighted by their durations.

Model predictive control and economic optimal control

A PID controller looks only at the present error and its recent history. A greenhouse, however, is slow, and much of what will disturb it is predictable: tomorrow's sunrise, tonight's frost, the hours when electricity is expensive. Model predictive control (MPC) uses a model of the process to predict the effect of possible future control sequences over a horizon, chooses the sequence that minimises a cost function while respecting constraints, applies only the first step, and repeats the whole calculation at the next sampling instant with fresh measurements — the receding horizon. MPC became the standard advanced control technology of the process industries (Qin & Badgwell, 2003), and its theory is now mature (Mayne, 2014). In greenhouses it is attractive because of the slow dynamics, the weather forecasts, the many interacting actuators and the hard constraints (Iddio et al., 2020).

Model predictive control: the finite-horizon optimisation(10.4.8)
\[ \min_{u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}}\ J = \sum_{k=1}^{N} q\,(\hat y_k - r_k)^2 + \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigl[c_k\,u_k + \rho\,(u_k-u_{k-1})^2\bigr] \] \[ \text{subject to}\quad \hat x_{k+1} = f(\hat x_k, u_k, \hat d_k),\quad \hat y_k = h(\hat x_k),\quad u_{\min}\le u_k\le u_{\max},\quad y_{\min}\le \hat y_k \le y_{\max} \]

over the next \(N\) steps, choose the sequence of control inputs that minimises the weighted sum of squared predicted deviations from the set-point, the cost of the inputs (for example, heating energy at a time-varying price) and a penalty on abrupt changes — subject to the process model driven by forecast disturbances and to limits on the inputs and outputs. Apply the first input, measure, and solve again one step later.

Common misconception

“Optimal control means keeping the crop at its biological optimum.” The biologically fastest growth is rarely the most profitable: heat, CO₂ and electricity cost money, prices change and the season has a deadline. Reinforcement learning and the Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge

MPC needs a model written by engineers. Reinforcement learning (RL) instead learns a control policy from experience: an agent observes the state of its environment, takes an action, receives a reward and a new state, and gradually learns which actions lead to high long-term reward (Sutton & Barto, 2018). You met the idea in Lesson 10.2; here is its mathematics. The environment is formalised as a Markov decision process (MDP): a set of states \(s\) (temperature, time of day, crop stage), actions \(a\) (heater off, half, full), transition probabilities \(P(s'\mid s,a)\) and rewards \(r(s,a)\). A policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) chooses actions; its quality is the expected return, the discounted sum of future rewards.

Return, Bellman optimality and Q-learning(10.4.10)
\[ G_t = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{j}\,r_{t+j+1},\qquad Q^{*}(s,a) = \mathbb E\Bigl[r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q^{*}(s',a')\Bigr],\qquad Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\Bigl[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\Bigr] \]

the return is the sum of all future rewards, each discounted by a factor \(\gamma\) per step. The optimal action value \(Q^*(s,a)\) — the best achievable return after taking action \(a\) in state \(s\) — equals the expected immediate reward plus the discounted value of acting optimally from the next state. Q-learning estimates it from experience: after each step, move the stored value a fraction \(\alpha\) towards the observed reward plus the discounted best value of the next state.

Follow the derivation
  1. Recursion. Split the return into the first reward and the rest: \(G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}\). Taking expectations under the best policy gives the Bellman optimality equation for \(Q^*\) (Bellman's principle: an optimal plan has optimal sub-plans).
  2. Sampling. We do not know \(P(s'\mid s,a)\), but every experienced transition \((s,a,r,s')\) is a sample of the right-hand side. The difference \(\delta = r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\), the temporal-difference error, says how surprised the agent is.
  3. Update. Moving \(Q\) a small step \(\alpha\) towards the sample, \(Q\leftarrow Q+\alpha\delta\), is a stochastic approximation of the Bellman equation. Watkins and Dayan (1992) proved that tabular Q-learning converges to \(Q^*\) if every state–action pair is tried infinitely often and \(\alpha\) decreases appropriately.
  4. Exploration. To keep trying all actions, the agent acts randomly with probability \(\varepsilon\) and greedily (\(\arg\max_a Q\)) otherwise.
  5. Example. \(Q(s,a) = -2.0\), \(r = -0.5\), \(\gamma = 0.9\), \(\max Q(s',\cdot) = -1.2\), \(\alpha = 0.1\): \(\delta = -0.5 - 1.08 + 2.0 = 0.42\), so \(Q\) becomes \(-2.0 + 0.042 = -1.958\).
Assumptions and validity

Tabular Q-learning needs a small, discrete state space and many trials. Real greenhouses have continuous states and one season per year, so practical RL replaces the table by a neural network (deep RL, as in the Atari-playing agent of Mnih et al., 2015) and learns in simulators — crop and climate models such as GreenLight (Katzin et al., 2020) — before touching a real crop. The gap between simulator and reality, safe exploration and reward design are the main open problems.

Can learning algorithms grow a real crop? Wageningen University & Research tested this in the Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge, in which international teams remotely control a high-tech greenhouse compartment with their own algorithms, competing on net profit (and in later editions also on sustainability and autonomy) against each other and, in the first editions, against a compartment run by professional growers.

2018 · 1st edition — cucumber

Five teams each grew cucumbers in a 96 m² compartment from 14 August to 7 December 2018, setting heating, ventilation, screens, lighting, fogging, CO₂ and irrigation remotely. Methods ranged from Bayesian reinforcement learning (team Sonoma) and deep deterministic policy gradients (iGrow) to convolutional and recurrent neural networks. Only one team beat the manually operated reference: Sonoma achieved a net profit of €24.78 m⁻² against €21.18 m⁻², with the lowest use of CO₂, heat and water per kilogram (Hemming et al., 2019).

2019–2020 · 2nd edition — cherry tomato

Five teams, six months of cherry tomato cultivation, the goal again maximum net profit. This time all AI-supported teams outperformed the human-operated reference compartment (Hemming et al., 2020); the winner was team AuTomatoes, with members from TU Delft and horticultural companies (LetsGrow.com, 2020).

2021–2022 · 3rd edition — lettuce

After an online round on lettuce images, teams ran two lettuce cycles in six compartments, deciding climate, plant spacing and harvest time from sensor data and depth-camera images; computer vision estimated plant height and coverage (Petropoulou et al., 2023; Lesson 10.3). The winner was team Koala, from the start-up Koidra and Cornell University (Greenhouse Grower, n.d.).

2024 · 4th edition — dwarf tomato

Six algorithms fully controlled the cultivation of dwarf tomatoes, a crop suited to robotic harvesting but with little prior cultivation data. All teams achieved a profitable strategy; the best reached a production equivalent to 45 kg m⁻² per year, higher than commercial high-wire cherry-tomato growing, mainly thanks to a much higher plant density. Measuring crop status to decide the harvest moment was the hardest part (Maree et al., 2025). Team IDEAS from Zhejiang University won (WUR, 2025).

2026 · 5th edition

Announced by WUR for 2026.

How should we read these results? They show convincingly that algorithms can run a modern greenhouse remotely and that learning-based and model-based strategies can match and exceed experienced growers on profit and resource use. But they are demonstrations, not controlled experiments in the sense of Lesson 3.1: each team had one compartment (\(n = 1\)), teams differed in many decisions at once, and the reference was a single team of growers working remotely under the same rules. It is also striking how much of the winning margin came from strategy decisions — plant density, spacing, harvest timing, cultivar-specific set-points — rather than from minute-by-minute climate control. In simulation, Morcego et al. (2023) formulated MPC and RL for greenhouse climate control in a common framework and analysed their differences and the situations in which each is preferable.

Frontier 2024–2026

Research is converging on hybrids: reinforcement learning to capture long-term economic consequences, MPC to handle constraints and forecasts in the short term. In a 2026 preprint, van Laatum et al. used an RL policy to supply the terminal cost and terminal region of a one-hour-horizon MPC on the GreenLight tomato model; the combination matched the performance of the high-performing guiding policy and clearly outperformed MPC with the same short horizon alone (van Laatum et al., 2026). Such controllers are also the “decide” layer of the digital twins of Lesson 10.5.

Key terms

Open-loop control
Control that follows a fixed plan without measuring the result (timers, schedules).
Feedback (closed-loop) control
Control that measures the controlled variable and acts on the error between set-point and measurement.
Feedforward control
Control that measures a disturbance and acts before it affects the controlled variable.
Set-point
The desired value of the controlled (process) variable.
Time constant
The time a first-order process needs to cover 63.2 % of a step change; \(\tau = C/K\) for a thermal process.
Dead time
The delay before a process output starts to respond to a change of input.
FOPDT model
First-order-plus-dead-time model with gain \(K\), time constant \(\tau\) and dead time \(\theta\), identified from a step test.
Hysteresis band
The dead band of an on/off controller that prevents rapid switching; it sets the amplitude of the resulting limit cycle.
PID controller
Controller whose output combines proportional, integral and derivative actions on the error.
Integral windup
Excessive growth of the integral term while the actuator is saturated, causing overshoot; prevented by anti-windup.
Cascade control
Nested loops in which an outer controller sets the set-point of a faster inner controller.
Temperature integration
Set-point strategy that controls the multi-hour or multi-day mean temperature, letting days run warm and nights cool.
Model predictive control (MPC)
Control that repeatedly optimises future inputs over a horizon using a model, forecasts and constraints, applying only the first input.
Markov decision process
Formal model of sequential decisions with states, actions, transition probabilities and rewards.
Q-learning
Reinforcement-learning algorithm that learns action values from experienced transitions by temporal-difference updates.
