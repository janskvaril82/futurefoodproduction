Resource-use efficiency indicators

"Ninety-five per cent less water." "A hundred times more lettuce per square metre." "Twelve kilowatt-hours per kilogram." Each of these claims about vertical farming can be true, and each can mislead, because an efficiency indicator is a ratio: a numerator, a denominator and a boundary drawn around the system. Change any of the three and the number changes — sometimes by a factor of ten. In this lesson you will learn to define, calculate and compare the indicators that engineers use to judge food production systems — water, energy, light, land, nutrients, CO₂ and carbon — and to see through the choices hidden inside them. At the end you will apply the same template to the system you are growing in your own project.

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish productivity, intensity and efficiency-ratio forms of a resource indicator, convert between them and state the system boundary behind each (LO3).
  • Calculate water-, energy-, light-, land-, nutrient- and CO₂-use efficiency and the carbon footprint per kilogram, with complete unit bookkeeping (LO3).
  • Explain the functional-unit problem — fresh versus dry weight, per nutrient, per cultivated versus per floor area — and show how it can reverse a ranking (LO3, LO7).
  • Compare open-field production, greenhouses in Spain, the Netherlands and Sweden, and plant factories using published data, and explain the trade-offs between land, water and energy (LO1, LO7).
  • Quantify how sensitive an indicator is to its assumptions with elasticities, tornado diagrams and uncertainty propagation (LO3, LO5).
  • Apply a step-by-step template to measure and report the indicators of your own project system (LO3, LO4).

One kilogram of lettuce, six resources

Every kilogram of food is the output of a production system that consumes several resources at once: land (square metres occupied for a year), water, energy (electricity, heat, fuel), light (photons, free from the sun or bought from a lamp), mineral nutrients and, in greenhouses and plant factories, CO₂. The previous lessons of this module wrote the balances for three of these flows — energy (Lesson 8.1), water vapour (Lesson 8.2) and CO₂ (Lesson 8.3). This lesson closes the module by turning balances into indicators: single numbers that say how much of a resource a system needs per unit of useful output, or how much output it delivers per unit of resource.

Indicators are indispensable. They let a grower benchmark her greenhouse against the national average, let an investor compare a vertical farm with a greenhouse, and let a policy maker ask where a litre of water or a kilowatt-hour of electricity produces the most food. They are also dangerous, because a ratio hides its ingredients. The most widely cited comparison of plant factories and greenhouses (Graamans et al., 2018) found that the plant factory needed the most purchased energy per kilogram of lettuce and at the same time used total energy — including free sunlight — most efficiently. Both statements are true; they simply divide different numerators by the same denominator.

Big idea

An efficiency indicator has three parts that must always be reported together: what is counted (the numerator: which water, which energy, which area), per what (the denominator or functional unit: kg fresh weight, kg dry weight, kcal, protein, per year) and where the boundary lies (field gate, farm gate, including or excluding transport, the solar park, the fertiliser factory). Change any of the three and the number — and sometimes the ranking — changes.

Three forms of a resource indicator, and a change of basis(8.4.1)
\[ \begin{gathered} \eta_X = \frac{Y}{X}, \qquad I_X = \frac{X}{Y} = \frac{1}{\eta_X}, \qquad \varepsilon_X = \frac{X_{\text{fixed}}}{X_{\text{supplied}}} \\[6pt] I_{X,\text{DW}} = \frac{I_{X,\text{FW}}}{d}, \qquad I_{X,\text{nutr}} = \frac{I_{X,\text{FW}}}{c} \end{gathered} \]

the productivity of resource X is the product obtained per unit of the resource; the intensity is its inverse, the resource needed per unit of product; the efficiency ratio is the fraction of the supplied resource that ends up fixed in the product. An intensity per kilogram of fresh weight becomes an intensity per kilogram of dry weight by dividing by the dry-matter fraction, and an intensity per unit of nutrient (kcal, protein) by dividing by the nutrient content per kilogram.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Productivity answers "how much food per unit of resource?" — the natural question when the resource is scarce (water in a desert, land in a city). Agronomists often call it "use efficiency" (water-use efficiency in kg m⁻³, energy-use efficiency in kg kWh⁻¹).
  2. Intensity answers "how much resource per unit of food?" — the natural question when you want to add up burdens along a supply chain (litres per kilogram, kWh per kilogram, kg CO₂e per kilogram). Life-cycle assessment works with intensities (Lesson 1.3).
  3. Efficiency ratio compares like with like: water fixed ÷ water supplied, CO₂ fixed ÷ CO₂ supplied, chemical energy in the crop ÷ light energy received. It is dimensionless and bounded by 1, which makes it the only true "efficiency" in the thermodynamic sense (Kozai, 2013).
  4. Change of basis. One kilogram of fresh weight contains \(d\) kg of dry weight, so a resource used per kg FW is spread over only \(d\) kg DW: \(I_{\text{DW}} = I_{\text{FW}}/d\). Likewise, per unit of a nutrient with content \(c\) per kg: \(I_{\text{nutr}} = I_{\text{FW}}/c\).
Assumptions and validity

All three forms are averages over a stated period (a crop cycle, a year) and a stated boundary. A productivity and an intensity are exact inverses only when they use the same boundary and basis. Efficiency ratios exist only for resources that can be incorporated into the product; for purchased energy in general there is no meaningful "fixed" amount, except the chemical energy of the biomass.

Two further distinctions matter before any number is calculated. Water, first, can be withdrawn (pumped from a river or an aquifer) or consumed (evaporated or built into the product, and therefore not returned to the catchment). Drainage returns to the environment, but often loaded with nitrate and phosphate — which is exactly what the grey water footprint tries to capture. Second, many systems have more than one product: a greenhouse may sell tomatoes and surplus heat, a combined heat-and-power plant sells electricity to the grid, and a plant factory may sell lettuce and the heat of its lamps to a district-heating network. The resources and emissions must then be shared between the products — by mass, by energy content or by economic value — or the co-product must be credited with what it replaces (system expansion; Lesson 1.3). Such allocation choices can shift an indicator as much as any technology, so they belong in the report next to the number.

Water: litres per kilogram — and where the water goes

Water is the resource for which controlled environments make their most spectacular claims, and for which the definitions vary most. Three indicators are in common use. The water productivity (often called water-use efficiency, WUE) is the yield per volume of water, in kg m⁻³ or, numerically identical, g L⁻¹. Its inverse, the product water use (PWU), is the volume of water per kilogram of product, in L kg⁻¹ (Nederhoff & Stanghellini, 2010). And the water footprint splits the water consumed along a product's life cycle into green water (rain), blue water (irrigation from surface and groundwater) and grey water (the volume needed to dilute pollution to water-quality standards; Mekonnen & Hoekstra, 2011).

Water productivity, product water use and water footprint(8.4.2)
\[ \text{WP} = \frac{Y}{W_{\text{in}}}, \qquad \text{PWU} = \frac{W_{\text{in}}}{Y} = \frac{1}{\text{WP}}, \qquad \text{WF} = \text{WF}_{\text{green}} + \text{WF}_{\text{blue}} + \text{WF}_{\text{grey}} \]

water productivity is the product obtained per volume of water brought into the system; product water use is the inverse, the water needed per kilogram. The water footprint adds up the rainwater and irrigation water consumed and the volume of water needed to absorb the pollution.

  1. Units: 1 kg m⁻³ = 1000 g ÷ 1000 L = 1 g L⁻¹, and \(1/(1\ \text{g L}^{-1}) = 1\ \text{L g}^{-1} = 1000\) L kg⁻¹. So a WP of 50 g L⁻¹ equals a PWU of \(1000/50 = 20\) L kg⁻¹.

  3. PWU therefore falls when yield rises (bigger denominator), when transpiration per m² falls (humid, less sunny indoor climate), when drainage is reused and when transpired water is recovered — four quite different mechanisms.
  4. The water footprint asks a different question: not "how much water did the farmer pump?" but "how much of the world's freshwater did this kilogram consume or pollute?" Green water from rain has a low opportunity cost in rainy regions such as central Sweden; blue water from an over-used aquifer has a high one.

Worked example 1Why the greenhouse tomato needs less water per kilogram

Nederhoff and Stanghellini (2010) give a numerical example: a drip-irrigated field crop of tomatoes uses about 300 L of water per m² over its season and yields about 5 kg m⁻²; a Dutch greenhouse uses about 1000 L m⁻² over a whole year and yields 50–80 kg m⁻². Calculate PWU and WP for both, decide which factor — water per m² or yield per m² — explains the difference, and compare with the Spanish "parral" greenhouse of Table 2.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Field: PWU \(= 300\ \text{L m}^{-2}/5\ \text{kg m}^{-2} = 60\) L kg⁻¹; WP \(= 5\ \text{kg}/0.300\ \text{m}^3 = 16.7\) kg m⁻³.
  2. Greenhouse: PWU \(= 1000/50 = 20\) to \(1000/80 = 12.5\) L kg⁻¹; WP \(= 50\) to 80 kg m⁻³.
  3. Decompose: per square metre the greenhouse uses \(1000/300 = 3.3\) times more water, but it produces \(50/5 = 10\) to 16 times more fruit. The yield — the denominator — explains the three- to fivefold advantage.
  4. Spain: the unheated Spanish "parral" uses 40 L kg⁻¹ (27 L kg⁻¹ with better-regulated ventilation): between the field and the Dutch glasshouse, mostly because the crop is shorter and yields less per m², while transpiration in the sunny south is high.
  5. Sanity check: the global average water footprint of tomatoes is 214 L kg⁻¹ (108 green, 63 blue, 43 grey; Mekonnen & Hoekstra, 2011) — the order of magnitude of the open-field values, as it should be, because most tomatoes are field-grown.
Field: 60 L kg⁻¹ (17 kg m⁻³); greenhouse: 12.5–20 L kg⁻¹ (50–80 kg m⁻³). The greenhouse uses more water per m² but far more yield per m² makes it 3–5 times more water-productive.

Nederhoff and Stanghellini (2010) describe the route from field to closed greenhouse as a ladder of water savings. In the field, the first rungs are a better soil (organic matter, mulch) and drip irrigation with a dripper at every plant, which cut deep drainage and the evaporation from wet soil. Moving the crop into a greenhouse is the largest single step: the yield per square metre rises thanks to controlled conditions, CO₂ and a longer season, while transpiration per square metre falls because the cover reduces the radiation and the inside air is more humid. Growing on substrate with a closed, recirculating nutrient solution saves another 10–50 % of the water compared with free drainage, in which 5–50 % of the supply leaves as drain. The top rung is to keep the vents shut and cool with cold water pumped from deep aquifers: in a Dutch experiment with a closed greenhouse about 40–50 % of the water supplied to the crop was recovered by condensation, giving the record of 4 L kg⁻¹, and a closed-greenhouse project in Spain recovered 75 % of its irrigation water. The ladder can also be climbed downwards. In hot, arid regions pad-and-fan and fog systems evaporate so much water for cooling that anecdotal data from Mexico suggest more than 60, or even 90, litres per kilogram of tomatoes.

Where does the water go? Figure 2 compares the pathways. In the open field much of the irrigation water drains below the root zone or evaporates from the soil. In a greenhouse nearly all water leaves as transpired vapour through the vents; the drain can be collected and reused. A plant factory closes the last loop: the vapour condenses on the cooling coil of the air-conditioner and is returned to the nutrient tank (Lesson 8.2).

Kozai (2013) defined a water-use efficiency for closed systems that is an efficiency ratio in the strict sense: the water that stays in the system (fixed in the plants or recovered as condensate) divided by the water supplied. It reaches 0.95–0.98 in plant factories against 0.02–0.03 in greenhouses with open vents.

System water balance and water-use efficiency of a closed system(8.4.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} W_S = W_T + W_P + W_D, \qquad W_T = W_C + W_L \\[6pt] \text{WUE}_{\text{sys}} = \frac{W_C + W_P}{W_S}, \qquad \text{PWU} = \frac{W_S - W_C}{Y} \end{gathered} \]

the water supplied to the crop is transpired, held in the growing plants or drained; transpired water is either recovered as condensate or lost to the outside. The system water-use efficiency is the recovered water plus the water held in the plants, divided by the water supplied. The fresh water that must be bought per kilogram is the supply minus the recovered condensate.

  1. Mass conservation for the liquid water entering the root zone: it is either taken up (transpired, \(W_T\), or incorporated, \(W_P\)) or leaves as drain \(W_D\).
  2. The transpired vapour is either condensed on the cooling coil and collected (\(W_C\)) or leaves with leakage air (\(W_L = N\,V\,(X_{\text{in}} - X_{\text{out}})\) in Kozai's notation — the same ventilation term as in Lessons 8.2 and 8.3).

Steady operation over the averaging period; storage changes in the tank neglected. The two indicators answer different questions: WUE\(_{\text{sys}}\) says how well the system keeps its water; PWU says how much must be bought per kilogram.

Common misconception

"95 % less water means 95 % more sustainable." Water saved by a plant factory is paid for with electricity: the air-conditioner that condenses the water also removes the heat of the lamps, and the lamps are needed because there is no sun. Where water is scarce and electricity is clean, that trade is excellent; in rainy Sweden, where field lettuce grows mostly on green (rain) water, it buys much less. An indicator that looks at one resource cannot tell you which trade is better — you need all of them, and the local scarcity of each (Graamans et al., 2018).

Energy: kilowatt-hours per kilogram

For greenhouses and plant factories, energy is the resource that dominates cost and carbon footprint (Lesson 8.1). The indicator is simple — energy divided by yield — but three questions must be answered before any comparison makes sense: which energy (purchased electricity and fuel, or total energy including sunlight, or primary energy at the power station), which boundary (farm gate or including packaging, cold chain and transport) and which product basis (fresh or dry weight).

Specific energy use and energy-use efficiency(8.4.4)
\[ e = \frac{\sum_i E_i}{Y}\ \ \left(\text{kWh kg}^{-1}\right), \qquad \text{EUE} = \frac{1}{e}\ \ \left(\text{kg kWh}^{-1}\right), \qquad e_{\text{MJ}} = 3.6\,e \]

the specific energy use is the sum of all energy inputs within the boundary divided by the product; the energy-use efficiency is its inverse. One kilowatt-hour is 3.6 megajoules.

Follow the reasoning: purchased, total and primary energy
  1. Purchased energy is what appears on the bills: electricity, gas, wood chips, district heat. Graamans et al. (2018) found 247 kWh of electricity per kg of lettuce dry weight in a plant factory against 70 (Netherlands), 111 (United Arab Emirates) and 182–211 kWh kg⁻¹ DW (Kiruna, Sweden, with and without supplementary lighting) in greenhouses.
  2. Total energy adds the free solar energy that greenhouses use. On that basis the same study found the plant factory more efficient: 1411 MJ kg⁻¹ DW against 1699 MJ kg⁻¹ DW for the best greenhouse (Sweden with lighting).
  3. Primary energy counts the fuel burned at the power station to make the electricity; it penalises electricity relative to fuel by a primary-energy factor that depends on the grid.

A specific energy use says nothing about emissions until the energy is multiplied by emission factors (Eq. 8.4.9), and nothing about cost until it is multiplied by prices. Comparing 1 kWh of electricity with 1 kWh of heat is legitimate for energy bookkeeping but not for value: electricity can be turned into several kWh of heat with a heat pump (Lesson 8.1).

Why does a plant factory buy more energy than a greenhouse — even one north of the Arctic Circle? A greenhouse receives its photons for free and pays mainly for heat; a plant factory makes every photon from electricity and then pays a second time to remove the same energy as heat (Lesson 8.1). The greenhouses modelled by Graamans et al. (2018) show how strongly their energy use depends on climate: 70 kWh kg⁻¹ DW in the Netherlands, 111 in the United Arab Emirates, where the greenhouse must be cooled, and 182–211 in northern Sweden, where heating and, with supplementary lighting, electricity carry the crop through the long dark winter. An insulated, closed building, by contrast, is largely decoupled from the outside climate: its energy use is governed by technology — lamp efficacy, light-use efficiency and the COP of the cooling — rather than by location. That is the crux of every comparison between the two systems. Improve the technology and the plant factory improves everywhere; improve the greenhouse and the gain depends on how much sun and how cold a winter the site has.

Light: grams per mole of photons

In a plant factory every photon is bought, so the most important biological indicator is the light-use efficiency (LUE): biomass produced per mole of photons received. It links the physics of Module 4 (photons per joule of electricity) to the biology of Module 2 (biomass per photon), and it explains most of the differences in kWh per kilogram between farms. Jin et al. (2023) collected LUE values of lettuce from 53 vertical-farm studies, 13 greenhouse studies and 8 field studies: on average 0.55 g DW mol⁻¹ in vertical farms, 0.39 in greenhouses and 0.23 in the field, with a maximum measured value of 1.63 g DW mol⁻¹ against a theoretical maximum of 1.26–1.81 g DW mol⁻¹.

Light-use efficiency(8.4.5)
\[ \text{LUE}_{\text{DW}} = \frac{\Delta W_{\text{DW}}}{\sum_{\text{days}}\text{DLI}}, \qquad \text{LUE}_{\text{FW}} = \frac{\text{LUE}_{\text{DW}}}{d}, \qquad \text{LUE}_{E} = \frac{f\,\Delta W_{\text{DW}}}{E_{\text{PAR}}} \]

the light-use efficiency is the dry (or fresh) biomass produced per square metre divided by the photons received per square metre over the same period, the sum of the daily light integrals. Kozai's energy-based version divides the chemical energy stored in the dry matter by the PAR energy received.

The light-use efficiency is not a constant of the crop; four factors dominate it. Interception: young plants cover only part of the growing area, and photons that fall between them are wasted, which is why many growers re-space their plants as they grow; a closed canopy captures most of the light. Intensity: photosynthesis saturates at high photon flux (Lesson 2.1), so every extra photon produces less — Carotti et al. (2021) measured 1.01 g DW mol⁻¹ at 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ but only 0.76 at 750. CO₂: enrichment raises the yield of every photon; in a plant factory, Chen et al. (2021) found a 29 % and 41 % higher LUE at about 800 and 1600 µmol mol⁻¹ than at ambient CO₂. Temperature: air and root-zone temperature drive leaf expansion and therefore interception; Carotti et al. (2021) obtained their highest efficiency at 24 °C air and 28 °C root-zone temperature. Because all four can be controlled indoors, Jin et al. (2023) concluded that vertical farms can in principle approach the theoretical maximum — the gap between today's average of 0.55 g DW mol⁻¹ and 1.3–1.8 is the largest efficiency reserve in the whole system.

Land: kilograms per square metre — but which square metre?

Land productivity is where vertical farms shine. Pennisi et al. (2025) found average lettuce yields of 60 kg FW per m² of cultivated area per year in scientific studies and 105 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ in company data, against 65 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ in Dutch high-tech greenhouses with supplementary light, 34 without, and a few kg in the open field. Stacking multiplies these numbers again: a large Swedish vertical farm grows about 5900 m² of lettuce on 1670 m² of floor, a land-use index of 3.5 m² m⁻², reaching about 311 kg FW per m² of floor per year, and a survey of 41 vertical farms found a land-use index of 3.05 and about 350 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (both as reported by Pennisi et al., 2025). But the electricity of a vertical farm also occupies land somewhere — in a hydropower reservoir, a wind farm or a solar park — and a fair comparison with a field that runs on sunlight must say whether that land is included.

Worked example 5 is not an argument against vertical farming; it is an argument for stating the question. If the question is how to grow salad on scarce urban land, the floor area is the right denominator, and the farm of the example produces on 1670 m² of floor about 350 t of lettuce a year (212 kg m⁻² × 1670 m²) — as much as some 9 ha of irrigated field would at the Arizona yield of Barbosa et al. (2015). If the question is how much of the planet's land a diet occupies, then the land behind the electricity counts too — and it depends entirely on the source. Nutrients: from fertiliser to harvest

Nutrient-use efficiency matters for cost and even more for the environment, because nitrogen and phosphorus that do not end up in the crop end up in groundwater, rivers and the Baltic Sea (Lesson 1.2). Two indicators are standard: the partial factor productivity (kg of product per kg of nutrient supplied) and the recovery efficiency (nutrient in the harvested crop per nutrient supplied), which is an efficiency ratio like Kozai's water and CO₂ efficiencies (his FUE; Kozai, 2013).

The nutrient balance says where the rest went: into the drain, into the solution left in the tank, or elsewhere (roots, substrate, gaseous losses).

Recirculation makes the nutrient indicators of soilless systems look excellent, but two caveats remain. First, fertiliser has a footprint of its own: nitrogen fertilisers are made from ammonia produced by the energy-intensive Haber–Bosch process, so every gram of nitrogen that is wasted also wastes the energy and emissions of its manufacture, which belong in the life-cycle inventory (Lesson 1.3). Second, even a closed system must now and then bleed or dump solution when sodium or unused ions accumulate (Nederhoff & Stanghellini, 2010), and that discharge is concentrated; collecting it as fertiliser for field crops, or treating it, closes the loop. Aquaponics goes one step further and feeds the plants from fish waste, which calls for a nitrogen balance of the whole ecosystem (Lesson 6.1).

Where the emissions come from differs completely between systems, and so do the levers. In the open field the main sources are the manufacture of nitrogen fertiliser, nitrous oxide from the soil, machinery and — often dominant — transport: Pennisi et al. The functional-unit problem

Every intensity divides by a functional unit, and the choice of unit is a value judgement about what the food is for. Lettuce is eaten for texture, taste, vitamins and fibre, not for energy or protein; comparing lettuce with potatoes per kilocalorie is therefore unfair to lettuce — but comparing them per kilogram is unfair to potatoes, which feed people. Within one crop, the choice between fresh and dry weight is equally consequential, as Worked example 3 showed. The most common functional-unit traps are:

  • Fresh versus dry weight. Lettuce dry matter varies between 2.6 and 4.2 % with light intensity alone (Carotti et al., 2021); a fresh-weight indicator rewards water in the product.
  • Per kilogram versus per head. Lettuce is often sold per head; a farm growing 250 g heads in fewer days may have a worse kWh per kg than one growing 350 g heads, but a better kWh per head.
  • Marketable versus total biomass. Roots, trimmed leaves and unsold product consumed resources too; they are outside the functional unit, which raises the intensity per kg sold (Lesson 15.1 adds waste after the farm gate).
  • Per cultivated area versus per floor area versus per total land (Eq. 8.4.7).
  • Per crop cycle versus per year. In a Dutch greenhouse with supplementary lighting, December yields are only about 55 % of those in May (Raaphorst & van Tuyll, 2023, as cited by Pennisi et al., 2025); annual averages hide seasonal gaps that must be filled by storage or imports.
  • Per kilogram versus per nutrient. Per 1000 kcal or per 100 g of protein, the ranking of foods changes completely (Figure 4).

Three robust patterns stand out. Land and water improve by one to two orders of magnitude from the field to the vertical farm. Purchased energy worsens by one to two orders of magnitude in the same direction, because free sunlight is replaced by electricity. Carbon follows energy unless the electricity is clean: a vertical farm in Sweden can compete with lettuce imported from distant greenhouses (Pennisi et al., 2025, estimate a break-even transport distance of roughly 1100 km for Sweden; Lesson 15.1 treats such break-even calculations), whereas the same farm on a fossil-heavy grid cannot. Which system is "most efficient" therefore depends on which resource is scarce where the food is produced — the conclusion of Graamans et al. (2018): "the local scarcity of resources determines the suitability of production systems".

Seen through that lens, the three European greenhouse regions of this lesson face different scarcities. In Spain, unheated plastic "parral" greenhouses harvest abundant sunshine with little purchased energy; water is the scarce resource, and it is there that the step from 40 to 27 L kg⁻¹ of tomatoes through better-regulated ventilation (Table 2) matters most. How fertiliser is managed matters too: open-field lettuce grown with generous nitrogen (13 g N per kg, reported for Spain) had more than twice the carbon footprint of a lean American regime (3 g N per kg): 0.18 against 0.08 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ (Casey et al., 2022, as cited by Pennisi et al., 2025). In the Netherlands, high-tech glasshouses reach the highest yields per square metre and, with closed hydroponics, 15 L kg⁻¹ of tomatoes, but they heat through a long winter and their electricity is comparatively carbon-intensive. In Sweden, field lettuce grows in summer largely on rain, winter production in greenhouses needs heating and lighting — the Kiruna greenhouse of Graamans et al. (2018) needed two and a half to three times the purchased energy of the Dutch one — but the electricity is among the cleanest in Europe. That combination explains why Swedish vertical farms reach 0.74–0.78 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹, far below the review average for vertical farms, and why the same farm design would perform very differently elsewhere.

Sensitivity to assumptions

Most indicators in this lesson are products and quotients of a few inputs, so their sensitivity to each input can be read off directly. The elasticity (or sensitivity coefficient) of an indicator with respect to an input is the relative change in the indicator per relative change in the input. For the energy chain of Eq. 8.4.6, a 10 % better photon efficacy or light-use efficiency lowers the kWh per kg by about 10 %; a 10 % higher dry-matter content raises the kWh per kg FW by 10 %; the COP matters less because only the HVAC share depends on it. The same coefficients tell you how measurement uncertainties propagate.

Elasticity and uncertainty of a composite indicator(8.4.10)
\[ S_{x} = \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}\,\frac{x}{I}, \qquad I = c\prod_i x_i^{a_i} \;\Rightarrow\; S_{x_i} = a_i, \qquad \left(\frac{u_I}{I}\right)^{2} \approx \sum_i \left(S_{x_i}\,\frac{u_{x_i}}{x_i}\right)^{2} \]

the elasticity of an indicator with respect to an input is its partial derivative times the ratio of input to indicator — the percentage change of the indicator per percentage change of the input. For an indicator that is a product of powers of its inputs, the elasticities are simply the exponents. Independent relative uncertainties of the inputs, weighted by their elasticities, add in quadrature to give the relative uncertainty of the indicator.

Follow the derivation
  1. Take logarithms of \(I = c\prod x_i^{a_i}\): \(\ln I = \ln c + \sum a_i\ln x_i\).
  2. Differentiate: \(\mathrm{d}I/I = \sum a_i\,\mathrm{d}x_i/x_i\) — relative changes add, weighted by the exponents. Hence \(S_{x_i} = \partial\ln I/\partial\ln x_i = a_i\).
  3. For small, independent random errors, the variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances (the first-order law of propagation of uncertainty; JCGM, 2008): \((u_I/I)^2 = \sum (a_i\,u_{x_i}/x_i)^2\).
  4. For inputs that enter as sums, such as \((1 + 1/\text{COP})\), the elasticity is smaller than one: \(S_{\text{COP}} = -(1/\text{COP})/(1 + 1/\text{COP})\) = −0.2 at COP = 4.
Assumptions and validity

First-order (linear) propagation is accurate when relative uncertainties are small (below about 20 %); correlated inputs need covariance terms; systematic errors (a biased meter) do not average out. For large, non-linear uncertainties use Monte Carlo simulation (Lesson 15.1) and see Lesson 9.3 for the full treatment.

Worked example 9How precise is your kWh per kilogram?

A student measures the electricity of a small system with a plug-in meter (relative standard uncertainty 2 %), weighs the harvest on a kitchen scale (1 %) and estimates the dry-matter content by oven-drying three small leaf samples (10 %). What is the relative uncertainty of kWh per kg FW and of kWh per kg DW?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Per kg FW: \(I = E/Y\), elasticities +1 and −1: \(u_I/I = \sqrt{0.02^2 + 0.01^2} = 0.022\) = 2.2 %.
  2. Per kg DW: \(I = E/(Y\,d)\): \(\sqrt{0.02^2 + 0.01^2 + 0.10^2} = 0.102\) = 10.2 %.
  3. Interpretation: the dry-matter estimate dominates. Dry larger samples to constant weight, replicate, and report the dry-matter content with its uncertainty — or report both bases.
≈ 2.2 % per kg FW; ≈ 10 % per kg DW (dominated by the dry-matter content)

Elasticities describe small changes around one operating point. Two further tools complete the sensitivity analysis. A scenario analysis recalculates the indicator for a few coherent sets of assumptions — for example "today's LEDs on the Dutch grid" against "best-available LEDs on the Swedish grid" — and is the honest way to present choices that are not small perturbations, such as the electricity source. A break-even analysis asks the inverse question: how large may an input become before the ranking flips? Lesson 15.1 derives the grid emission factor below which a vertical farm beats imported lettuce; the same algebra gives the photon efficacy a farm needs to match a greenhouse's kWh per kilogram. When many inputs are uncertain at once, Monte Carlo simulation — drawing all inputs from their distributions thousands of times — turns a single number into a distribution, and a ranking into a probability. A ranking that survives all three tests is robust; one that flips within the plausible range of an assumption should be reported as uncertain, whatever the headline says.

Research frontier

Stanghellini and Katzin (2024) estimated a theoretical energy-use efficiency of about 0.30 kg FW kWh⁻¹ for vertical farms — two to four times today's typical 0.08–0.13 (Pennisi et al., 2025). Closing the gap is the research agenda of 2024–2026: LEDs approaching their physical limits (Lesson 4.3), canopies that intercept almost every photon, cultivars bred for high light-use efficiency, heat recovery into district heating, and dynamic lighting that follows electricity prices and grid emission factors hour by hour.

A template for your own system

Your course project is a small production system (project overview). Its indicators will not match a commercial farm — small systems waste light at the edges, run pumps continuously and grow sparse canopies — but calculating them properly is the best way to understand what the published numbers mean. Follow the six steps and use the calculator in Figure 6.

  1. Define the system and the functional unit. Draw the boundary (Figure 1): does it include the germination tray, the nutrient tank, the room heating? Choose the functional unit (kg of marketable fresh shoots is the default) and the period (from transplanting to harvest).
  2. Measure the inputs. Record them in your grow log (grow-log tool) as you go — afterwards it is too late.
  3. Measure the outputs. Weigh the marketable shoots immediately after harvest; oven-dry a representative sample for the dry-matter content.
  4. Calculate the indicators with Eqs. 8.4.1–8.4.9, keeping every unit.
  5. Estimate the uncertainty of the most important indicators with Eq. 8.4.10.
  6. Compare and report against Table 3, stating the basis, boundary and period of every number.

Small systems have their own traps, and knowing them in advance saves a crop cycle. Edges: on a 0.5 m² shelf a large share of the light falls outside the canopy or on the walls, so both PPFD and LUE depend on where you measure — map the PPFD on a grid and use the mean over the cultivated area. Hidden consumers: the plug-in meter must sit upstream of everything that belongs to the system — lamps, pump, air pump, fan, chiller, heater — or the kWh per kg will look too good. Water: count the initial fill of the tank, every top-up and every litre discarded when the solution is renewed; the water left in the tank at harvest can be subtracted if it is reused. Weighing: leaves lose water within minutes after cutting, so weigh immediately and always the same way (roots removed, no free water on the leaves). Time: a 35-day cycle becomes an annual yield only if the shelf is replanted at once; nursery days, empty days and cleaning belong to the year. None of these is exotic — they are exactly the choices that make published indicators differ.

Key terms

Resource-use efficiency
Umbrella term for indicators relating a product to the resources used to make it; productivity, intensity or efficiency-ratio form.
Productivity
Product per unit of resource (kg m⁻³, kg kWh⁻¹, g mol⁻¹, kg m⁻² yr⁻¹); often called "use efficiency".
Intensity
Resource per unit of product (L kg⁻¹, kWh kg⁻¹, kg CO₂e kg⁻¹); the inverse of productivity.
Efficiency ratio
Fraction of a supplied resource that is fixed or retained (water, CO₂, nutrients, energy); dimensionless and at most 1 (Kozai, 2013).
Functional unit
The reference quantity of product to which all inputs and outputs are related, e.g. 1 kg of marketable fresh lettuce at the farm gate.
Product water use (PWU)
Water brought into the system per kilogram of product (L kg⁻¹).
Water footprint
Consumptive use of rain (green) and irrigation (blue) water plus the water needed to dilute pollution (grey), per unit of product.
Specific energy use
Energy input per kilogram of product (kWh kg⁻¹ or MJ kg⁻¹); its inverse is the energy-use efficiency (kg kWh⁻¹).
Purchased versus total energy
Energy bought as electricity and fuel, versus all energy used including free solar radiation.
Light-use efficiency (LUE)
Biomass produced per mole of photons received (g DW mol⁻¹ or g FW mol⁻¹).
Land-use index
Cultivated area per floor area of a multi-tier farm (m² m⁻²).
Partial factor productivity
Product per kilogram of nutrient supplied (kg kg⁻¹ N).
Nitrogen recovery efficiency
Nitrogen in the harvested product divided by nitrogen supplied.
Carbon footprint
Life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions per functional unit, in kg CO₂e.
Elasticity
Relative change of an indicator per relative change of an input, \(S_x = (\partial I/\partial x)(x/I)\).
