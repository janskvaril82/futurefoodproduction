Global regulatory landscapes for new foods

On 19 December 2020 a restaurant called 1880 in Singapore served the first cultivated chicken ever sold to paying customers. By 2026 the same class of product had been authorised in Singapore, the United States, Israel and Australia–New Zealand, was sold as dog treats in Britain, was under assessment in the European Union, Switzerland and the United Kingdom — and was prohibited in Italy and banned or placed under moratorium in eight US states. One product can be legal, pending and unlawful at the same time, depending on which border it crosses. This lesson compares how the world's regulators decide which new foods reach the plate: who decides, how long it takes, what international trade law allows, and why governments increasingly compete — and sometimes collide — over the rules.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare the architecture of new-food regulation in Singapore, the United States, the United Kingdom, Israel, Australia–New Zealand, Switzerland and China: what triggers pre-market review, who decides, and what the outcomes have been (LO6).
  • Trace the US pathways for new ingredients (food additive petition, GRAS notice, self-affirmed GRAS) and for cultivated meat (FDA pre-market consultation followed by USDA-FSIS inspection and labelling), and explain the 2025–2026 moves to make GRAS notification mandatory (LO6).
  • Explain how Codex Alimentarius and the WTO SPS and TBT Agreements constrain national food rules, and analyse bans such as Italy's Law 172/2023 and US state laws against them (LO6).
  • Calculate review clocks, times to authorisation, capped fines and the expected value of alternative launch strategies across jurisdictions (LO6, LO3).
  • Evaluate how regulatory divergence and competition between jurisdictions shape where, how and in what form new food technologies reach consumers first (LO6, LO1).

One food, many rulebooks

In Lesson 14.1 you followed a new food through one legal system, the European Union's novel food regulation. Every jurisdiction in the world must answer the same two questions about a food that has not been eaten before: does it need permission before it is sold, and who grants that permission? The answers differ strikingly. The cultivated chicken that Singapore's food agency cleared in November 2020 is technologically close to the products for which the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) completed its safety consultations in November 2022 and March 2023 and that the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) placed under inspection in June 2023 — yet since 1 July 2024 such products may not be sold in Florida. In the European Union no cultivated meat had been authorised by September 2026, and Italy had banned it outright in December 2023. In Great Britain cultivated chicken can be sold as a pet treat but, as of 2026, not as human food.

Four design choices explain most of these differences, and you can use them as a checklist whenever you meet a new regulatory system:

  1. The trigger. What makes a food need pre-market review? The EU uses a historical test (no significant consumption in the Union before 15 May 1997) plus ten categories. Singapore asks whether a substance has been "used to a significant degree as food for a period of at least 20 years" anywhere in the world, or whether the food is made by a process not used in food production for 20 years. Australia and New Zealand require approval of "novel foods", non-traditional foods that need a safety assessment. The United States has no general novel-food category at all: every substance added to food is a food additive requiring approval unless it is "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS), while meat and poultry are inspected by USDA whatever their origin.
  2. The gatekeeper. An expert agency may decide alone (Singapore's SFA, Israel's Ministry of Health, the FDA for GRAS notices), a scientific agency may advise a political decision-maker (the European Commission and Member States in comitology; ministers in Great Britain; the FSANZ Board, whose decisions food ministers may send back for review), or a legislature may take the decision itself — as in the bans of Section 14.3.7.
  3. The strength of the gate. Pre-market authorisation may be mandatory (EU, Singapore, Australia–New Zealand, Great Britain), a voluntary notification to the regulator (a US GRAS notice), or a firm's own determination without any notification ("self-affirmed" GRAS).
  4. The reach of a decision. A decision may be generic, open to any producer who meets the specification (the EU Union list; a schedule in the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code), or tied to a named firm and its described process (Singapore's published approvals list the applicant, cell line and production process; an FDA response letter answers one firm's notice).
Big idea

For the products assessed so far, independent scientific assessments — by Singapore's SFA, the FDA, Israel's Ministry of Health and FSANZ — have reached similar conclusions about the safety of cultivated meat. Differences in market access arise mainly from legal triggers, procedures and political choices, not from disagreement about hazards. Regulation is where science meets sovereignty.

Singapore: the first mover

Singapore is a city-state of about six million people that imports most of its food. That dependence explains its interest in new production technologies — and its willingness to regulate them early. In 2019 the government set the goal of producing 30 % of the country's nutritional needs locally by 2030 ("30 by 30"). In November 2025 it replaced that goal with more modest, better-defined targets under Singapore Food Story 2: by 2035 local farms should supply 20 % of the fibre (vegetables, bean sprouts and mushrooms) and 30 % of the protein (eggs and seafood) that Singapore consumes, from 2024 baselines of 8 % and 26 % (Ministry of Sustainability and the Environment, 2025). The revision is itself a lesson for this course: high-tech local production proved more expensive and slower to scale than hoped.

The novel food framework was introduced in 2019. The SFA defines a novel food as a substance "that has not been used to a significant degree as food for a period of at least 20 years", whether within or outside Singapore, or a food made by a process not previously used in food production for at least 20 years; materials of mineral origin and foods containing engineered nanomaterials are also covered. Applicants submit a safety assessment that identifies the risks and shows how they are managed; the SFA is advised by a Novel Food Safety Expert Working Group of 13 members from food science, nutrition, microbiology, toxicology, bioinformatics and paediatrics, and offers pre-submission "virtual clinics" (Singapore Food Agency, 2026b). The legal basis was strengthened by the Food Safety and Security Act 2025, passed on 8 January 2025. From 28 November 2025 it is an offence to supply a novel or genetically modified food without pre-market approval, or an insect-like species that is not in the SFA's catalogue.

Singapore also publishes more than most regulators. Its List of approved novel foods gives, for every approval, the decision date, the applicant, the production strain or cell line, any genetic modification, the growth media, the manufacturing process with its critical control points, and the product specification (Singapore Food Agency, 2026a). The list is worth reading as an engineer. Eat Just's first approval, for example, concerns chicken cells derived from the fibroblast line UMNSAH/DF1 grown in a DMEM/F12 basal medium supplemented with foetal bovine serum; the specification requires 85–95 % moisture and 5–10 % protein. Vow's quail cells are expanded in stirred-tank bioreactors of 200–20,000 L and harvested at a density of at least 30 g L⁻¹, so one full 20,000 L batch yields at least \(20\,000\ \text{L}\times0.030\ \text{kg L}^{-1} = 600\) kg of cell biomass.

Two features of the Singapore model deserve emphasis. First, approvals are specific: the list describes each applicant's cell line, medium and process, and a change such as removing serum required a new approval in 2023. Second, approval is not availability. Commercial volumes of cultivated meat have remained small everywhere; most companies have launched through a handful of restaurants (FoodNavigator, 2026). Singapore's early and predictable procedure has nevertheless made it a showcase: companies from the United States, Australia and France chose it as their first market, and Israel's Aleph Farms as its first market abroad.

Decision date or announcement date?

Regulators and companies often report different dates. The SFA list gives 26 November 2020 as the decision date for Eat Just's chicken; the public announcement came on 2 December and the first sale on 19 December. Aleph Farms' approval is dated 22 July 2026 in the list and was announced on 4 August 2026. When you compute durations, state which date you use.

United States: GRAS, consultations and two agencies

Food additives and the GRAS exemption

US food law starts from the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) of 1938, amended by the Food Additives Amendment of 1958. A "food additive" is, broadly, any substance whose intended use results in it becoming a component of food; it needs pre-market approval by the FDA through a food additive petition, which ends in a regulation listing the permitted uses. The 1958 amendment exempted substances "generally recognized, among experts qualified by scientific training and experience to evaluate its safety, as having been adequately shown … to be safe" — the GRAS exemption. General recognition can rest on scientific procedures, with the evidence publicly available, or on experience from common use in food before 1 January 1958.

Who decides that a substance is GRAS? Here the US system differs fundamentally from the EU. Since the late 1990s — formalised by a final rule in 2016 (FDA, 2016) — a company may send a GRAS notice to the FDA. The FDA evaluates it and answers with a letter: it has "no questions" about the notifier's conclusion, it finds an "insufficient basis" for it, or it has "ceased to evaluate" the notice at the notifier's request. But the notice is voluntary. A company may also reach its own GRAS conclusion with an expert panel and market the substance without telling the FDA — so-called self-affirmed GRAS. Many precision-fermentation ingredients and microbial biomasses have entered the US market as GRAS substances, because a GRAS notice is usually faster and cheaper than a food additive petition.

Critics have long argued that the voluntary route leaves the regulator ignorant of what is in the food supply. A comprehensive review found that of about 6,000 safety decisions that allow more than 10,000 substances in US food, federal agencies made only about 40 %; the rest were GRAS conclusions by manufacturers or a trade association without FDA review (Neltner et al., 2011). On 10 March 2025 the Secretary of Health and Human Services directed the FDA to explore rulemaking to eliminate the self-affirmed GRAS pathway, and on 11 August 2026 the FDA published a proposed rule that would make GRAS notification mandatory for human and animal food (FDA, 2026; Docket FDA-2025-N-3262). Its main elements are:

  • a duty to notify every substance marketed under the GRAS provision, with exemptions — among them substances that already have a "no questions" letter for the use, substances listed or affirmed in 21 CFR parts 182, 184 or 186, and uses covered by a threshold-of-regulation exemption or an effective food contact notification;
  • a one-year window for streamlined submissions for substances already in interstate commerce before the rule takes effect, without the full supporting data;
  • a 45-day pre-filing evaluation, a 180-day review after filing and up to two 90-day extensions (the 2016 rule allows one);
  • entry into force 60 days after publication of the final rule, and compliance 18 months after that. Comments are open until 9 December 2026.

The proposal also reports the record of the voluntary programme: of 1,215 filed GRAS notices that had been resolved, 231 ended with the FDA ceasing to evaluate them at the notifier's request. As of September 2026 the rule was only proposed: self-affirmed GRAS remained lawful.

Cultivated meat: two agencies, one product

Cultivated meat does not fit neatly into either the FDA's world (most foods) or the USDA's (meat, poultry and some eggs and catfish, under the Federal Meat Inspection Act and the Poultry Products Inspection Act). On 7 March 2019 the two agencies signed a formal agreement on food made from cultured cells of livestock and poultry. The FDA oversees cell collection, cell banks and cell growth and differentiation; at the harvest stage jurisdiction passes to USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS), which oversees processing, packaging and labelling, and inspects the establishment. Cultivated seafood is overseen by the FDA alone, from cell bank to label.

The FDA's part takes the form of a pre-market consultation. The firm submits its safety evaluation; the FDA examines "the firm's production process and the cultured cell material", including the establishment of the cell lines and cell banks, manufacturing controls and all components and inputs. At the end the FDA writes that it has "no further questions at this time" about the firm's safety conclusion — explicitly not an approval — and publishes the firm's safety narrative, its response and its scientific memo (FDA, 2022). The firm then needs an FSIS grant of inspection for the establishment and a mark of inspection on the food, which requires an approved label.

Federal clearance does not guarantee a national market. From 2024 several states used their police powers to prohibit cultivated meat; Section 14.3.7 analyses these laws and the court cases against them. The US therefore illustrates a principle that recurs in this lesson: in a federal system, who regulates can matter as much as what the rules say.

Common misconception

"The FDA approved lab-grown chicken in 2022." The FDA completed a voluntary pre-market consultation and said it had no further questions about the firm's safety conclusion. Legal permission to sell came from USDA-FSIS inspection and label approval. For regulators, the difference between "no questions" and "approval" matters: responsibility for safety stays with the firm.

Labels are the last step. FSIS approved the term "cell-cultivated chicken" for UPSIDE Foods in 2023. A US experiment with 4,385 consumers tested five candidate terms on beef, chicken and salmon packages: "cultured" and "cultivated" failed to distinguish the novel products from wild-caught and farm-raised salmon, whereas "cell-cultured", "cell-cultivated" and "cell-based" met both regulatory criteria — signalling that the product differs from conventional meat and that allergens may be present — with a slight consumer-acceptance advantage for "cell-cultured" (Hallman et al., 2023). Naming rules in the EU are covered in Lesson 14.2.

United Kingdom: divergence after Brexit

When the Brexit transition period ended on 31 December 2020, Great Britain kept the EU's novel food regulation as retained — since 2024 "assimilated" — law, with the same 15 May 1997 cut-off. Northern Ireland continues to apply EU rules under the Windsor Framework. What changed is the machinery: the Food Standards Agency (FSA, for England and Wales) and Food Standards Scotland (FSS) run the risk assessment for Great Britain, advised by the Advisory Committee on Novel Foods and Processes (ACNFP), and ministers in England, Wales and Scotland decide. From 1 April 2025 a statutory instrument implemented two reforms for regulated products, including novel foods: authorisations take effect after the ministerial decision and are published in an official register instead of being written into a new statutory instrument each time, and the ten-yearly renewal of feed additive, GMO and smoke-flavouring authorisations was abolished (FSA, 2025).

Britain has used its regulatory autonomy to court new food technologies in three ways:

  • A regulatory sandbox for cell-cultivated products. In October 2024 the FSA and FSS received £1.6 million from the government's Engineering Biology Sandbox Fund. The two-year programme started in March 2025 and runs to February 2027 with eight companies: Hoxton Farms, BlueNalu, Mosa Meat, Gourmey, Roslin Technologies, Uncommon Bio, Vital Meat and Vow. Its targets are to complete safety assessments of two cultivated-meat applications by February 2027 and to reach a typical timeframe of about 2.5 years for a routine, non-complex application. By mid-2026 it had published guidance on tasting trials, classification (cell-cultivated products are treated as products of animal origin), allergens and nutrition, and on cell-line identity, production and microbiology. The average time to the first request for further information on an application fell from 195 days before the sandbox to 90 days, and to 46 days for requests issued in 2026 (FSA, 2026a, 2026b). No cultivated meat had been authorised for human consumption in Great Britain by September 2026.
  • Pet food first. In July 2024 the Animal and Plant Health Agency cleared Meatly's cultivated chicken for use in pet food — the first cultivated-meat approval in Europe — and dog treats containing it went on sale in February 2025.
  • Precision breeding. The Genetic Technology (Precision Breeding) Act 2023 took gene-edited organisms whose changes could have arisen by traditional breeding out of the GMO regime in England. The Genetic Technology (Precision Breeding) Regulations 2025 brought it into force for plants on 13 November 2025, with a two-tier FSA authorisation for food and feed. The first precision bred organism marketing notice — for a Rothamsted barley with higher lipid content, intended as ruminant feed — was published on 13 March 2026, followed by a low-asparagine wheat. The EU's comparable regulation (Lesson 14.2) applies only from 2028.

The divergence may not last. At their summit of May 2025 the UK and the EU agreed to negotiate a sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement based on dynamic alignment of GB rules with EU food law. The FSA told its Board in September 2026 that negotiations were at an advanced stage but not concluded, and that it was planning for implementation around summer 2027. If the agreement comes into force as planned, GB authorisations of regulated products would cease to apply and businesses would need EU authorisations — which is why the fate of England's precision-breeding regime is one of the most-watched questions in the negotiations (FSA, 2026c).

A "divergence dividend" with an expiry date?

Regulatory autonomy only attracts investment if firms believe it will last. Ask yourself: would you build a British cultivated-meat plant on the strength of the sandbox if, from 2027, the product might need an EFSA opinion anyway? Stability is part of regulatory quality.

Israel, Australia–New Zealand, Switzerland, China and beyond

Israel

Israel's Ministry of Health regulates novel foods through its National Food Service under the Public Health Protection (Food) Law of 2015. The ministry approved Remilk's precision-fermented β-lactoglobulin in April 2023, and on 17 January 2024 announced a "no questions" letter for Aleph Farms' cultivated beef — the world's first approval of cultivated beef. The first product, a "Petit Steak", combines non-modified, non-immortalised cells from a Black Angus cow with a plant-protein matrix of soy and wheat (Food Safety News, 2024). Israel thus combines an active food-tech start-up sector with a conventional, product-by-product safety assessment.

Australia and New Zealand

Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) is a bi-national agency that develops the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code; decisions are notified to the Food Ministers' Meeting of the two countries' governments, which may ask for a review. Its assessment of Vow's cultured quail (application A1269) is the most transparent public record of a cultivated-meat approval anywhere (FSANZ, 2025):

  • application received in February 2023 and assessed under the "major procedure";
  • two statutory rounds of public consultation: 40 submissions in the first (completed February 2024) and 22 in the second (12 November 2024 to 12 January 2025);
  • the FSANZ Board approved the variation on 26 March 2025, and the Food Ministers' Meeting was notified on 7 April 2025;
  • no review was requested within the statutory period, and the variation was gazetted on 18 June 2025. It created a standard for cell-cultured foods (Standard 1.5.4), a production and processing standard (Standard 3.4.1) and a schedule listing permitted cell-cultured foods (Schedule 25A); labels must use the term "cell-cultured" or "cell-cultivated".

The assessment concluded that the quail cell line was genetically stable, that microbiological risks required a comprehensive HACCP-based approach — especially during cell expansion — and that there were no toxicological concerns at the estimated consumption levels. Note the combination: the product was assessed, but the Code gained permanent, general standards for all future cell-cultured foods.

Switzerland

Switzerland is outside the EU and has its own Federal Food Act, which entered into force on 1 May 2017 together with a new ordinance on novel foods. That reform made Switzerland the first European country to permit three insect species as food — mealworm larvae, house crickets and migratory locusts — four years before the EU authorised its first insect. Aleph Farms submitted the first European application for cultivated meat to the Federal Food Safety and Veterinary Office in July 2023, and Mosa Meat, supported by the Swiss meat processor Bell Food Group, applied for its cultivated beef fat in 2025. Both applications were still pending in 2026.

China, Japan and Thailand

In China, a food without a history of consumption is a "new food raw material" that needs a safety review by the National Health Commission under the Food Safety Law; licensing and market supervision involve the State Administration for Market Regulation, and agricultural biotechnology the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs. No cultivated meat had been approved by 2026, but the state is investing: in May 2025 the state-owned State Development & Investment Corporation committed more than CNY 4 billion (about US$555 million) to biomanufacturing infrastructure, in which "new proteins" feature prominently (GFI, 2026). Japan introduced a notification system for genome-edited foods without foreign DNA in 2019 and saw the world's first CRISPR-edited food, a tomato with elevated γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), go on sale in 2021 (Waltz, 2022); by early 2026 it had not approved cultivated meat. Thailand has received its first cultivated-meat application, from Aleph Farms.

Codex Alimentarius and the WTO: the international layer

National food rules are embedded in international law. The Codex Alimentarius Commission, created in 1963 by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO), adopts food standards, guidelines and codes of practice. Codex texts are voluntary, but they gained legal weight in 1995, when the World Trade Organization's Agreement on the Application of Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures (SPS Agreement) named Codex as the reference for food safety. For foods from biotechnology, Codex adopted principles for risk analysis (CAC/GL 44-2003) and guidelines for the safety assessment of foods from recombinant-DNA plants (CAC/GL 45-2003) and micro-organisms (CAC/GL 46-2003) — the international basis for assessing precision-fermentation ingredients. There is no Codex standard specific to cell-based food; in 2023 FAO and WHO published a hazard identification for cell-based food, from cell sourcing and culture media to harvesting and processing, as a first step (FAO & WHO, 2023). An industry-wide review reached a similar conclusion: most hazards of cultivated meat are not expected to be novel, so existing assessment methods from foods, biotechnology and pharmaceuticals largely apply (Ong et al., 2021).

The SPS Agreement lets every WTO Member choose its own level of health protection, but it disciplines how measures that protect human, animal or plant health from food-borne risks are justified:

Two disputes show the stakes. In EC — Hormones the Appellate Body found in 1998 that the EU's ban on beef from cattle treated with growth hormones was not based on a risk assessment as required by Article 5.1; the EU kept the ban and faced authorised retaliation by the United States and Canada. In EC — Biotech Products a panel found in 2006 that a de facto moratorium on GMO approvals between 1999 and 2003 had caused "undue delay" contrary to Annex C and Article 8. Labels are usually governed not by the SPS but by the Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT), which requires technical regulations to pursue a legitimate objective — consumer information and the prevention of deceptive practices qualify — without being more trade-restrictive than necessary (Article 2.2).

History — asynchronous approvals

For GM crops, trade friction arose less from outright bans than from asynchrony: a variety approved in the exporting country but not yet in the importing one could block whole shipments if traces were detected. The same could happen to gene-edited crops, which England and Japan treat differently from the EU until 2028, and whose edits often cannot be detected in a laboratory (Eriksson et al., 2019; Lesson 14.2).

Bans and moratoria

Italy: Law 172/2023

In March 2023 the Italian government approved a bill banning cultivated meat. Italy notified the draft to the European Commission under the Technical Regulation Information System (TRIS) — the procedure of Directive (EU) 2015/1535, which requires Member States to notify draft technical regulations and to observe a standstill period so that the Commission and other Member States can comment — but withdrew the notification in October 2023. Parliament adopted the law in November; Law No 172 of 1 December 2023 was published in the Gazzetta Ufficiale the same day and entered into force on 16 December 2023. It does two things:

  • it prohibits food and feed business operators from producing, selling, importing, distributing or otherwise placing on the market food or feed "consisting of, isolated from or produced from cell or tissue cultures derived from vertebrate animals"; and
  • it prohibits the use of meat names for processed products containing plant proteins (compare the Court of Justice's Protéines France judgment of 2024 and the EU's reserved meat terms of 2026 in Lesson 14.2).

Article 5 sets administrative fines of €10,000 to €60,000 — or 10 % of the annual turnover in the last financial year if that is higher — up to a maximum of €150,000, together with confiscation, exclusion from public funding for one to three years and closure of the production plant for the same period. The same sanctions apply to anyone who finances or promotes the prohibited conduct.

Worked example 3Who does a capped fine deter?

Compute the maximum fine under Eq. 14.3.2, in euros and as a percentage of turnover, for (a) a start-up with a turnover of €200,000, (b) a restaurant group with €1.2 million and (c) a meat processor with €50 million. What does the result suggest about the deterrent effect of the law?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(0.10\times200\,000 = 20\,000 < 60\,000\), so \(F_{\max} = 60\,000\) EUR = 30 % of turnover.
  2. (b) \(0.10\times1.2\times10^{6} = 120\,000\), between 60,000 and 150,000: \(F_{\max} = 120\,000\) EUR = 10 %.
  3. (c) \(0.10\times5\times10^{7} = 5\times10^{6} > 150\,000\): the cap binds, \(F_{\max} = 150\,000\) EUR = 0.3 % of turnover.
  4. Interpretation. Relative to size, the fine is regressive: it can be existential for a start-up but minor for a large firm. The non-monetary sanctions — confiscation, closure of the plant and exclusion from public funding for one to three years — are therefore the stronger deterrent for established companies.

Is the law compatible with EU law? The question will become concrete if a cultivated-meat product is added to the Union list. Regulation 2015/2283 harmonises the authorisation of novel foods, and a Union-list authorisation is valid in every Member State; EU food law lets a Member State take interim protective measures against a food that is likely to constitute a serious risk (Article 54 of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002), which is not the stated basis of the Italian law. A national prohibition justified by the protection of agri-food heritage would have to be reconciled with the free movement of goods (Articles 34–36 TFEU). The TRIS history adds a further question, because under the case law of the Court of Justice a technical regulation adopted in breach of the notification and standstill obligations can be unenforceable against individuals (Case C-194/94, CIA Security International, 1996). As of 2026 these issues had not been settled by the Court.

The United States: bans in eight states

In the United States the legal logic is different: states may regulate the sale of food within their borders unless federal law pre-empts them or the state law discriminates against interstate commerce. Between 2024 and 2026 eight states enacted bans or moratoria (Table 5). Their sponsors cited the protection of farmers and ranchers and uncertainty about safety; critics called them protectionism. According to the lawsuit against the Texas law, one of its sponsors said on the House floor that "the goal of this bill is to protect our agriculture industry".

Common misconception

"A ban is the most cautious safety decision." None of the bans in this section was based on a risk assessment of a specific product; several are explicitly temporary or justified by cultural and economic aims. Precaution in the legal sense (SPS Article 5.7; the EU precautionary principle) requires identified scientific uncertainty, provisional measures and a commitment to review — a different thing from a legislative prohibition.

Divergence, competition and strategy

When rules differ, firms and governments react. Regulatory competition describes jurisdictions designing their rules to attract investment — Singapore's early framework, Israel's early approvals, the UK sandbox and FSANZ's permanent standards for cell-cultured foods are all, in part, bids for a new industry. Competition can also run the other way: bans signal support for incumbent producers. Two classic mechanisms describe how divergent rules nevertheless spread. The California effect (Vogel, 1997) is "trading up": exporters adopt the stricter standards of a large, rich market, and then lobby for the same standards at home. The Brussels effect (Bradford, 2020) describes how the EU's large market and its preference for strict, precautionary rules make EU standards the global default when firms find it cheaper to make one product for all markets.

For a company, divergence has three concrete consequences:

  • Product design follows the strictest assessor. Mosa Meat explained that it applied for cultivated beef fat first because EFSA assesses each novel ingredient separately, "unlike for example in Singapore — where entire products are assessed" (Mosa Meat, 2025). Hybrid products that combine cultivated cells with plant proteins, such as Aleph Farms' steaks, reduce the amount of the novel ingredient needed and its cost.
  • Every dossier is expensive. Mosa Meat's EU dossier ran to almost 1,000 pages, based on 450 samples, 652 analytical documents, 23 newly developed analytical methods and 148 parameters per batch, prepared by more than ten full-time employees for nearly two years. Much of this can be reused elsewhere, but not all.
  • Sequencing matters. A first approval in a fast jurisdiction brings revenue, production experience and credibility, which help with investors and later regulators.

Follow the derivation
  1. A margin \(\pi\) earned continuously from \(t = T\) to \(t = T+L\) and discounted at rate \(r\) has present value \(\int_T^{T+L}\pi e^{-rt}\,\mathrm{d}t = \frac{\pi}{r}\left(e^{-rT} - e^{-r(T+L)}\right) = \frac{\pi}{r}e^{-rT}\left(1 - e^{-rL}\right)\).
  2. The margin is earned only if the application succeeds (probability \(p\)); the cost \(C\) is incurred in any case: \(\mathbb{E}[V] = p\cdot\mathrm{PV} - C\).
  3. Delay acts through \(e^{-rT}\): with \(r = 0.10\) a⁻¹, every extra year of procedure costs about 10 % of the value (\(1 - e^{-0.1} = 0.095\)).
Assumptions and validity

Risk-neutral decision-maker; margins constant over the window; no interaction between markets (in reality a first approval can raise \(p\) and lower \(C\) elsewhere); dossier costs paid at the start. The model ignores liquidity: a start-up that runs out of cash before \(T\) earns nothing, which is the "valley of death" of Lesson 14.4.

Reveal the reasoning

Frontier 2026 — convergence or fragmentation?

Watch four developments: whether the FDA's mandatory GRAS notification becomes final; whether EFSA issues the first opinion on a cultivated-meat product, which would test national bans against EU law; whether a UK–EU SPS agreement absorbs Britain's precision-breeding and sandbox regimes into EU rules; and whether regulators move towards reliance — taking account of each other's assessments to raise the reusable share \(s\) of Eq. 14.3.4 without giving up their own decisions.

Key terms

Pre-market approval (Singapore)
Authorisation by the Singapore Food Agency required before a novel or genetically modified food may be supplied; an offence to supply without it since 28 November 2025 (Food Safety and Security Act 2025).
Food additive (US)
Under the FD&C Act, any substance whose intended use results in it becoming a component of food, unless it is GRAS or otherwise excluded; requires approval through a food additive petition.
GRAS
"Generally recognized as safe": the US exemption from the food additive definition for substances whose safety is generally recognised among qualified experts, based on public scientific evidence or common use before 1958.
Self-affirmed GRAS
A GRAS conclusion reached by a company and its expert panel and used to market a substance without notifying the FDA; the target of the 2026 proposed rule.
GRAS notice
A voluntary submission of a GRAS conclusion to the FDA, answered by a letter of "no questions", "insufficient basis" or "ceased to evaluate".
Pre-market consultation (FDA)
The FDA's evaluation of a firm's safety conclusion for food from cultured animal cells, ending with a letter that the agency has no further questions — not an approval.
Grant of inspection
USDA-FSIS permission for an establishment to produce meat or poultry products under federal inspection; required for cultivated meat and poultry after harvest.
Assimilated law
EU law retained in UK law after Brexit (renamed from "retained EU law" in 2024), such as the GB version of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283.
Regulatory sandbox
A time-limited programme in which a regulator works closely with innovators to learn about a new technology and adapt guidance and procedures, without lowering safety standards.
Precision bred organism
Under the UK Precision Breeding Act 2023, an organism whose genetic changes could have resulted from traditional processes; regulated outside the GMO regime in England.
Codex Alimentarius
The collection of international food standards adopted by the FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius Commission (since 1963); the SPS Agreement's reference for food safety.
SPS Agreement
WTO Agreement on the Application of Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures (1995): health measures affecting trade must be science-based, founded on risk assessment or international standards, non-discriminatory and not unnecessarily trade-restrictive.
TBT Agreement
WTO Agreement on Technical Barriers to Trade, which governs technical regulations such as labelling requirements that do not aim at food-borne risks.
TRIS standstill
The obligation under Directive (EU) 2015/1535 to notify draft technical regulations to the Commission and wait before adopting them, so that barriers to the internal market can be discussed.
Brussels effect
The tendency of EU rules to become global standards because firms prefer to comply with the strictest large market everywhere (Bradford, 2020).
