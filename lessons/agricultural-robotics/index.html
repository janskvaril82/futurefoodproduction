Agricultural robotics and automation

At four in the morning a solar-powered robot the size of a small car creeps along a sugar-beet field, hoeing between plants whose exact positions it recorded when it sowed them. In a barn in Västmanland a cow walks into a milking robot because she has decided it is time to be milked. In a Dutch greenhouse a robotic arm reaches for a pepper — and, more often than its designers would like, misses. Robots have been part of dairy farming for three decades but are still rare in orchards and vegetable fields. This lesson asks why: it takes a robot apart into its sensing, thinking and acting parts, puts numbers on machine vision, weeding and harvesting, and examines the rules, costs and social consequences of handing field work to machines.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the anatomy of an agricultural robot — perception, localisation, planning and actuation — and classify systems by their level of autonomy (LO5).
  • Explain machine-vision crop–weed discrimination from colour indices and thresholding to deep learning, and evaluate a detector with precision, recall and F1 (LO5).
  • Compare mechanical, laser and spot-spray weeding and calculate herbicide savings from weed density, detection performance and spray resolution, judging manufacturers' claims critically (LO3, LO7).
  • Explain with quantitative performance measures why harvesting robots remain difficult while milking robots succeeded (LO1, LO5).
  • Discuss safety standards (ISO 18497, the EU Machinery Regulation), liability, the EU rules for spraying drones, and the economic and social implications of agricultural robots (LO6, LO7).

Why robots, and why now?

For a century, agricultural mechanisation meant bigger machines: wider booms, heavier combines, more horsepower per driver. That strategy made one farmer able to cultivate hundreds of hectares, but it has limits. Heavy machines compact soil (Lesson 11.1); wide implements treat every square metre the same; and some tasks — picking fruit, weeding within crop rows, milking — resisted mechanisation because they require perception and dexterity at the scale of the individual plant or animal. Robotics attacks exactly these limits by adding sensing and decision-making to the machine, so that it can treat each plant individually and work without a driver.

Four pressures push in the same direction (Duckett et al., 2018; Oliveira et al., 2021). Labour: seasonal work in horticulture depends on migrant workers who are increasingly hard to recruit. Chemicals: the EU Farm to Fork strategy aims to reduce the use and risk of chemical pesticides by 50 % by 2030 (European Commission, 2020), few new herbicides reach the market, and herbicide-resistant weeds are spreading — especially a problem in vegetables and organic crops, where robotic weeders fill a gap (Fennimore & Cutulle, 2019). Precision: the data–decision–action cycle of precision agriculture reaches its logical end at the single plant. Enabling technology: centimetre-level RTK positioning, cheap cameras, embedded processors and deep learning have made perception affordable.

Early 1990s

Anatomy of a field robot

Every robot, from a milking arm to a weeding rover, runs the same loop: sense → perceive and localise → plan → act, many times per second, with a safety layer that can override everything else. Figure 1 shows the components of a typical autonomous field robot.

Localisation fuses RTK-GNSS (the centimetre positioning of Lesson 11.1) with inertial sensors and wheel odometry, usually in a Kalman filter, so that short GNSS outages under trees or buildings do not stop the machine. In row crops, cameras that detect the rows themselves often provide the most relevant reference: what matters is the position relative to the plants, not to the satellites. Planning ranges from covering a field in parallel passes (the same geometry as guidance, Lesson 11.1) to choosing which weed to shoot next. Control turns the plan into steering and speed commands. A classic, simple path-following law — used in many field robots — is pure pursuit: aim at a point on the path a fixed distance ahead, and steer along the circular arc that reaches it.

Pure-pursuit path following(11.4.1)
\[ \kappa=\frac{2\,y_L}{L_d^{2}}=\frac{2\sin\alpha}{L_d},\qquad \delta=\arctan\left(W_b\,\kappa\right) \]

the curvature of the arc that takes the robot to a look-ahead point on the path equals twice the lateral offset of that point divided by the square of the look-ahead distance (or twice the sine of the angle to the point divided by the look-ahead distance); the front-wheel steering angle is the arctangent of the wheelbase times the curvature.

\(L_d\)look-ahead distance from the rear axle to the target point on the pathm1–5
\(y_L\)lateral offset of the target point in the robot's framem0–0.5
\(\alpha\)angle between the robot's heading and the line to the target, \(\sin\alpha=y_L/L_d\)rad–
\(\kappa\)curvature of the steering arc (1/radius)m⁻¹0–0.5
\(W_b\)wheelbase (bicycle model)m1–3
\(\delta\)front-wheel steering anglerad (°)±30°
Follow the derivation (circle geometry)
  1. Place the robot's rear axle at the origin, heading along the x axis. The target point is at \((x_L, y_L)\) with \(x_L^2+y_L^2=L_d^2\).
  2. A circle of radius \(R\) tangent to the heading passes through the origin with its centre at \((0,R)\): \(x^2+(y-R)^2=R^2\), i.e. \(x^2+y^2=2Ry\).
  3. For the circle to pass through the target: \(L_d^2 = 2Ry_L\), so \(\kappa = 1/R = 2y_L/L_d^2\). With \(y_L = L_d\sin\alpha\): \(\kappa = 2\sin\alpha/L_d\). ✓
  4. In the bicycle model a vehicle with wheelbase \(W_b\) and steering angle \(\delta\) drives on a circle with \(\tan\delta = W_b/R = W_b\kappa\). ✓
Assumptions and validity

No wheel slip, low speed, flat ground. A short look-ahead tracks tightly but oscillates; a long one is smooth but cuts corners — the look-ahead is usually increased with speed. On soft soil, slopes and with implements that pull sideways, real controllers add feedback terms or model-predictive control (Lesson 10.4).

κ: m/m² = m⁻¹ ✓; \(W_b\kappa\): m × m⁻¹ = dimensionless, as the argument of arctan must be ✓
Worked example 1Getting back onto the row

A weeding robot with a 1.5 m wheelbase has drifted so that its target point, 2.0 m ahead on the planned path, lies 0.30 m to the left. Calculate the curvature, the turning radius and the steering angle commanded by pure pursuit. What happens if the look-ahead is shortened to 1.0 m?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Curvature (Eq. 11.4.1): \(\kappa = 2\times0.30/2.0^2 = 0.15\) m⁻¹; radius \(R = 1/0.15 = 6.7\) m.
  2. Steering angle: \(\delta = \arctan(1.5\times0.15) = \arctan(0.225) = 12.7^{\circ}\).
  3. Shorter look-ahead: \(\kappa = 2\times0.30/1.0^2 = 0.60\) m⁻¹ and \(\delta = \arctan(0.90) = 42^{\circ}\) — beyond the steering limit of most machines, and a recipe for oscillation.
  4. Engineering choice. The look-ahead trades responsiveness against stability; at 1 m s⁻¹ in a sugar-beet row, 1.5–3 m is a reasonable range.
κ = 0.15 m⁻¹ (R ≈ 6.7 m), δ ≈ 12.7°; with Ld = 1 m, δ ≈ 42°

Machine vision: telling crop from weed

For a weeding robot, everything depends on one question asked thousands of times per minute: is this plant a crop or a weed? A digital image is a grid of pixels, each with red, green and blue values (and, in multispectral cameras, near-infrared and red-edge values — the physics of Lesson 11.2). The processing chain has three stages: separate plants from background (segmentation), find individual plants (detection), and decide what each one is (classification) — see also Lesson 10.3.

The first stage exploits the fact that green vegetation reflects relatively more green than red or blue light. Woebbecke et al. (1995) compared colour indices under various soils, residues and lighting and found the excess-green index among the best:

Excess-green index for plant segmentation(11.4.2)
\[ r=\frac{R}{R+G+B},\quad g=\frac{G}{R+G+B},\quad b=\frac{B}{R+G+B},\qquad \text{ExG}=2g-r-b \]

first normalise each colour channel by the sum of all three to obtain chromatic coordinates; the excess-green index is twice the green coordinate minus the red and blue coordinates. A pixel is classified as plant if its ExG exceeds a threshold.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(R, G, B\)pixel values in the red, green and blue channelsdigital number (0–255)–
\(r, g, b\)chromatic coordinates, \(r+g+b=1\)–≈ 1/3 each for grey soil
ExGexcess-green index, −1 to +2–≈ 0 soil; 0.1–0.5 green leaves
\(t\)segmentation threshold (e.g. from Otsu's method)–0.05–0.2
Follow the reasoning
  1. Normalisation removes overall brightness: a leaf in sun and in shadow has similar \(r, g, b\) although its \(R, G, B\) values differ by a factor of several — the same idea as the normalised difference of Lesson 11.2.
  2. For grey soil \(r\approx g\approx b\approx 1/3\), so ExG ≈ 0. For a green leaf \(g\) is larger than \(r\) and \(b\), so ExG is positive.
  3. Thresholding. Otsu (1979) chooses the threshold \(t\) that maximises the between-class variance of the histogram of index values: \(\sigma_B^2(t)=\omega_0(t)\,\omega_1(t)\,[\mu_0(t)-\mu_1(t)]^2\), where \(\omega\) are the fractions of pixels below and above \(t\) and \(\mu\) their mean values. It adapts automatically to each image.
  4. Connected groups of plant pixels ("blobs") are then treated as plant candidates for classification.
Assumptions and validity

ExG separates green from not-green, not crop from weed; green weeds among a green crop ("green-on-green") need shape, texture, position or deep learning. Red-leaved plants, green algae on soil, strong shadows and specular reflections cause errors. Near-infrared bands, where available, give a much stronger plant–soil contrast.

all quantities dimensionless ratios ✓

Separating crop from weed ("green-on-green") is harder. Earlier systems used hand-crafted features — leaf shape, size, texture, position in the row. Since about 2018, convolutional neural networks trained on thousands of labelled images have become standard (Hasan et al., 2021; Lesson 10.3). A second strategy avoids the problem: if the robot records the position of every crop seed when it sows (as the FarmDroid robots do with RTK-GNSS), any plant elsewhere is a weed by definition (Allmendinger et al., 2022). A third uses 3D cameras: crop plants of a known age have a predictable height and structure.

How good is the detector?

A detector makes two kinds of mistakes: it misses weeds (false negatives) and it attacks crop plants or bare soil (false positives). Accuracy — the fraction of correct decisions — is a poor summary when weeds are rare. The standard measures come from the confusion matrix:

Precision, recall and F1 score(11.4.3)
\[ \text{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \text{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad F_1=\frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}} \]

precision is the fraction of plants called "weed" that really are weeds; recall is the fraction of real weeds that were found; the F1 score is their harmonic mean.

SymbolMeaning (for the class "weed")Consequence in the field
TPtrue positives: weeds detected as weedsweed controlled
FPfalse positives: crop plants (or soil) called weedscrop damaged or herbicide wasted
FNfalse negatives: weeds missedweed survives, competes, sets seed
TNtrue negatives: crop plants left alone–
Why the harmonic mean?
  1. A detector can reach recall = 1 trivially by calling everything a weed (and spraying the whole field), or high precision by calling only the most obvious plants weeds. Neither is useful.
  2. The harmonic mean \(2PR/(P+R)\) is dominated by the smaller of the two, so F1 is high only if both are high.
  3. Moving the decision threshold trades precision against recall; plotting one against the other gives the precision–recall curve, whose area summarises detector quality. Figure 2 lets you explore this trade-off.
Assumptions and validity

Metrics depend on the test images: a detector trained in one field and season may perform much worse in another (different soils, light, growth stages, weed species). Report metrics on independent test data from the conditions of use, and state the prevalence of weeds, which affects precision.

counts/counts = dimensionless (0–1) ✓
Worked example 2A 94 % accurate detector that damages 5 % of the crop

A weed detector is tested on 1000 plants in a sugar-beet field: 200 weeds and 800 beet plants. It detects 180 of the weeds and wrongly labels 40 beet plants as weeds. Calculate accuracy, precision, recall and F1, and the share of beet plants that a spot sprayer with a non-selective herbicide would hit.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Confusion matrix: TP = 180, FN = 20, FP = 40, TN = 760.
  2. Accuracy \(= (180+760)/1000 = 0.94\).
  3. Precision \(= 180/(180+40) = 0.818\); recall \(= 180/200 = 0.90\).
  4. F1 \(= 2\times0.818\times0.90/(0.818+0.90) = 0.857\).
  5. Crop hit: \(40/800 = 5\) % of the beet plants would be sprayed — with a non-selective herbicide, 5 % of the crop killed. And 10 % of the weeds survive.
  6. Lesson. "94 % accuracy" sounds excellent but hides the two numbers that matter agronomically. Manufacturers should report precision and recall under realistic field conditions.
Accuracy 0.94; precision 0.82; recall 0.90; F1 0.86; 5 % of crop plants hit

Weeding robots: mechanical, laser and spot spraying

Weeding is where field robotics has come furthest commercially, because it combines a clear economic need, a tolerable consequence of occasional errors and a task that suits slow, light machines. Three families of actuators are used (Slaughter et al., 2008; Gerhards et al., 2022):

  • Mechanical weeding. Camera-guided inter-row hoes keep their blades centred between rows; Gerhards et al. (2022) report that camera guidance increased selectivity and weed-control efficacy compared with manually steered hoeing, at higher driving speeds. The hard part is the intra-row weeds between crop plants in the same row, which robots remove with blades or fingers that move in and out around each crop plant. The Danish FarmDroid robots avoid vision altogether: they record the RTK position of every seed at sowing (the manufacturer states an accuracy of 8 mm) and later hoe around those positions, both between and within rows. According to the manufacturer, the FD20 model works up to 12 rows over a 3.5 m width, is powered by four solar panels, weighs 1250 kg and is intended for about 20 ha per season.
  • Thermal and laser weeding. A laser beam aimed at the apical meristem heats the water in the growing point until the tissue dies; efficacy depends on wavelength, spot size, exposure time and weed species (Mathiassen et al., 2006). Carbon Robotics' tractor-drawn LaserWeeder, commercial since 2022, carries 30 CO₂ lasers of 150 W and 42 cameras; the company states a work rate of 0.20–0.61 ha h⁻¹, "5,000+ weeds per minute" and control of "up to 99 %" of weeds. Equation 11.4.5 below checks whether such numbers are physically plausible.
  • Spot spraying. Cameras detect weeds and individual nozzles open only over them. "Green-on-brown" systems spray any green plant on bare soil (before sowing, between rows); "green-on-green" systems must distinguish weeds within the crop. The Swiss Ecorobotix ARA sprayer carries 156 nozzles spaced 4 cm apart and states a spray spot of 6 cm × 6 cm, a working width of 6.2 m at 7.2 km h⁻¹ and savings of plant-protection products "from 50 % to over 95 %, depending on the target, crop stage and weed infestation". John Deere's See & Spray, developed from the Blue River Technology company acquired in 2017, uses boom-mounted cameras to switch individual nozzles in large-scale row crops. Independently, a review of patch-spraying trials found herbicide savings of at least 50 % in various crops without additional weed-control costs in the following years (Gerhards et al., 2022).
Reading manufacturers' claims critically

"Up to 95 % savings" is a best case, not an average. The saving of any spot sprayer depends above all on how much of the field actually has weeds at the resolution of the sprayer — which Eq. 11.4.4 makes explicit. Ask: savings compared with what (the same product broadcast, or a different programme)? At which weed density and growth stage? Measured by whom, and over how many fields? Can the herbicide programme be spot-applied at all (soil-acting residual herbicides cannot)? How many crop plants were damaged? Peer-reviewed on-farm comparisons are still scarce.

Treated area and herbicide saving of a spot sprayer(11.4.4)
\[ p_w = 1-e^{-\lambda a},\qquad f_T = r\,p_w + q\,(1-p_w),\qquad S = 1-f_T \]

for weeds scattered at random with density lambda, the probability that a spray cell of area a contains at least one weed is one minus e to the minus lambda a; the treated fraction of the field is the recall times that probability plus the false-positive rate times the probability of an empty cell; the saving compared with broadcast spraying is one minus the treated fraction.

\(\lambda\)weed densityweeds m⁻²1–100
\(a\)area of one spray cell (nozzle width × pulse length)m²0.0036 (6 × 6 cm) – 9 (3 m boom section)
\(p_w\)probability that a cell contains ≥ 1 weed––
\(r\)recall: probability that a weedy cell is sprayed–0.85–0.98
\(q\)false-positive rate: probability that a weed-free cell is sprayed–0.005–0.05
\(f_T\), \(S\)treated fraction of the area; saving relative to broadcast––
Follow the derivation
  1. Random weeds. If weeds are placed independently at random (a Poisson process) with mean density λ, the number in a cell of area \(a\) is Poisson-distributed with mean \(\lambda a\). The probability of zero weeds is \(e^{-\lambda a}\), so a cell contains at least one weed with probability \(p_w = 1-e^{-\lambda a}\).
  2. Imperfect detection. A weedy cell is sprayed with probability \(r\) (recall at cell level); an empty cell is sprayed by mistake with probability \(q\). By the law of total probability, the expected treated fraction is \(f_T = r\,p_w + q(1-p_w)\).
  3. Saving. If the treated cells receive the full label rate, product use is proportional to treated area, so \(S = 1-f_T\).
  4. Limits. Small cells or few weeds (\(\lambda a\ll1\)): \(p_w\approx\lambda a\) and \(f_T\approx r\lambda a + q\) — the saving is limited by the false-positive rate. Large cells or many weeds (\(\lambda a\gg1\)): \(p_w\to1\) and \(f_T\to r\) — spot spraying degenerates into broadcast spraying.
Assumptions and validity

Real weeds are clustered in patches; for the same mean density, clustering leaves more cells empty and raises the saving (explore this in Figure 3). Spray drift, nozzle response time and the minimum dose per spot are ignored, and so is the time-dependence: weeds that emerge after the pass need another treatment.

λa: m⁻² × m² = dimensionless (the Poisson mean) ✓; all fractions dimensionless ✓
Worked example 3Spot spraying sugar beet: why resolution matters

A sugar-beet field has 5 weeds m⁻², scattered at random. Compare the herbicide saving of (a) a sprayer with 6 cm × 6 cm cells and (b) one that switches 0.5 m boom sections in 0.5 m pulses, both with recall 0.95 and false-positive rate 0.01. The broadcast programme would use 1.0 kg of active ingredient per hectare.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Fine cells: \(a = 0.06^2 = 0.0036\) m², \(\lambda a = 0.018\), \(p_w = 1-e^{-0.018} = 0.0178\). \(f_T = 0.95\times0.0178+0.01\times0.9822 = 0.0169+0.0098 = 0.0268\) → saving 97.3 %, i.e. 0.027 kg a.i. ha⁻¹.
  2. (b) Coarse cells: \(a = 0.25\) m², \(\lambda a = 1.25\), \(p_w = 1-e^{-1.25} = 0.713\). \(f_T = 0.95\times0.713+0.01\times0.287 = 0.681\) → saving 32 %, i.e. 0.68 kg a.i. ha⁻¹.
  3. Note that in (a) more than a third of the treated area comes from false positives — at low weed densities, precision matters as much as resolution.
  4. Sanity check. At 50 weeds m⁻² even the fine sprayer treats \(1-e^{-0.18} = 16\) % of cells and saves "only" about 83 %: savings claims are only meaningful together with the weed density.
(a) ≈ 97 % saving (0.027 kg a.i. ha⁻¹); (b) ≈ 32 % saving (0.68 kg a.i. ha⁻¹)

Is a laser weeder physically plausible?

Energy per weed and maximum throughput of a laser weeder(11.4.5)
\[ E_{\text{w}} \approx \frac{m\left(c_w\,\Delta T + f_v\,L_v\right)}{\eta_{\text{abs}}},\qquad \dot n_{\max}=\frac{N_{\text{L}}\,P_{\text{L}}}{E_{\text{w}}} \]

the laser energy needed per weed is roughly the mass of tissue to be killed times the heat to warm it plus the heat to evaporate a fraction of its water, divided by the fraction of the beam energy that the tissue absorbs; the maximum number of weeds treated per second is the total laser power divided by that energy.

\(m\)mass of meristem tissue to be killed (young weed)kg≈ 5 × 10⁻⁶ (5 mg)
\(c_w\)specific heat of water-rich tissueJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹4186
\(\Delta T\)temperature rise to a lethal levelK≈ 60 (from 20 to 80 °C)
\(f_v, L_v\)fraction of water evaporated; latent heat of vaporisation–; J kg⁻¹0–1; 2.26 × 10⁶
\(\eta_{\text{abs}}\)fraction of beam energy absorbed by the target–0.3–0.8
\(N_L, P_L\)number of lasers; optical power per laser–; W30; 150
Follow the reasoning
  1. Killing a meristem thermally means raising its temperature to a level at which proteins denature (roughly 60–80 °C), or boiling off part of its water. The sensible heat is \(mc_w\Delta T\); boiling a fraction \(f_v\) adds \(f_vmL_v\).
  2. With \(m = 5\) mg, \(\Delta T = 60\) K: \(5\times10^{-6}\times4186\times60 = 1.3\) J; evaporating half the water adds \(0.5\times5\times10^{-6}\times2.26\times10^{6} = 5.7\) J. With 50 % absorption, \(E_w\approx (1.3+5.7)/0.5 \approx 14\) J per weed.
  3. 30 lasers of 150 W deliver 4500 J s⁻¹, enough for up to \(4500/14\approx 320\) weeds per second if lasers were always firing on target.
  4. The manufacturer's "5,000 weeds per minute" is 83 weeds s⁻¹, i.e. an average budget of \(4500/83 = 54\) J per weed — four times our estimate, which leaves room for aiming, tracking and larger weeds. The claim is therefore physically plausible; whether 99 % of weeds die in a given field is an agronomic question only field trials can answer.
Assumptions and validity

An order-of-magnitude energy balance: heat conduction away from the spot, reflection, the size of the weed and whether the meristem is hit are all simplified. Larger weeds need much more energy, which is why laser weeding works best at early growth stages (Mathiassen et al., 2006). Lasers also require strict eye-safety measures (the implement is a class 4 laser product).

Harvesting robots: why picking is hard

Picking a strawberry is trivial for a person and extraordinarily difficult for a machine. The fruit must be found among leaves, judged ripe, reached without collisions, detached without bruising and placed gently — in an environment that is never the same twice. In a systematic review of 50 harvesting robots for high-value crops, Bac et al. (2014) found averages of 85 % localisation success, 75 % detachment success, 66 % overall harvest success, 5 % fruit damage, 45 % peduncle damage and a cycle time of 33 s per fruit; performance had not improved over three decades, and none of the robots had been commercialised.

The EU project SWEEPER built and tested a sweet-pepper harvesting robot in a commercial Dutch greenhouse (Arad et al., 2020). Over a four-week test with 262 fruits, the average cycle time was 24 s, of which about half was logistics (7.8 s to discharge the fruit and 4.7 s for platform movements). The harvest success rate was 61 % under the best-fit crop conditions — but only 18 % in current commercial crop conditions. Laboratory tests showed that the cycle time could be cut to 15 s by running the arm faster.

Throughput of a harvesting robot and robots needed(11.4.6)
\[ \dot n = \frac{3600\,s}{t_c}\,k_{\text{arm}},\qquad N_{\text{rob}}=\left\lceil\frac{\dot n_{\text{req}}}{\dot n\,\eta_t}\right\rceil \]

the number of fruits a robot harvests per hour is 3600 seconds times its success rate, divided by the cycle time in seconds, times the number of arms; the number of robots needed is the required harvest rate divided by the throughput of one robot times its fraction of productive time, rounded up.

SymbolMeaningUnitSWEEPER value
\(s\)harvest success rate (fraction of target fruits picked)–0.61 (best-fit) / 0.18
\(t_c\)cycle time per attempts24
\(k_{\text{arm}}\)number of arms working in parallel–1
\(\eta_t\)fraction of time the robot is productive (not charging, moving, failing)–0.5–0.9
\(\dot n_{\text{req}}\)required harvest rate of the farmfruits h⁻¹–
Follow the reasoning
  1. In one hour a robot makes \(3600/t_c\) attempts; a fraction \(s\) succeeds. Parallel arms multiply the rate (if they do not get in each other's way).
  2. Missed fruit must be picked by hand, so the human labour saved is proportional to \(s\), not to the number of attempts.
  3. Dividing the farm's required rate by the effective rate of one robot, \(\dot n\eta_t\), gives the fleet size.
Assumptions and validity

Assumes every attempt takes the same time and that failures do not damage plants or fruit. In reality damaged fruit reduce value, and a robot must also move between plants and rows. The equation shows the two levers: shorter cycles and higher success — the second often requires changing the crop (variety, training system, plant spacing) as much as the robot (Bac et al., 2014).

Worked example 4How many pepper robots would a greenhouse need?

Suppose a pepper greenhouse must harvest 2 000 fruits per hour during the harvest shift (an assumed figure). Using SWEEPER's measured performance (24 s cycle; 61 % success in best-fit and 18 % in current crop conditions) and a productive-time fraction of 0.8, how many single-arm robots are needed? Reveal the reasoning

  2. Current crop: \(\dot n = 3600\times0.18/24 = 27\) fruits h⁻¹; effective 21.6; robots \(= \lceil2000/21.6\rceil = 93\) — and 82 % of the fruit still needs hand picking.

The success story: milking robots

If harvesting robots show how hard agricultural robotics is, automatic milking systems (AMS) show what success looks like. Commercial AMS have been available since the early 1990s and are now common on dairy farms across northern Europe (Jacobs & Siegford, 2012). Two of the leading manufacturers are Lely (the Netherlands; Astronaut) and DeLaval, a Swedish company with its head office in Tumba near Stockholm, whose roots go back to AB Separator, founded in 1883 by Gustaf de Laval — inventor of the centrifugal cream separator — and Oscar Lamm. DeLaval's robot is called the VMS, the voluntary milking system.

The key idea is voluntary milking: instead of being herded to the parlour twice a day, each cow decides when to visit the robot, attracted by concentrate feed. In the box, the robot identifies the cow, cleans the teats, locates them with a laser or 3D camera, attaches the cups one by one, milks each quarter separately, measures yield, conductivity and milk quality, and releases the cow. Jacobs and Siegford (2012) summarise the potential as up to 12 % more milk (more frequent milking) and up to 18 % less labour, with welfare benefits from cows choosing their own rhythm — while stressing that many farms do not realise these benefits fully, because management and barn layout matter more than the machine.

Key idea — why milking robots succeeded

Compare with harvesting: (1) the environment is structured — a milking box, not an open field; (2) the animal comes to the robot, not the robot to the plant; (3) the task is one task repeated 2–3 times per cow per day, all year; (4) the labour saved is exceptionally valuable — twice-daily milking, 365 days a year, ties a farm family down; and (5) the robot produces data on every cow at every milking. Successful agricultural robots simplify the world they work in.

Spraying drones and robot swarms

Spraying drones are widely used in parts of Asia, but the EU is restrictive. The Sustainable Use Directive requires member states to "ensure that aerial spraying is prohibited", with derogations only in special cases where there are no viable alternatives or where aerial application has clear advantages for health and the environment (Directive 2009/128/EC, Art. 9). The Directive defines aerial spraying as application from an aircraft (plane or helicopter); in practice spraying drones have generally been treated as aerial application requiring a derogation, and how the rules should apply to small drones has been under discussion in the EU (as of 2026). Aviation rules add a second layer: in the EU "open" drone category, the aircraft must stay within 120 m of the ground, must not carry dangerous goods and must not drop any material; operations that do not meet these conditions — spraying included — fall in the "specific" category and need an operational authorisation (Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2019/947). Drones are, however, freely used for sensing (Lesson 11.2).

A swarm replaces one large machine by many small, light, cooperating robots. The appeal is physical and economic: small machines compact the soil less, a failure stops one unit rather than the whole operation, capacity scales by adding units, and — with no driver to pay per machine — the traditional economies of size of big tractors weaken. Using experience from the Hands Free Hectare project, Lowenberg-DeBoer et al. (2021) showed that autonomous equipment allowed a medium-sized UK grain farm to approach minimum production costs, keeping even small, irregular fields economic and reducing the pressure to remove hedges and field trees. The capacity of a machine — and thus the number of robots needed — follows from simple kinematics:

Effective field capacity and fleet size(11.4.7)
\[ C_e=\frac{w\,v\,\eta_f}{10},\qquad N=\left\lceil\frac{A}{C_e\,t_{\text{day}}\,D}\right\rceil \]

the effective field capacity in hectares per hour is the working width in metres times the speed in kilometres per hour times the field efficiency, divided by ten; the number of machines needed is the area divided by the capacity times the working hours per day times the number of days in the window, rounded up.

SymbolMeaningUnitTractor hoe / small robot
\(w\)working widthm6 / 3.5
\(v\)working speedkm h⁻¹8 / ≈ 1
\(\eta_f\)field efficiency (turns, refilling, stops)–0.75 / 0.7
\(C_e\)effective field capacityha h⁻¹3.6 / ≈ 0.25
\(t_{\text{day}}\), \(D\)working hours per day; days in the operation windowh d⁻¹; d10 / up to 24; 10
Follow the derivation
  1. In one hour a machine of width \(w\) (m) at speed \(v\) (km h⁻¹ = 1000 m h⁻¹) sweeps \(1000\,w\,v\) m² = \(w v/10\) ha.
  2. Turning, overlapping, refilling and stops reduce this by the field efficiency \(\eta_f\).
  3. The work that must be done in the window is \(A\) ha; one machine does \(C_e t_{\text{day}} D\) ha; the ratio, rounded up, is the fleet size.
Assumptions and validity

Uniform fields; the window is set by agronomy (weeds must be removed while small) and by weather. Robots can work 24 h per day, which compensates for their low speed — if the battery or solar supply allows.

m × km h⁻¹ / 10 = ha h⁻¹ (since 1 m × 1 km = 1000 m² = 0.1 ha) ✓
Worked example 5One tractor or a fleet of solar robots?

A farm grows 120 ha of sugar beet. Each weeding pass must be completed within 10 days. Compare a 6 m tractor-mounted camera hoe (8 km h⁻¹, field efficiency 0.75, 10 h per day with a driver) with solar robots that, according to their manufacturer, can cover up to 5–6 ha per day each (5 ha in the FD20 factsheet, up to 6 ha on the website).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Tractor hoe: \(C_e = 6\times8\times0.75/10 = 3.6\) ha h⁻¹ → 36 ha per 10 h day → 120 ha in 3.3 days: one machine and one driver suffice, with time to spare for bad weather.
  2. Robots: at 5 ha per day each, \(\lceil 120/(5\times10)\rceil = 3\) robots; at the optimistic 6 ha per day, \(\lceil 120/(6\times10)\rceil = 2\) (more if weather or charging reduces the daily rate).
  3. What the numbers do not show. The robots also hoe within the rows, which the camera hoe cannot, saving hand weeding; they weigh about a quarter of a tractor; they need no driver, but someone must supervise and move them between fields. The comparison is therefore about labour and intra-row weeding, not capacity.
1 tractor hoe (≈ 3.3 days) versus 2–3 robots (10 days)

Safety, standards and liability

A driver notices a child, a deer or a sleeping farm dog; a robot must be designed to. For highly automated agricultural machinery the international standard ISO 18497 was first published in 2018 as "Safety of highly automated agricultural machines — Principles for design", and has since been revised into a multi-part standard on the "Safety of partially automated, semi-autonomous and autonomous machinery", with parts covering machine design principles and vocabulary and design principles for obstacle protection systems, among others. Standards like these define how a manufacturer can demonstrate that a machine meets the essential requirements of the law.

In the EU, the law is changing. The new Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 replaces the Machinery Directive and applies from 20 January 2027. For the first time it contains explicit requirements for autonomous mobile machinery: where relevant, such machinery must have a supervisory function that lets a remote supervisor receive information and only "stop and start remotely the machinery … or move it to a safe position and a safe state". Safety components with "fully or partially self-evolving behaviour using machine learning approaches ensuring safety functions" are placed among the high-risk categories that require third-party conformity assessment. In parallel, the revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853, which applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026, makes clear that no-fault liability for defective products covers software, including AI systems — so a manufacturer can be liable for damage caused by a faulty detection algorithm even without negligence.

Stopping distance and required detection range(11.4.8)
\[ d_{\text{stop}} = v\,t_r+\frac{v^{2}}{2a},\qquad d_{\text{detect}}\ \ge\ d_{\text{stop}}+d_{\text{margin}} \]

the stopping distance is the distance travelled during the reaction time of the detection and control chain plus the braking distance, the speed squared over twice the deceleration; the obstacle-detection system must see at least that far plus a safety margin.

SymbolMeaningUnitSmall robot / autonomous tractor
\(v\)travel speedm s⁻¹0.3–1.5 / 2–5
\(t_r\)total reaction time: sensing, processing, actuating brakess0.2–1.0
\(a\)achievable deceleration (depends on soil, slope, load)m s⁻²1–3
\(d_{\text{margin}}\)safety margin (e.g. for detection uncertainty)m0.5–2
Follow the derivation
  1. During the reaction time the machine continues at speed \(v\): distance \(vt_r\).
  2. Braking at constant deceleration \(a\) from \(v\) to rest takes time \(v/a\) and distance \(v^2/2a\) (from \(v^2 = 2as\)).
  3. Add the two, then a margin: the sensor must reliably detect a person at that distance under all expected conditions (dust, crop, rain, darkness).
Assumptions and validity

Constant deceleration on firm ground; wet clay or a downhill slope lowers \(a\) sharply. Because the braking term grows with \(v^2\), doubling speed more than doubles the required detection range — one reason why many field robots are slow.

m s⁻¹ × s + (m² s⁻²)/(m s⁻²) = m ✓
Worked example 6How far must the robot see?

Compare a small weeding robot at 3.6 km h⁻¹ with an autonomous tractor at 12 km h⁻¹. Both have a reaction time of 0.5 s and a deceleration of 1.5 m s⁻² on a moist field. Add a 1 m margin. What detection range is needed?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Robot: \(v = 1.0\) m s⁻¹: \(d = 1.0\times0.5 + 1.0^2/3.0 = 0.50+0.33 = 0.83\) m → detection range ≥ 1.8 m.
  2. Tractor: \(v = 3.33\) m s⁻¹: \(d = 1.67 + 3.33^2/3.0 = 1.67+3.70 = 5.37\) m → detection range ≥ 6.4 m.
  3. Consequence. Tripling the speed multiplies the braking distance by nine. The tractor must detect a person lying in a standing crop at more than 6 m — a much harder perception problem than the robot's 2 m, and one reason why the first autonomous machines were small and slow.
≈ 0.8 m (robot) vs ≈ 5.4 m (tractor) stopping distance; detection ≥ 1.8 m and ≥ 6.4 m

Who is liable when something goes wrong? The manufacturer answers for defects in the product — hardware and, now explicitly, software; the farmer or contractor for how the machine is used, supervised and maintained; and insurers need data to assign responsibility, which is why autonomous machines log their decisions. Clear rules on data access, cybersecurity and software updates are part of what makes autonomy acceptable (Sparrow & Howard, 2021).

Economics and society

Do robots pay? Lowenberg-DeBoer et al. (2020) screened almost 5 000 documents on the economics of agricultural automation and robotics and analysed the 18 relevant studies in depth. All found some scenarios in which the technologies were profitable; most used partial budgets — the method of Lesson 11.1 — and relied on design targets or prototype data rather than farm experience; all were set in developed countries. The robot's cost per hectare is the key quantity:

Annual cost per hectare of a robot(11.4.9)
\[ c_{\text{ha}}=\frac{K\,\dfrac{i}{1-(1+i)^{-n}} + C_{\text{O\&M}} + h_s\,w_s}{A} \]

the cost per hectare per year is the investment times the annuity factor, plus the annual operation and maintenance cost, plus the supervision hours times the wage, all divided by the area the robot covers per year.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(K\)purchase price€
\(i, n\)interest rate; economic lifeyr⁻¹; yr
\(C_{\text{O\&M}}\)maintenance, insurance, software subscriptions, energy€ yr⁻¹
\(h_s, w_s\)hours of supervision and moving between fields; wageh yr⁻¹; € h⁻¹
\(A\)area served per year (per pass or per season)ha yr⁻¹
Follow the reasoning
  1. The annuity factor \(i/[1-(1+i)^{-n}]\) converts an investment into an equal annual payment that repays it with interest over \(n\) years (Lesson 11.1, Eq. 11.1.12).
  2. Add the running costs and the value of the human time the robot still needs.
  3. Divide by the area served. Compare with the cost of the operation it replaces — for intra-row weeding in organic vegetables or sugar beet, often manual labour.
Assumptions and validity

A static comparison: it ignores changes in yield, timeliness, soil compaction, herbicide costs and environmental benefits, which a full partial budget would include, and the fact that early machines may have shorter lives or rapid obsolescence.

(€ × yr⁻¹ + € yr⁻¹ + h yr⁻¹ × € h⁻¹)/ha = € ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 7What does robotic weeding cost per hectare?

Assume (illustrative figures) that a seeding-and-weeding robot costs €80 000, lasts 8 years at 5 % interest, costs €2 000 per year to maintain, and needs 150 h per year of supervision and moving at €30 h⁻¹. It serves 20 ha of organic sugar beet per season. What is the cost per hectare? How does it change if it serves 30 ha?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Annuity factor: \(0.05/[1-1.05^{-8}] = 0.05/0.3232 = 0.1547\) yr⁻¹ → capital cost \(80\,000\times0.1547 = \)€12 380 yr⁻¹.
  2. Running costs: €2 000 + 150 × €30 = €6 500 yr⁻¹. Total ≈ €18 880 yr⁻¹.
  3. Per hectare: 18 880/20 = €944 ha⁻¹; at 30 ha: €629 ha⁻¹.
  4. Interpretation. The robot pays if it replaces more than this in hand weeding, herbicides and yield losses. In organic sugar beet or vegetables, where intra-row hand weeding can require many hours per hectare, that threshold can be reached; in conventional cereals it cannot. Utilisation (hectares per robot per year) is the strongest lever — which favours contractors and machine sharing.
≈ €944 ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ at 20 ha; ≈ €629 ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ at 30 ha

Beyond profit, robots change farming and rural society (Sparrow & Howard, 2021). They can relieve people of heavy, repetitive and hazardous work and reduce dependence on precarious seasonal labour — but they also displace jobs, demand new skills (programming, data interpretation, maintenance) and may concentrate power and data with a few machinery and software companies. Environmental benefits — less herbicide, less compaction, less fuel — are real but depend on how the technology is used: cheaper weed control could also make it profitable to farm land that is better left uncultivated (a rebound effect). Who owns the data a robot collects, whether farmers may repair their own machines, and whether small farms and poorer regions can access the technology are political as well as technical questions.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Research is moving towards generalist field robots that swap tools (seeding, weeding, sensing) and learn new crops from few labelled images; towards crop systems designed for robots, from planting patterns to varieties with accessible fruit; towards fleets coordinated from the cloud; and towards certified safety for machine-learning perception, as required by the new EU Machinery Regulation. The bottleneck is shifting from hardware to reliable perception in all conditions and to business models that make the machines affordable.

Key terms

Agricultural robot
A machine that senses its environment, decides and acts in agricultural tasks with some degree of autonomy.
Localisation
Estimating the robot's position and heading, typically by fusing RTK-GNSS, inertial sensors, odometry and cameras.
Pure pursuit
A path-following law that steers along the circular arc to a look-ahead point on the path; curvature κ = 2 sin α / Ld.
Autonomous mobile machinery
In EU law, mobile machinery with an autonomous mode in which all essential safety functions are ensured without permanent operator interaction.
Excess-green index (ExG)
2g − r − b computed from chromatic coordinates; separates green plants from soil and residues in colour images.
Green-on-green detection
Distinguishing weeds from crop plants (both green), as opposed to detecting any green plant on bare soil (green-on-brown).
Precision and recall
The fraction of detections that are correct, and the fraction of true targets that are detected; combined in the F1 score.
Spot spraying
Applying herbicide only to detected weeds by switching individual nozzles or small boom sections.
Intra-row weeding
Removing weeds between crop plants within the same row, where conventional hoes cannot work.
Cycle time
The time a harvesting robot needs per attempt, including detection, motion, detachment and fruit handling.
Automatic milking system (AMS)
A robot that milks cows that visit it voluntarily, attaching cups automatically and recording milk data for each cow.
Swarm robotics
The use of many small cooperating robots instead of one large machine.
Supervisory function
A remote, non-permanent surveillance of autonomous machinery that can only stop, start or move it to a safe state.
ISO 18497
The international standard series on the safety of partially automated, semi-autonomous and autonomous agricultural machinery.
