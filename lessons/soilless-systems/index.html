Soilless systems and growing media

Soil does four jobs for a plant: it holds it upright, stores water, supplies nutrients and lets the roots breathe. Hydroponics takes those four jobs away from a living, forgiving medium and hands them to engineering — to a tank, a pump, a slab of stone wool, a pH controller. This lesson shows how the classic soilless systems solve the four jobs, what the physical properties of growing media mean in numbers, and what you gain and risk when you replace soil. By the end you should be able to choose, size and defend the system you will build in the course project.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how nutrient film technique, deep-water culture, ebb-and-flow, drip irrigation on slabs, wick systems and the Kratky method supply water, nutrients and oxygen to roots, and sketch each system with its flow paths (LO2).
  • Define and calculate total porosity, air-filled porosity, water-holding capacity and bulk density of a growing medium, and explain why container height changes the air–water balance (LO2, LO3).
  • Calculate capillary rise in a wick, the water budget of a Kratky container and the drain fraction and nutrient emission of an open system (LO3).
  • Compare open (drain-to-waste) and closed (recirculating) operation, and yield and water use of soilless and soil-grown lettuce, stating the assumptions behind published comparisons (LO2, LO3, LO7).
  • Identify the single points of failure and disease risks of each system and estimate how long a crop survives a pump or power failure (LO2, LO3).
  • Choose and justify a soilless system for your own growing experiment and plan how you will measure its performance (LO4).

What soil does — and what replaces it

Hydroponics is often introduced as "growing plants in water". That description misses the point. Plants have never needed soil as such; they need what soil provides. A root system needs mechanical support, a continuous supply of water, the thirteen or so mineral nutrients that plants take up as ions, and oxygen for root respiration. A good agricultural soil provides all four with remarkable robustness: it holds several days of water, buffers pH and nutrient supply through its clay and organic matter, and its pore network keeps air in contact with roots. Soilless cultivation separates these four functions and supplies each deliberately. That is its great strength — every factor can be controlled and optimised — and its great weakness, because nothing is buffered unless the designer builds the buffer in.

This separation of functions gives a useful way to think about any soilless system you meet, from a jam jar on a windowsill to a 10-hectare Dutch tomato greenhouse. Ask four questions: What holds the plant? Where is the water stored, and how much? How are nutrients delivered and renewed? How does oxygen reach the root surface? The answers define the system type, its costs and — critically — its failure modes.

Two words need defining precisely because they are used loosely. Hydroponics in the strict sense (solution culture) means roots growing directly in a nutrient solution, with at most a small plug of medium to hold the seedling: nutrient film technique, deep-water culture, aeroponics and the Kratky method. Soilless culture is the wider term and also includes substrate culture, in which roots grow in a porous, usually inert or low-activity medium — stone wool, coconut coir, perlite, peat, wood fibre — that is irrigated with a nutrient solution. In substrate culture the medium stores water and air between irrigations; in solution culture the designer must provide oxygen in another way. The European Union's organic regulation uses essentially this definition when it prohibits "hydroponic production": growing plants "with their roots in a nutrient solution only or in an inert medium to which a nutrient solution is added" (Regulation (EU) 2018/848, Annex II, Part I, point 1.2).

From Woodward's spearmint to the Kratky bucket: a short history

Soilless culture is older than most people think. The modern technology grew out of three centuries of plant physiology experiments in which water culture was the tool used to discover which elements plants need (Hershey, 1994).

1699

John Woodward grows spearmint in water from different sources (rain, river, and water mixed with garden soil) and notices that plants grow better the more "earthy matter" the water contains — an early hint that something dissolved from soil, not the water itself, feeds plants.

1860–1865

Julius von Sachs and Wilhelm Knop publish standard recipes for nutrient solutions made of pure salts and grow plants to maturity in them. Water culture becomes the standard laboratory method for studying mineral nutrition — which is why the essential elements were identified using hydroponics.

Late 1920s–1937

William F. Gericke at the University of California, Berkeley, scales water culture up from the laboratory to crop production, growing tomatoes in large tanks outdoors. In a short 1937 paper in Science he introduces the word hydroponics (Gericke, 1937), and in 1940 publishes a popular book on soilless gardening.

1938 / 1950

Dennis Hoagland and Daniel Arnon publish their circular The water-culture method for growing plants without soil (revised 1950). "Hoagland solution" becomes the most-cited nutrient recipe in plant science (Hoagland & Arnon, 1950; see Lesson 5.2).

1940s

Hydroponic farms supply fresh vegetables to military bases on islands and in places where soil is poor or contaminated — an early demonstration that soilless culture is a tool for producing food where soil fails.

1965–1979

Allen Cooper at the Glasshouse Crops Research Institute in Littlehampton (England) develops the nutrient film technique (NFT): a shallow stream of recirculating solution flowing down a sloping channel (Cooper, 1975; Graves, 1983). His booklet The ABC of NFT (1979) spreads the method worldwide.

1970s–1980s

Stone wool (rock wool) slabs, originally an insulation material, are adopted in Denmark and the Netherlands for greenhouse vegetables. Drip-irrigated substrate culture becomes the industry standard for tomato, cucumber and sweet pepper in northern Europe (Savvas & Gruda, 2018).

1993–2009

Bernard Kratky at the University of Hawaiʻi describes a non-circulating method: the whole crop is grown with the water and nutrients put in the tank at planting, with no pump, no electricity and no aeration (Kratky, 1993, 2004). His 2009 overview of three variants — the 4-litre bottle, the frame-supported tank cover and the float-support raceway — is the standard reference (Kratky, 2009).

2010s–2026

Soilless culture dominates high-tech greenhouse production of fruiting vegetables and is the basis of plant factories with artificial lighting (Lesson 7.2). Research focuses on closing water and nutrient loops, on replacing peat and stone wool with more sustainable media, and on sensors and models for automated control.

History

Gericke credited the word "hydroponics" (Greek hydro, water, and ponos, labour) to his Berkeley colleague William Setchell. The name stuck; the original idea — that a crop could be grown commercially without soil at all — was controversial at the time. Hoagland and Arnon's circular was written partly to temper exaggerated public claims about hydroponic yields, a debate that has echoes in today's discussions of vertical farming.

The six classic systems: how they work

Figure 2 shows the six systems you are most likely to meet — and to build in your project. They differ in three physical choices: whether the roots sit in solution or in a substrate; whether the solution moves (pumped) or is static; and whether used solution is recirculated or discharged. Every choice trades control against robustness.

A · Deep-water culture (DWC, raft or floating system)

Plants sit in net pots in a sheet of extruded polystyrene that floats on 15–30 cm of nutrient solution. Because the tank holds tens of litres of solution per square metre, DWC is thermally and chemically buffered: EC, pH and temperature change slowly, and a pump failure does not immediately dry the roots. The price is oxygen. Water holds only about 8–9 mg of oxygen per litre at 20–25 °C, and a dense root mass consumes it within hours, so the solution must be aerated with air stones or, in commercial ponds, enriched with pure oxygen. The Cornell lettuce system, one of the best-documented commercial raft systems, keeps dissolved oxygen around 8 mg L⁻¹ (7–10) with pure oxygen and notes that lettuce grows satisfactorily above about 4 mg L⁻¹ (Brechner & Both, 2013). Oxygen supply to roots is the subject of Lesson 5.3.

B · Nutrient film technique (NFT)

In NFT the roots lie in a sloping channel (gully) through which a very shallow film of solution — typically a few millimetres deep — flows continuously and returns to a reservoir. The film is thin enough that oxygen diffuses easily to the upper part of the root mat, which is also exposed to humid air. NFT uses little solution per plant and heats and cools quickly, which makes it responsive but fragile: if the pump stops, the channel drains within minutes and the roots are left with only the water held in the root mat. The physics of the film — how its depth depends on slope and flow — is derived in Lesson 5.4.

C · Ebb-and-flow (flood-and-drain)

Plants grow in pots or blocks of substrate standing on a watertight bench. A timer-controlled pump floods the bench for a few minutes; the substrate wicks up solution, then the bench drains back to the reservoir through the pump or an overflow. Draining pulls fresh air into the substrate pores. Ebb-and-flow is widely used for pot plants and seedling production (the Cornell system raises its lettuce seedlings on ebb-and-flow benches). Its weak point is disease: all plants share the same flood water.

D · Drip irrigation on substrate slabs

The standard system for tomato, cucumber and sweet pepper in northern European greenhouses. Each plant (or pair of plants) sits on a slab of stone wool or coir; drippers fed by a lateral pipe deliver short pulses of solution many times per day. Growers deliberately apply more than the crop uses, so that a fraction — the drain fraction, often 10–40 % — leaves the slab, flushing out accumulating salts. The drain is either discharged (an open system) or collected, disinfected and mixed back into the supply (a closed system, Section 5.1.5).

E · Wick systems

A wick of absorbent fabric connects the substrate to a reservoir below; capillary forces lift solution into the medium as the plant uses it. No pump is needed. Wick systems are self-regulating but slow: the flux a wick can carry falls sharply as the lift height approaches the maximum capillary rise (Eq. 5.1.1), so they suit small plants such as herbs, not large, fast-transpiring crops.

Capillary rise in a wick or pore (Jurin's law)(5.1.1)
\[ h_{\max} = \frac{2\,\gamma\,\cos\theta}{\rho\,g\,r} \]

the maximum height to which water can be lifted by capillarity equals twice the surface tension times the cosine of the contact angle, divided by the product of water density, gravitational acceleration and the pore radius. Narrower pores lift water higher.

\(h_{\max}\)equilibrium capillary rise above the free solution surfacem0.05–0.5 in wicks
\(\gamma\)surface tension of water–air interfaceN m⁻¹ = J m⁻²0.0728 (20 °C), 0.0720 (25 °C)
\(\theta\)contact angle between water and the fibre surface°0–60 (cotton, polyester)
\(\rho\)density of the solutionkg m⁻³≈ 998
\(g\)gravitational accelerationm s⁻²9.81
\(r\)effective (equivalent) pore radius of the wickm10–200 µm
Follow the derivation
  1. Model a pore as a vertical glass-like tube of radius \(r\). Where the water surface (meniscus) meets the wall, surface tension pulls along the wall with a force per unit length \(\gamma\), inclined at the contact angle \(\theta\) to the vertical.
  2. The upward vertical force around the whole circumference is \(F_\uparrow = 2\pi r\,\gamma\cos\theta\).
  3. The column of liquid raised to height \(h\) has volume \(\pi r^2 h\) and weight \(F_\downarrow = \rho g\,\pi r^2 h\).
  4. At equilibrium \(F_\uparrow = F_\downarrow\): \(2\pi r\gamma\cos\theta = \rho g \pi r^2 h\). Dividing by \(\pi r\rho g r\) gives \(h = 2\gamma\cos\theta/(\rho g r)\).
  5. Equivalently, the pressure just below a curved meniscus is lower than atmospheric by the Young–Laplace pressure \(\Delta p = 2\gamma\cos\theta/r\); the rise is the height of water column that balances that suction, \(\Delta p=\rho g h\). This "suction" view is the one used for substrates (Section 5.1.4).
Assumptions and validity

Real wicks are a tangle of fibres, not tubes: \(r\) is an effective radius that you can determine by measuring the equilibrium rise. Jurin's law gives only the maximum height, at zero flow. When the plant is drinking, the wick must also carry a flux, which requires a pressure gradient along the wick (Darcy's law); the deliverable flow falls towards zero as the required lift approaches \(h_{\max}\). Design wicks so that the lift is less than about half of \(h_{\max}\), and use several wicks for thirsty plants. Dissolved salts barely change \(\gamma\); surfactants and algae films do.

\(\dfrac{\text{N m}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-3}\cdot\text{m s}^{-2}\cdot\text{m}} = \dfrac{\text{kg s}^{-2}}{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}} = \text{m}\) ✓ (using N = kg m s⁻²)

A student builds a wick system for basil. The substrate surface sits 18 cm above the solution in the reservoir when the reservoir is full, and 26 cm when it is nearly empty. A test with the chosen felt strip (hung vertically in water) shows an equilibrium rise of 21 cm at 20 °C. (a) What is the effective pore radius of the felt, assuming a contact angle of 30°? Reveal the reasoning

Fixes: raise the reservoir (keep the lift below ≈ 10 cm), use a finer, denser wick (smaller \(r\)) or several wicks in parallel.
  5. Sanity check. Paper towel lifts water about 5–15 cm; dense felt 15–30 cm. A 61 µm effective radius is in the plausible range for felt.

F · The Kratky non-circulating method

The Kratky method is the most important system for your project because it is cheap, silent, needs no electricity and — used correctly — gives excellent lettuce. A covered, light-proof container is filled with nutrient solution; the seedling sits in a net pot whose lower 1–3 cm dip into the solution, so capillarity wets the growing medium. No solution is added afterwards. As the plant transpires, the level falls and an enlarging, humid air space opens under the lid. Kratky (2009), following Imai (1987), distinguishes the roots that proliferate in this moist air space — "oxygen roots", whose main function is aeration — from the "water and nutrient roots" that extend into the solution. The crucial rule follows directly: the solution level may stay the same or fall, but must not be raised, because submerging the oxygen roots "drowns" the plant (Kratky, 2009).

Kratky's numbers make the method easy to size. A model system holds 4–8 L of solution per lettuce plant; a 150–250 g head typically consumes 3–6 L; water-use efficiencies below 20 L kg⁻¹ of lettuce are common and 11 L kg⁻¹ has been recorded; leaf and semi-head lettuce is harvested about 6–7 weeks after seeding (or about 5 weeks after transplanting) with a starting EC around 1.5 mS cm⁻¹ (Kratky, 2009).

Kratky water budget and level drop(5.1.2)
\[ V_0 \ge n\,W_p\,(1+s), \qquad \Delta z = \frac{n\,W_p}{A_{\text{tank}}} \]

the initial solution volume must be at least the number of plants times the water each plant will use over the crop, plus a safety margin; the solution level falls by the total water used divided by the free surface area of the tank.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(V_0\)initial solution volume in the tankL4–8 L per lettuce
\(n\)number of plants sharing the tank–1–20
\(W_p\)water used per plant from transplanting to harvest (transpiration + growth + evaporation)L3–6 L (lettuce, Kratky 2009)
\(s\)safety margin (solution that must remain at harvest)–0.25–0.5
\(\Delta z\)fall of the solution level by harvestm (report in cm)6–15 cm
\(A_{\text{tank}}\)free surface area of solution (tank inner area)m²0.05–0.5
Follow the derivation
  1. Water balance of a closed tank. Nothing is added after planting, so \(V(t) = V_0 - \sum W(t)\), where \(W\) is cumulative water loss by transpiration, incorporation into plant tissue and evaporation through gaps in the lid.
  2. Feasibility. The crop must reach harvest before the tank is empty, with some solution left so that roots stay in contact: \(V_0 - nW_p \ge s\,nW_p\), i.e. \(V_0 \ge nW_p(1+s)\).
  3. Geometry. For a tank with vertical walls, volume = area × height, so the level falls by \(\Delta z = nW_p/A_{\text{tank}}\). Convert litres to m³ (÷1000) before dividing by m².
  4. Design check. The tank must be deep enough: initial depth \(z_0 = V_0/A\) must exceed \(\Delta z\) plus the residual depth, and the net pot must initially touch the solution.
Assumptions and validity

Constant-area (vertical-walled) container; tapered tubs need the frustum formula. \(W_p\) depends strongly on climate: Kratky's figures come from Hawaii; under cooler, more humid indoor conditions with LED lighting, transpiration per plant is lower, while under summer greenhouse conditions it can be higher. Measure your own \(W_p\) by marking the level weekly — it is one of the best cheap datasets in the course project.

Growing media: the physics of a good substrate

In substrate culture the growing medium is a porous solid whose pores are shared between water and air. The fundamental design tension is simple: pores full of water store nutrients and water; pores full of air let oxygen diffuse to the roots. A good substrate provides both after it has been irrigated and allowed to drain. Four physical properties describe this balance, and all are defined per unit of bulk volume (the volume the medium occupies in the container, solids plus pores).

Total porosity from bulk and particle density(5.1.3)
\[ \varepsilon_t = 1 - \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_s}, \qquad \rho_b = \frac{m_{\text{dry}}}{V_{\text{bulk}}} \]

total porosity — the fraction of the bulk volume that is pore space — equals one minus the ratio of the bulk density to the particle (solid) density; bulk density is the oven-dry mass divided by the bulk volume.

\(\varepsilon_t\)total porosity (pore volume / bulk volume)m³ m⁻³ (often given in %)0.85–0.97 for most media
\(\rho_b\)dry bulk densitykg m⁻³ (or g cm⁻³)stone wool ≈ 70–90; peat ≈ 60–200
\(\rho_s\)particle density (density of the solid material itself)kg m⁻³organic ≈ 1450–1550; mineral ≈ 2500–2700
\(m_{\text{dry}}\)oven-dry mass of the samplekg–
\(V_{\text{bulk}}\)volume occupied by the sample in the containerm³–
Follow the derivation
  1. The bulk volume is the sum of the solid volume and the pore volume: \(V_{\text{bulk}} = V_s + V_p\).
  2. The solid volume follows from the dry mass and the density of the solid: \(V_s = m_{\text{dry}}/\rho_s\).
  3. Total porosity is \(\varepsilon_t = V_p/V_{\text{bulk}} = 1 - V_s/V_{\text{bulk}} = 1 - (m_{\text{dry}}/\rho_s)/V_{\text{bulk}}\).
  4. Since \(m_{\text{dry}}/V_{\text{bulk}} = \rho_b\): \(\varepsilon_t = 1-\rho_b/\rho_s\).
Assumptions and validity

Assumes all pores are connected and measurable. Some media (e.g., expanded perlite, pumice) contain closed internal pores that water never fills: their "effective" porosity measured by saturation is lower than Eq. 5.1.3 predicts. Bulk density depends on how tightly the medium is packed — always report the packing method.

\(\dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-3}}{\text{kg m}^{-3}}\) = dimensionless ✓

Total porosity tells you how much space there is, not how it is shared. After a substrate is saturated and allowed to drain freely, the fraction of the bulk volume still filled with water is called container capacity or water-holding capacity; the pores that have drained are the air-filled porosity (also "air capacity").

Partition of pore space after drainage(5.1.4)
\[ \varepsilon_t = \theta_{cc} + \varepsilon_a, \qquad \varepsilon_a = \frac{V_{\text{drained}}}{V_{\text{bulk}}}, \qquad \theta_{cc} = \frac{V_{\text{added}} - V_{\text{drained}}}{V_{\text{bulk}}} \]

total porosity is the sum of the water-filled fraction at container capacity and the air-filled fraction. In the classic container test, the air-filled porosity is the volume of water that drains out of a saturated sample divided by the sample's bulk volume, and the water-holding capacity is the water that stays behind divided by the bulk volume.

The container method, step by step
  1. Seal the drainage holes of a container of known volume \(V_{\text{bulk}}\) (e.g. 1.0 L) and fill it with dry medium, packed as it will be used.
  2. Slowly add water from below until the surface glistens; record \(V_{\text{added}}\). Leave for several hours so trapped air escapes.
  3. Open the holes and collect the drainage for 30–60 minutes: \(V_{\text{drained}}\).
  4. Then \(\varepsilon_t \approx V_{\text{added}}/V_{\text{bulk}}\), \(\varepsilon_a = V_{\text{drained}}/V_{\text{bulk}}\), \(\theta_{cc} = \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_a\).
Assumptions and validity

The result is specific to the container height used (Section below: the perched water table). Incomplete wetting of hydrophobic dry peat or coir underestimates total porosity; always pre-wet. The De Boodt & Verdonck targets — total pore space above about 85 %, air content 20–30 %, easily available water 20–30 % and water buffering capacity 4–10 % by volume — are rules of thumb for pot plants, not laws; stone wool slabs for vegetables operate successfully with less air because they are irrigated many times a day with oxygenated solution.

The perched water table: why container height matters

A surprising fact confuses many beginners: the same substrate holds more air in a tall container than in a shallow one. After drainage, water does not simply run out of the bottom. At the base of the container, water is held at zero suction — a saturated zone called the perched water table, because water cannot leave a free surface without being pulled out. At a height \(z\) above the base, water is under a suction equal to \(z\) (in centimetres of water): the pores that can hold water against that suction stay full (Eq. 5.1.1, read backwards), larger pores drain. Each substrate has a water-retention curve \(\theta(h)\) that describes how much water it holds at each suction.

Water-content profile in a drained container(5.1.5)
\[ \theta(z) = \theta_r + \frac{\theta_s - \theta_r}{\left[1 + (\alpha z)^{n}\right]^{1-1/n}}, \qquad \bar\theta = \frac{1}{H}\int_0^{H}\theta(z)\,\mathrm{d}z, \qquad \bar\varepsilon_a = \theta_s - \bar\theta \]

at equilibrium after drainage, the suction at height z above the bottom equals z (in centimetres of water), so the water content at each height is given by the substrate's retention curve (van Genuchten form). The average water content of a container of height H is the mean over the height, and its air-filled porosity is the saturated water content minus that mean.

\(z\)height above the base of the container = suction head at equilibriumcm (of water)
\(\theta_s,\ \theta_r\)saturated and residual volumetric water contents (\(\theta_s\approx\varepsilon_t\))m³ m⁻³
\(\alpha\)inverse of a characteristic air-entry suction; large \(\alpha\) = coarse porescm⁻¹
\(n\)pore-size distribution parameter; large \(n\) = uniform pores, sharp drainage–

Follow the derivation
  1. After drainage stops, the water is at hydrostatic equilibrium: the total potential (pressure + gravity) is the same everywhere. At the base, pressure is atmospheric (zero suction).
  2. Moving up a height \(z\), gravitational potential rises by \(\rho g z\); for the total to stay constant, pressure must fall by the same amount: suction \(\psi = \rho g z\), or expressed as a water column, \(h = z\).
  3. A pore stays filled at suction \(h\) if its capillary rise (Eq. 5.1.1) exceeds \(h\), i.e. if its radius is smaller than \(2\gamma\cos\theta/(\rho g h)\). The retention curve \(\theta(h)\) summarises how much pore volume has radii below each threshold.
  4. Van Genuchten's (1980) empirical function fits measured retention curves of soils and substrates with four parameters. Substituting \(h=z\) gives the profile \(\theta(z)\).
  5. The container average is the integral divided by height. Because \(\theta(z)\) decreases with \(z\), a taller container has a lower average water content and more air.
Assumptions and validity

Assumes equilibrium (no evaporation, no uptake, drainage complete), a homogeneous medium and free drainage at the base. Hysteresis (drying vs wetting curves differ) and shrinkage of organic media are ignored. The parameters used in Figure 4 are illustrative, chosen to reproduce typical behaviour of each medium class; real products vary widely — measure your own.

\((\alpha z)\): cm⁻¹ × cm — dimensionless, as required inside a power ✓; θ in m³ m⁻³ ✓
Common misconception

"Adding a layer of gravel or clay pebbles at the bottom of a pot improves drainage." It does the opposite. The fine substrate above the gravel cannot drain into the coarse layer until its own suction at the interface falls to near zero, so the perched water table simply moves up to the top of the gravel — the wet zone now sits higher in the root zone, and the effective container height for air is reduced. Use a coarser substrate or a taller container instead.

Two chemical properties complete the picture. The cation exchange capacity (CEC, in cmol₍c₎ per kg or per litre) measures how many cations the medium can hold on negatively charged surfaces. A high CEC (peat, coir) buffers the root zone against sudden changes but also means the medium can release or absorb nutrients — coir, for example, releases potassium and sodium and binds calcium unless it has been pre-treated. A medium with negligible CEC (stone wool, perlite) is chemically "transparent": what you feed is what the roots see, which is why stone wool dominates precision-fed greenhouse vegetables. The pH of raw peat is very low and must be raised with lime; fresh stone wool is slightly alkaline and is soaked in acidified solution before planting.

Frontier · peat and stone wool alternatives

Peat has been the dominant component of horticultural growing media for decades, but draining and harvesting peatlands turns carbon sinks into carbon sources. Research and industry are therefore testing wood fibre, composted bark, coir, hemp and other fibres, biochar and even recycled stone wool as replacements, both alone and in blends. The challenge is to reproduce peat's combination of high water retention, adequate air, low weight and consistent quality at a low price, without new problems such as nitrogen immobilisation in wood fibre or the transport footprint of coir (Gruda, 2019). For your project, a peat-free plug (coir, stone wool or foam) in a net pot works perfectly.

Open and closed operation: drain fraction and nutrient emissions

Substrate systems face a salt problem that soil systems solve with rain. Irrigation water and fertilisers bring in ions the crop does not take up in the same proportion — sodium, chloride, sulfate, excess calcium. As the crop transpires pure water, these ions concentrate in the root zone. To keep the root-zone EC under control, growers apply more solution than the crop uses and let the surplus drain away, carrying salts with it. The ratio of drain to supply is the drain fraction.

Drain fraction and nutrient emission of an open system(5.1.6)
\[ \text{DF} = \frac{V_d}{V_s}, \qquad V_s = \frac{V_{ET}}{1-\text{DF}}, \qquad E_N = V_d\;c_{N,d} \]

the drain fraction is the drained volume divided by the supplied volume. Because what is not drained is used by the crop (transpiration plus evaporation), the supply needed to meet a crop water use \(V_{ET}\) at a chosen drain fraction is \(V_{ET}/(1-\text{DF})\). The nutrient emission is the drained volume times the nutrient concentration in the drain.

Follow the derivation
  1. Water balance of the substrate over a day (storage change ≈ 0 at steady state): supply = crop use + drain, \(V_s = V_{ET} + V_d\).
  2. Divide by \(V_s\): \(1 = V_{ET}/V_s + \text{DF}\), so \(V_{ET} = V_s(1-\text{DF})\) and \(V_s = V_{ET}/(1-\text{DF})\).
  3. Mass balance of a nutrient leaving with the drain: emission = drained volume × concentration.
  4. Salt balance (Lesson 5.4): at steady state the salt entering with the supply equals the salt leaving with the drain, \(V_s c_s = V_d c_d\), so \(\text{DF} = c_s/c_d\) — the drain is more concentrated than the supply by the factor 1/DF.

Worked example 3How much nitrogen does an open basil system lose?

A greenhouse grows basil on coir slabs with drip irrigation, drain to waste. On a typical day, 2.5 L m⁻² is supplied and 0.75 L m⁻² is collected as drain. The drain contains 180 mg L⁻¹ nitrogen. Calculate the drain fraction, the crop water use, and the nitrogen emission per hectare and year (assume the same conditions all year). Compare with the 170 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ cap on nitrogen from livestock manure in nitrate-vulnerable zones under the EU Nitrates Directive.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Drain fraction: DF = 0.75/2.5 = 0.30 (30 %).
  2. Crop water use: \(V_{ET} = 2.5 - 0.75 = 1.75\) L m⁻² d⁻¹.
  3. Daily N emission: \(0.75\ \text{L}\times0.180\ \text{g L}^{-1} = 0.135\) g N m⁻² d⁻¹.
  4. Annual, per hectare: \(0.135\times365 = 49.3\) g m⁻² yr⁻¹; 1 ha = 10 000 m², so 493 000 g = 493 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹.
  5. Compare. The emission is almost three times the manure-N cap that applies to farmland in vulnerable zones — and it goes straight to drains or groundwater rather than onto a crop. (The comparison is illustrative: the Nitrates Directive regulates manure application, not greenhouse drainage, but the environmental load is the same kind.) This is why the EU Water Framework Directive and national rules push greenhouse growers towards closed systems (van Os, 2010).
DF = 30 %; VET = 1.75 L m⁻² d⁻¹; ≈ 490 kg N ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ emitted

A closed (recirculating) system collects the drain, disinfects it to kill root pathogens, adjusts EC and pH, and mixes it back into the supply. Water and fertiliser use fall dramatically, but closing the loop creates two new problems. First, ions that the crop takes up slowly — sodium and chloride from tap water, sulfate — accumulate, so a small bleed must still be discharged when their concentration becomes too high; the mass balance is derived in Lesson 5.2. Second, a single infected plant can seed pathogens into the water that reaches every plant (Section 5.1.7). Truly "zero-discharge" operation therefore needs good-quality water (rain water or reverse-osmosis water), careful nutrient balancing and reliable disinfection (van Os, 2010).

How much better is soilless? Yield and water compared with soil

Claims that hydroponics uses "90 % less water" and gives "ten times the yield" are common. Where do such numbers come from, and what do they hide? The most-cited quantitative comparison for lettuce is Barbosa et al. (2015), who modelled a hypothetical 815 m² hydroponic (NFT) greenhouse and conventional field production in Yuma, Arizona — a major US winter lettuce region.

The yield and water advantages are real and have simple physical causes. Yield per square metre is higher because the greenhouse produces year-round (about 12 harvests per year in the model, with lighting 24 h a day), plants are spaced optimally and nothing limits growth that the grower can control. Water use per kilogram is lower because almost all water leaves through the plant: there is no drainage below the root zone, no evaporation from bare soil, no runoff. But the energy column is equally real: heating, cooling (which dominated) and lighting made the hydroponic lettuce about 80 times more energy-intensive in the model. The comparison is therefore not "hydroponics is better", but "hydroponics trades land and water for energy and capital" — a trade that is worth making where water or land is scarce, or where the energy is cheap and low-carbon. These trade-offs are analysed properly in Lesson 8.4 and Lesson 15.1.

Read the small print

When you meet a water-saving claim, ask: Which crop, where, and compared with what irrigation? Field lettuce in a desert is irrigated with far more water than lettuce in rainy Sweden, where much of the water is rain that would have fallen anyway ("green water"). Is the soilless system open or closed? An open system with 30 % drain loses nearly a third of its water. Is energy for cooling or dehumidification counted? Vertical farms recover transpired water with dehumidifiers — using electricity. Is the yield per unit of ground area or per unit of growing area? Stacked systems multiply the latter.

Strengths, risks and single points of failure

Soilless culture's advantages come directly from control: no soil-borne diseases or weeds at the start, no dependence on arable land, precise nutrition, clean produce, high yields and — in closed systems — very efficient use of water and fertiliser. Its risks come from the loss of buffering. In soil, a day without irrigation is rarely a problem. In NFT, an hour without flow on a sunny day can be.

Engineers call a component whose failure stops the whole system a single point of failure. In most soilless systems there are several: the circulation pump, the aeration pump, the electricity supply, the timer, the dosing controller, a clogged filter. The crop's tolerance to a failure depends on how much buffer the system provides — water stored in the substrate or reservoir, or oxygen dissolved in the solution — compared with the rate at which the crop consumes it.

Time to damage after a supply failure(5.1.8)
\[ \tau_{\text{water}} = \frac{V_{\text{avail}}}{E_p}, \qquad \tau_{O_2} = \frac{V_{\text{sol}}\,\big(C^{*}(T)-C_{\text{crit}}\big)}{R_{O_2}\,Q_{10}^{(T-20)/10}} \]

when water supply stops, the time before the plant runs short is the plant-available water stored per plant divided by the plant's transpiration rate. When aeration stops in solution culture, the time before roots become hypoxic is the volume of solution per plant times the oxygen that can be consumed before a critical level is reached, divided by the root oxygen consumption rate, which increases with temperature.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(V_{\text{avail}}\)plant-available water stored per plant (film, root mat, substrate, reservoir)mL≈ 40 (NFT) … 5000 (Kratky)
\(E_p\)transpiration rate per plant at the time of failuremL h⁻¹5–60 (lettuce, basil)
\(V_{\text{sol}}\)solution volume per plantL5–20 (DWC)
\(C^{*}(T)\)dissolved-oxygen saturation at solution temperature \(T\) (Benson & Krause, 1984)mg L⁻¹9.1 at 20 °C, 8.3 at 25 °C
\(C_{\text{crit}}\)critical DO below which stress appearsmg L⁻¹≈ 3–4 (lettuce)
\(R_{O_2}\)root oxygen consumption per plant at 20 °Cmg h⁻¹1–8
\(Q_{10}\)factor by which respiration increases per 10 °C–≈ 2
Follow the derivation
  1. A buffer of size \(B\) is consumed at a constant rate \(r\); it lasts \(B/r\). This "reservoir over rate" estimate is the most useful single idea in systems engineering.
  2. For water, the buffer is the plant-available water \(V_{\text{avail}}\) and the rate is the transpiration \(E_p\), which does not stop when the pump does.
  3. For oxygen, the buffer is the mass of O₂ that can be used before stress, \(V_{\text{sol}}(C^* - C_{\text{crit}})\), assuming the solution starts at saturation. The rate is root respiration, scaled from 20 °C with the \(Q_{10}\) rule (Lesson 5.3).
Assumptions and validity

Rates are treated as constant; in reality transpiration falls as plants wilt and respiration falls as oxygen declines, so the true time to death is longer than \(\tau\) — but \(\tau\) is a good estimate of the time to damage. Oxygen diffusing in through the solution surface is neglected (it slows the decline in shallow tanks). The critical DO for lettuce is taken from the Cornell guidelines (Brechner & Both, 2013) and Goto et al. (1996), who found no growth difference between 2.1 and 16.8 mg L⁻¹ in well-mixed floating culture.

mL ÷ mL h⁻¹ = h ✓; L × mg L⁻¹ ÷ mg h⁻¹ = h ✓

Disease is the second systemic risk. In recirculating systems, water is a highway for zoosporic pathogens — Pythium and Phytophthora species that produce swimming spores. Stanghellini and Rasmussen (1994) titled their classic review "Hydroponics: a solution for zoosporic pathogens" precisely because the recirculating solution delivers the pathogen to every root. Warm solution, low oxygen and stressed roots favour infection. Management — hygiene, temperature control, disinfection, beneficial microbes — is covered in Lesson 5.3.

Design for failure

Before you build, write down what happens if (1) the pump stops, (2) the power is off for a weekend, (3) a fitting leaks, (4) one plant gets root rot. For the course project: prefer passive systems (Kratky, wick) or add buffer (deeper DWC tanks, oversized reservoirs); put pumps on a timer and check them daily; protect electrical equipment from water with a residual-current device (RCD); and log the solution level so that you notice a leak before the plants do.

Soilless and organic: a regulatory boundary

In the European Union, hydroponic produce cannot be certified organic. Regulation (EU) 2018/848 requires organic crops, except those naturally grown in water, to be "produced in living soil … in connection with the subsoil and bedrock", and explicitly prohibits hydroponic production (Annex II, Part I, points 1.1–1.2). A transitional derogation allows cultivation in "demarcated beds" only on surfaces certified before 28 June 2017 in Finland, Sweden and Denmark, and that derogation expires on 31 December 2031 (point 1.5, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2020/1693). Other jurisdictions have taken different positions; the arguments — naturalness, soil ecology, resource efficiency, consumer expectations — are discussed in Lesson 14.2.

Choosing and sizing the system for your project

In the course project you will grow a leafy crop — typically lettuce or basil — for about five weeks after transplanting, monitor it daily and analyse its growth. The ideal project system is cheap, robust, easy to replicate (you need several identical units for a proper experiment — see Lesson 3.1) and measurable. The Kratky method meets all four criteria; DWC with an aquarium air pump is a good second choice and allows oxygen experiments; NFT is the most instructive hydraulically but needs a pump, careful levelling and a backup plan.

Key idea

A soilless system is a set of buffers and fluxes. Water, nutrients and oxygen flow to the roots from buffers — a tank, a slab, a thin film, the air. Good design matches each buffer to the flux the crop draws from it, and asks what happens when a flux stops. The next three lessons quantify each buffer: nutrients (5.2), oxygen and temperature (5.3), and water flows (5.4).

Practical checklist for the project system: (1) opaque containers and lids (light causes algae); (2) food-safe plastics; (3) net pots with an inert plug (stone wool, coir or foam); (4) a way to measure EC, pH, temperature and — if you can borrow a meter — dissolved oxygen; (5) a ruler or level mark on the tank to log water use; (6) identical replicate units; (7) a written failure plan. The project guide Build your soilless system gives step-by-step building instructions.

Key terms

Hydroponics (solution culture)
Growing plants with their roots in a nutrient solution, with at most a small inert support; coined by Gericke in 1937.
Soilless culture
Any cultivation without soil, including solution culture and substrate culture in inert or low-activity media.
Nutrient film technique (NFT)
Recirculating system in which a thin film of solution flows down sloping channels past the roots.
Deep-water culture (DWC)
Raft or floating system with roots hanging in a deep, aerated solution; chemically and thermally well buffered.
Ebb-and-flow
Periodic flooding and draining of a bench of substrate-filled containers.
Kratky method
Non-circulating hydroponics in which all solution is added at planting and the falling level creates a humid air space for root aeration.
Total porosity
Fraction of the bulk volume of a medium occupied by pores, \(1-\rho_b/\rho_s\).
Air-filled porosity
Fraction of the bulk volume filled with air after saturation and free drainage; depends on container height.
Container capacity
Volumetric water content of a medium after saturation and free drainage (water-holding capacity).
Perched water table
Saturated zone at the base of a freely drained container, above which water content decreases with height.
Cation exchange capacity (CEC)
Amount of exchangeable cations a medium can hold on charged surfaces, expressed in cmol₍c₎ kg⁻¹ or per litre.
Drain fraction
Ratio of drained to supplied solution volume in substrate irrigation.
Open / closed system
Drain-to-waste versus recirculating operation of a soilless system.
Single point of failure
A component whose failure stops the whole system, e.g. a circulation pump in NFT.
