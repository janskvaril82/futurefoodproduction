Why we eat what we eat: food choice and neophobia

Cricket flour has a smaller land footprint than beef, cultivated meat spares animals, and a lettuce from a vertical farm needs almost no pesticides — yet none of this matters if people will not put the food in their mouths. This lesson opens the black box of the eater. You will meet the models psychologists use to explain food choice, learn to measure food neophobia with validated scales, and see why disgust, a feeling of “unnaturalness” and plain unfamiliarity are the biggest hurdles for the foods of the future — and what the Nordic evidence says about getting over them.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the food-choice process model of Furst et al. (1996) and Köster's (2009) multidisciplinary view, and classify determinants of food choice as biological, psychological, social, cultural or economic (LO6).
  • Explain food neophobia, food-technology neophobia, disgust and the naturalness heuristic, and predict how each shapes reactions to insects, cultivated meat, mycoprotein, seaweed and hydroponic produce (LO6).
  • Score the Food Neophobia Scale and the Food Technology Neophobia Scale correctly, including reverse-scored items, and interpret a score against published population data (LO4, LO6).
  • Use simple quantitative tools — expectancy–value attitudes, price elasticity, standard scores and odds from logistic models — to reason about the size of food-choice effects (LO4, LO6).
  • Apply evidence on familiarity, exposure, social norms and culture, including Swedish and Nordic studies, to choose a product format and design the tasting study in your course project (LO6).

A technology that nobody wants to eat is not a solution

Engineers are trained to optimise what can be measured: kilowatt-hours per kilogram, litres of water, kilograms of CO₂-equivalents. The earlier modules of this course did exactly that. But a food system is not a factory that ends at the farm gate; it ends in a mouth. Every new food technology you have studied — insect farming, precision fermentation, cultivated meat, vertical farming — must pass a final test that no life-cycle assessment can simulate: a person has to look at the product, smell it, perhaps read its label, and decide to eat it, and then decide to eat it again next week. Review after review concludes that consumer acceptance, not technical feasibility, is often the rate-limiting step for new foods (Siegrist & Hartmann, 2020; Hartmann & Siegrist, 2017).

Food choice is peculiar among human decisions. We make it many times a day, mostly quickly and habitually, with strong emotions attached, and it is loaded with identity: what we eat says who we are, which group we belong to and what we value. The French sociologist Claude Fischler (1988) called this the omnivore's paradox. As omnivores, humans need variety and are drawn to new foods (neophilia), but every unknown food is also a potential poison, so we are simultaneously wary of it (neophobia). The tension between curiosity and caution is the psychological background against which every novel food is judged.

Big idea

Acceptance of a new food is not a single yes/no decision. It is the outcome of a chain: willingness to try (dominated by neophobia, disgust and expectations), the first tasting experience (dominated by sensory quality), and repeated choice (dominated by price, convenience, habit and identity). A product can fail at any link. This lesson focuses on the first link; Lessons 13.3 and 13.4 teach you to measure the second.

Models of the food-choice process

There is no single “equation of food choice”, but there are useful conceptual models. They serve the same role as a free-body diagram in mechanics: they tell you which forces to look for before you start measuring.

The food-choice process model (Furst et al., 1996)

Tanis Furst and colleagues at Cornell interviewed 29 adults, deliberately chosen for their diversity, about how they chose food in the grocery store. From the interviews they built a process model with three layers (Furst et al., 1996):

  1. The life course — personal history, past experiences, the food environment one grew up in and the transitions one has lived through (leaving home, becoming a parent, moving country). It shapes everything below it.
  2. Influences — five groups of factors that the life course brings into each choice: ideals (cultural expectations of what one ought to eat), personal factors (preferences, needs, health), resources (money, time, skills, equipment), social framework (family, friends, relationships) and the food context (what is available, where and when).
  3. The personal food system — the mental routines a person builds to turn influences into choices, consisting of value negotiations (weighing sensory perceptions, monetary considerations, convenience, health and nutrition, managing relationships and quality) and strategies (habits and rules of thumb, e.g., “I always buy the cheapest bread”, “never meat on weekdays”).

Köster's multidisciplinary critique (2009)

Egon Peter Köster, a sensory psychologist with decades of industry experience, argued that food-choice research has leaned too heavily on explicit, cognitive models in which people report attitudes and intentions that are then assumed to cause behaviour. In practice, he showed, past behaviour, habit and hedonic appreciation are usually better predictors of actual food choice than attitudes and intentions (Köster, 2009). Much food behaviour is driven by implicit learning and memory: we remember whether something was pleasant or unpleasant, often without being able to say why, and we notice change in a familiar food far more readily than we can describe the food itself. Köster grouped the determinants into intrinsic product factors (sensory properties), extrinsic product factors (packaging, price, information, brand), biological and physiological factors, psychological factors (personality, emotions, memory), situational factors and socio-cultural factors — and insisted that they can only be understood together, which requires genuinely interdisciplinary research.

Two practical lessons follow for your project. First, ask what people do, not only what they intend: “Would you buy cricket bread?” over-predicts real purchases. Second, the first tasting matters enormously: a disappointing sensory experience creates a durable implicit memory that no information leaflet can overwrite.

Motives: the Food Choice Questionnaire

To measure why people choose food, Steptoe, Pollard and Wardle (1995) developed the Food Choice Questionnaire (FCQ) through factor analysis of responses from 358 adults, confirmed in a second sample of 358. Nine motives emerged: health, mood, convenience, sensory appeal, natural content, price, weight control, familiarity and ethical concern. The FCQ is still the most used instrument for food-choice motives, and it is a good place to start if you want to profile your tasting panel.

A motive profile becomes predictive when combined with how a person believes a product performs on each attribute. The classic formalisation is the expectancy–value model of attitudes.

Expectancy–value model of attitude (Fishbein)(13.1.1)
\[ A_{o} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} b_i\, e_i \]

the attitude toward an object (for example, “bread made with cricket flour”) equals the sum, over all salient beliefs about it, of how strongly the person believes the object has that attribute multiplied by how good or bad the person finds that attribute.

SymbolMeaningUnit / rangeTypical scale
\(A_o\)attitude toward object \(o\)scale points\(-9m\) … \(+9m\)
\(b_i\)belief strength: subjective likelihood that \(o\) has attribute \(i\)scale points−3 (very unlikely) … +3 (very likely)
\(e_i\)evaluation of attribute \(i\)scale points−3 (very bad) … +3 (very good)
\(m\)number of salient (readily accessible) beliefs–typically 5–9
Where does this come from?
  1. Fishbein (1963) proposed that an attitude is not a free-floating feeling but is built from beliefs linking the object to attributes, each carrying an evaluation.
  2. If you believe cricket bread is “climate-friendly” (\(b = +2\)) and you value climate-friendliness (\(e = +3\)), that belief contributes \(+6\).
  3. A belief can hurt even if it is weak: “probably slightly disgusting” (\(b = +1\)) evaluated as very bad (\(e = -3\)) contributes \(-3\).
  4. Summing assumes that beliefs combine additively and compensate each other — a strong positive can outweigh a weak negative.
  5. The same structure reappears as the “attitude” component of the theory of planned behaviour in Lesson 13.2.
Assumptions and validity

The model is compensatory: it cannot represent “deal-breakers”. Disgust often behaves non-compensatorily — no amount of climate benefit compensates for a food that makes you gag (Rozin & Fallon, 1987). Multiplying two bipolar scales also has known measurement problems (the product of two interval scales is not invariant to where the zero point is placed). Use it as a structured way of thinking, not as a precise predictor.

Units: (scale points) × (scale points) summed over beliefs → a dimensionless attitude index. Only comparisons on the same belief set are meaningful ✓

Reveal the reasoning

Biological, psychological, social, cultural and economic determinants

The models above organise determinants; this section fills them with evidence. It helps to think of the determinants as nested — biology sets the range of possibilities, psychology and learning select within it, society and culture define what counts as food, and economics decides what is affordable today.

Biology. A few preferences are present at birth. Jacob Steiner (1979) photographed the faces of newborns before their first feed: sweet solutions elicited relaxed, “smiling” expressions and bitter ones gaping and rejection. These reflexes make evolutionary sense — sweetness signals energy and bitterness often signals toxins — and they explain why reducing bitterness is a recurring technical challenge for plant-protein isolates and insect flours. Genetics adds individual differences: the ability to taste the bitter compound 6-n-propylthiouracil (PROP) varies widely between people and is linked to how bitter some vegetables taste (Tepper, 2008). In a panel of 24 students, a handful may experience a seaweed snack as considerably more bitter than the rest.

Psychology and learning. Almost everything else about food liking is learned. We learn to like foods that are followed by pleasant consequences (satiety, social warmth) and to dislike foods followed by illness — a single episode of nausea can create a lifelong aversion. The most robust learning effect is simple familiarity, discussed in its own section below. Personality traits such as food neophobia and variety seeking modulate how quickly we approach new foods.

Social and cultural context. Other people are the most powerful food cue there is. Children watch what adults eat; adults eat more in company; and what counts as “food” at all is defined by culture. A locust is a delicacy in parts of Africa and Asia and a pest in Europe; blood pudding is traditional in Sweden and disgusting to many visitors. The food anthropologist's point is that these are not errors of judgement but categories — and categories change slowly.

Economics. For repeated purchase, price and convenience dominate. The standard way to quantify price sensitivity is the elasticity of demand.

Own-price elasticity of demand(13.1.2)
\[ \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta Q/Q}{\Delta P/P} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{Q_1}{Q_0} = \left(\frac{P_1}{P_0}\right)^{\varepsilon} \]

the elasticity is the percentage change in the quantity bought divided by the percentage change in price that caused it. If the elasticity is constant, the ratio of new to old quantity equals the price ratio raised to the power of the elasticity.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(\varepsilon\)own-price elasticity (negative for normal goods)–−0.27 to −0.81 for food groups
\(Q_0, Q_1\)quantity bought before and after the price changekg or units per period
\(P_0, P_1\)price before and afterSEK kg⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Define elasticity in the limit of small changes: \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}Q/Q}{\mathrm{d}P/P} = \dfrac{\mathrm{d}\ln Q}{\mathrm{d}\ln P}\).
  2. If \(\varepsilon\) is constant, integrate: \(\ln Q_1 - \ln Q_0 = \varepsilon(\ln P_1 - \ln P_0)\).
  3. Exponentiate: \(Q_1/Q_0 = (P_1/P_0)^{\varepsilon}\). This is the constant-elasticity (log-log) demand curve.
  4. For small changes, \(\Delta Q/Q \approx \varepsilon\,\Delta P/P\) (the linear approximation). For a 20 % price change the two forms differ by 2–3 percentage points.
Assumptions and validity

Andreyeva, Long and Brownell (2010) reviewed 160 studies and found mean elasticities between 0.27 and 0.81 in absolute value, with food away from home, soft drinks, juice and meats most responsive (0.7–0.8). These are averages for established categories; the elasticity for a new product with few loyal buyers is likely larger, and cross-price effects (switching between meat and meat alternatives) are not captured by the own-price elasticity.

\(\varepsilon = \dfrac{\text{kg}/\text{kg}}{\text{SEK}/\text{SEK}}\) — dimensionless, so elasticities can be compared across currencies and units ✓
Worked example 2What does a price premium cost in sales?

A pea-protein mince sells 1 000 packs per week at 49 SEK. Assume its own-price elasticity is −0.7 (the range reported for meats). (a) The producer must raise the price by 25 % because of higher protein-isolate costs. (b) Alternatively, a retailer campaign cuts the price by 20 %. Estimate weekly sales in both cases with the linear approximation and with constant elasticity.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Linear: \(\Delta Q/Q \approx -0.7\times(+25\,\%) = -17.5\,\%\) → 825 packs.
  2. (a) Constant elasticity: \(Q_1/Q_0 = 1.25^{-0.7} = e^{-0.7\ln 1.25} = e^{-0.1562} = 0.855\) → 855 packs (−14.5 %).
  3. (b) Linear: \(-0.7\times(-20\,\%) = +14\,\%\) → 1 140 packs.
  4. (b) Constant elasticity: \(0.80^{-0.7} = e^{0.1562} = 1.169\) → 1 169 packs (+16.9 %).
  5. Revenue check. Because \(|\varepsilon| < 1\), revenue moves with price: (a) \(855\times61.25 = 52\,370\) SEK versus \(49\,000\) SEK before; (b) \(1169\times39.20 = 45\,820\) SEK.
  6. Sanity check. The two methods differ by ≈3 percentage points for these large changes — negligible compared with the uncertainty in \(\varepsilon\) itself.

Food neophobia and food-technology neophobia

Food neophobia is the reluctance to eat, or the avoidance of, new foods (Pliner & Hobden, 1992; Dovey et al., 2008). It is a trait: a stable disposition that differs between people and can be measured with a questionnaire. It is distinct from picky or fussy eating, which is the rejection of a large number of familiar foods as well (Dovey et al., 2008).

The Food Neophobia Scale (FNS)

Patricia Pliner and Karen Hobden (1992) developed the ten-item Food Neophobia Scale, which has since been translated into many languages and used in hundreds of studies. Respondents rate their agreement with each statement on a seven-point scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. Five statements are worded in the neophobic direction and five in the neophilic direction; the latter are reverse-scored so that a high total always means more neophobia.

Food Neophobia Scale score with reverse-scored items(13.1.3)
\[ \text{FNS} = \sum_{i\in N} x_i + \sum_{i\in R}\left(8 - x_i\right) = S_N + 40 - S_R \]

the FNS total equals the sum of the raw answers to the five neophobic items plus, for each of the five neophilic items, eight minus the raw answer. Because there are five reversed items, this is the same as the neophobic sum plus forty minus the neophilic sum.

Why “8 minus”? Follow the derivation
  1. Reverse-scoring must map the scale onto itself in the opposite order: 1 ↔ 7, 2 ↔ 6, 3 ↔ 5 and 4 ↔ 4.
  2. A linear map \(x' = a - x\) does this if \(1' = 7\), so \(a = 8\). In general \(x' = (\min + \max) - x\); for a 5-point scale that is \(6 - x\).
  3. Sum the five reversed items: \(\sum_{i\in R}(8-x_i) = 5\times8 - S_R = 40 - S_R\).
  4. Check the limits: a maximally neophobic person answers 7 on every neophobic item and 1 on every neophilic item: \(35 + 40 - 5 = 70\) ✓. The opposite gives \(5 + 40 - 35 = 10\) ✓.
Assumptions and validity

Summing assumes that the items measure one underlying trait (unidimensionality) with similar weight. Factor analyses often find two correlated factors (e.g., interest in new foods versus fear of unknown foods; Tuorila et al., 2001), and a cross-national confirmatory analysis recommended dropping two items for comparisons between the US, Sweden and Finland (Ritchey et al., 2003). Always check internal consistency (Cronbach's α, Lesson 13.2) in your own sample; forgetting to reverse the five items produces a negative α — a tell-tale error.

Units: every term is in scale points, and the constant 40 = 5 items × 8 points, so FNS is in points (10–70) ✓

A single FNS score means little on its own; it needs a reference population. The simplest comparison is a standard score.

Standard score and percentile against a reference population(13.1.4)
\[ z = \frac{x - \mu_{\text{ref}}}{\sigma_{\text{ref}}}, \qquad \text{percentile} \approx 100\,\Phi(z) \]

the standard score is how many reference standard deviations the individual's score lies above the reference mean; if the reference scores are roughly normally distributed, the percentage of the reference population scoring lower is the standard normal cumulative probability of z.

Assumptions and validity

FNS scores are bounded (10–70) and often right-skewed, so the normal approximation is poor in the tails. Reference values differ between countries and samples: studies summarised by Szakály et al. (2021) report means of roughly 25–27 (SD 7.5–9.1) in Swedish samples, 34–38 (SD 10.5–11.4) in Finnish samples and 39.8 (SD 12.6) in a Hungarian sample. A percentile is only meaningful relative to a stated reference.

A panellist in your cricket-bread study answers the ten FNS items (in order) with 5, 2, 3, 6, 2, 5, 2, 4, 3, 6. Compute the FNS score and express it as a standard score relative to a Swedish reference (\(\mu \approx 26\), \(\sigma \approx 8.3\)) and a Finnish reference (\(\mu \approx 36\), \(\sigma \approx 11\)).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Reverse items 1, 4, 6, 9, 10: \(8-5 = 3\), \(8-6 = 2\), \(8-5 = 3\), \(8-3 = 5\), \(8-6 = 2\); their sum is 15 (equivalently \(40 - S_R = 40 - 25 = 15\)).

  4. Swedish reference: \(z = (28-26)/8.3 = 0.24\) → \(\Phi(0.24) \approx 0.60\): about the 60th percentile.

Food-technology neophobia (FTNS)

Food neophobia concerns unfamiliar foods. Many future foods, however, are familiar products made in unfamiliar ways: a burger made from cultured cells, milk proteins from precision fermentation, lettuce from a windowless vertical farm. Cox and Evans (2008) therefore developed the Food Technology Neophobia Scale: 13 items on a seven-point scale, grouped into four factors — new food technologies are unnecessary, perception of risks, healthy choices and information/media. Nine items are phrased sceptically (e.g., “The benefits of new food technologies are often grossly overstated”, “It can be risky to switch to new food technologies too quickly”) and four positively (items 10–13, e.g., “New food technologies give people more control over their food choices”, “The media usually provides a balanced and unbiased view of new food technologies”); the four positive items are reverse-scored, giving totals from 13 to 91, where high means more technology-neophobic. FNS and FTNS correlate only moderately: a person may love exotic street food and still distrust “lab” food, or the reverse.

For your project

Include the FNS (and, for technology-based products such as vertical-farm lettuce or mycoprotein, the FTNS) in your panel questionnaire. It costs two minutes and lets you (1) describe your panel honestly — students are usually less neophobic than the general population — and (2) test whether liking of the novel sample depends on neophobia. Remember that the scale should be answered before tasting, so that the tasting does not influence it.

How neophobia varies with age, gender and education

In a representative Finnish sample (n = 1 083), FNS scores decreased with increasing education and degree of urbanisation, men were more neophobic than women, and the oldest group (66–80 years) was more neophobic than the other age groups; high-neophobic respondents were less likely to have tasted the rated foods and less willing to try unfamiliar ones (Tuorila et al., 2001). In Swedish families with children aged 2–17 years, overall neophobia was relatively low, children (especially boys) were more neophobic than their parents, neophobia tended to decrease with age among the children, and the more neophobic the mother and child, the fewer uncommon foods had been served in the family (Koivisto & Sjödén, 1996). In early childhood neophobia is strongest; reviews emphasise that early-life exposure is the most promising way to attenuate it (Dovey et al., 2008).

How large is the effect of neophobia on actual adoption? A Belgian study of 368 consumers used logistic regression to predict who was “ready to adopt insects as a substitute for meat” (Verbeke, 2015). Its results are best read through the logistic model.

Odds and odds ratios in a logistic model(13.1.5)
\[ \ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{OR} = e^{\beta_1},\qquad p_1 = \frac{o_0\,\text{OR}^{\Delta x}}{1 + o_0\,\text{OR}^{\Delta x}},\; o_0 = \frac{p_0}{1-p_0} \]

the natural logarithm of the odds of adoption is a straight-line function of the predictor; each one-unit increase in the predictor multiplies the odds by the odds ratio, and a probability is recovered by dividing the odds by one plus the odds.

\(p_0\)baseline probability of adoption–0.128 (males, other predictors at mean; Verbeke, 2015)
\(o_0\)baseline odds \(p_0/(1-p_0)\)–0.147

Follow the derivation
  1. Probabilities are bounded (0–1), so a straight line in \(p\) would eventually predict impossible values. Odds \(o = p/(1-p)\) run from 0 to ∞ and log-odds from −∞ to +∞, which a straight line can model.
  2. From \(\ln o = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\): \(o(x+1)/o(x) = e^{\beta_0+\beta_1(x+1)}/e^{\beta_0+\beta_1 x} = e^{\beta_1}\) — the odds ratio.
  3. After a change \(\Delta x\): \(o_1 = o_0\,\text{OR}^{\Delta x}\). Invert the odds: \(p_1 = o_1/(1+o_1)\).
Assumptions and validity

The odds ratio is constant, but the change in probability is not: the same odds ratio changes a probability of 0.5 much more than one of 0.05. The neophobia score in Verbeke (2015) was measured on that study's own scale, so the numbers are illustrative of the size of the effect and must not be transferred to FNS totals of 10–70.

Odds and odds ratios are ratios of probabilities — dimensionless ✓

Reveal the reasoning

Disgust and the insect problem

Neophobia is a fear of the unknown. Disgust is different: it is a strong aversion to something that is known and judged offensive. Rozin and Fallon (1987) defined core disgust as revulsion at the prospect of oral incorporation of an offensive object, and identified its key features: the object is perceived as contaminating (brief contact with something disgusting makes an otherwise acceptable food disgusting), the reaction is emotional rather than based on beliefs about danger, and many disgust elicitors are animals or animal products. Two consequences matter for novel foods:

  • Contagion. “Once in contact, always in contact.” A soup stirred with a sterilised comb is still rejected. For insects, the mere knowledge that an ingredient comes from an insect can contaminate the whole product.
  • Disgust is resistant to information. Because disgust does not rest on risk beliefs, explaining that crickets are safe and nutritious does little. Disgust sensitivity can be measured, for example with the Food Disgust Scale (Hartmann & Siegrist, 2018), and it predicts rejection of insects and cultivated meat.

Yet insects are not disgusting everywhere. The FAO estimates that insects supplement the diets of about two billion people and that more than 1 900 species have been used as food (van Huis et al., 2013). Cross-cultural comparisons show how learned the reaction is. German consumers (n = 502) rated insect products less favourably than Chinese consumers (n = 443) on taste, nutrition, familiarity and social acceptance, and Germans accepted processed insect foods more readily than unprocessed ones; in both countries low neophobia, positive taste expectations, social acceptance and previous insect eating predicted willingness to eat (Hartmann et al., 2015). Focus groups in Thailand and the Netherlands showed that cultural exposure creates expectations about which species are appropriate to eat and how they should be prepared, while individual experience determines whether people judge from memories of eating or from the visual properties and associations of the insect (Tan et al., 2015).

Social context can override personal disgust. In a Danish study with 189 college participants, trait-level pathogen disgust and perceived infectability did not consistently predict insect-eating disgust, whereas the perceived norm of insect eating was a significant predictor of actually tasting insects (Jensen & Lieberoth, 2019). The title of the paper says it all: “We will eat disgusting foods together.”

Common misconception

“If we just explain the benefits of insects, people will accept them.” Disgust and naturalness judgements are affective and partly moral; they are not corrected by nutritional facts. Knowledge about benefits helps people who are already on the fence, but for strongly disgusted consumers the effective levers are product form (familiar carrier, invisible insect), social norms (tasting with others who eat) and repeated exposure — not leaflets. Lesson 13.2 shows that information can even backfire.

The naturalness heuristic

If you ask European consumers what they want from food, “natural” is near the top of every list. A systematic review of 72 studies from 32 countries covering 85 348 consumers concluded that for the majority of consumers food naturalness is crucial, across countries and survey years (Román et al., 2017). The review also found that naturalness is judged along three dimensions: how the food was grown (origin), how it was produced (technologies and ingredients) and the properties of the final product.

Paul Rozin's experiments show that naturalness is judged mostly by process history, not by chemical content (Rozin, 2005). Participants rated natural entities before and after transformations. Contagion again explained a lot — contact with something unnatural reduced naturalness — and chemical transformations reduced perceived naturalness much more than physical ones; mixing like with like barely mattered; inserting a gene from another species produced the largest drop, whereas domestication, which changes genotype and phenotype enormously, was seen as much less damaging.

Naturalness judgements differ between cultures: in a comparison of European and American respondents, the meanings and importance attached to “natural” differed between the countries studied (Rozin et al., 2012). For novel food technologies, perceived naturalness is a major predictor of acceptance: in Swiss studies the perceived naturalness of food additives and of cultured meat predicted their acceptance (Siegrist & Sütterlin, 2017), and low acceptance of cultured meat was explained by its being perceived as unnatural — informing participants about how cultured meat is produced and its benefits even had the paradoxical effect of increasing acceptance of traditional meat (Siegrist et al., 2018).

Key idea — a heuristic, not an analysis

“Natural = good” is a mental shortcut. It saves effort and is often right, but it ignores quantities: botulinum toxin and deathcap mushrooms are natural; the vitamin C in a tablet is chemically identical to that in an orange. For a technology such as vertical farming, which can be framed either as “high-tech, artificial” or as “clean, pesticide-free, local”, the naturalness heuristic means that framing — the subject of Lesson 13.2 — can decide acceptance.

Familiarity, mere exposure and variety seeking

The single most reliable way to make people like a food is to let them eat it repeatedly. Zajonc (1968) showed that mere repeated exposure to a stimulus — a word, a face, a melody — increases liking for it, even without any reward. Food is no exception. Two-year-olds' preferences for novel fruits and cheeses rose with the number of times they had tasted them (Birch & Marlin, 1982). In a randomised trial in three London primary schools, children offered a taste of sweet red pepper in eight daily sessions significantly increased both liking (p = 0.006) and consumption (p = 0.03) compared with a control group, whereas rewarding children with stickers for eating the pepper gave an intermediate, non-significant result (Wardle et al., 2003).

For planning, it helps to describe an exposure effect with a simple saturating curve. The equation below is a descriptive tool — the same form describes any quantity that approaches a plateau — not a law of psychology.

A descriptive exposure (learning) curve(13.1.6)
\[ L(n) = L_{\infty} - \left(L_{\infty} - L_0\right) e^{-n/\tau} \]

liking after n tastings equals the plateau liking minus the initial gap to the plateau, shrunk by an exponential factor that decays with the number of exposures; tau is the number of exposures needed to close about 63 % of the gap.

Follow the derivation
  1. Assume each exposure closes a constant fraction of the remaining gap to the plateau: \(\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}n = (L_\infty - L)/\tau\).
  2. Separate variables: \(\mathrm{d}L/(L_\infty - L) = \mathrm{d}n/\tau\) → \(-\ln(L_\infty - L) = n/\tau + C\).
  3. With \(L(0) = L_0\): \(L_\infty - L = (L_\infty - L_0)e^{-n/\tau}\), which rearranges to Eq. 13.1.6.
  4. After \(n = \tau\) exposures, \(1 - e^{-1} = 63\,\%\) of the gap is closed; after \(3\tau\), 95 %.
Assumptions and validity

The parameters are illustrative, not estimates from a specific study. Real exposure effects depend on the food, the age of the eater and the sensory quality: exposure increases liking for foods that are merely unfamiliar, but repeated exposure to an intensely bitter or badly made product can instead produce boredom or aversion. Fit the curve to your own data if you run a repeated-tasting study.

\(n/\tau\) is exposures/exposures, dimensionless, so the exponential is dimensionless and \(L\) keeps the units of \(L_0\) and \(L_\infty\) ✓

With the illustrative values, eight exposures raise liking from 4.0 to \(6.5 - 2.5e^{-2} = 6.16\). The practical implication for your project is sobering: a single tasting session measures first-exposure liking, which for a truly novel product underestimates long-term liking. Say so in your report.

The counterforce to familiarity is variety seeking — the tendency to switch between foods for the sake of change, measured for foods by the VARSEEK scale (Van Trijp & Steenkamp, 1992). Variety seekers and people low in neophobia are the natural early adopters of new foods. Fischler's omnivore's paradox reappears here: familiarity makes food safe, variety makes it interesting, and every eater balances the two.

Cross-cultural, age and Nordic evidence

Where does Sweden stand? In a confirmatory factor analysis of representative samples from the United States, Sweden and Finland, the Swedish respondents were generally more willing to try novel foods than those from the US and Finland (Ritchey et al., 2003), and later studies summarised by Szakály et al. Three patterns stand out. Frontier 2024–2026

As of 2026, several edible insects are authorised as novel foods in the EU (for example dried Tenebrio molitor larva since 2021 and partially defatted house-cricket powder since 2023), whereas no cultivated meat product has yet been authorised in the EU, although applications have been submitted since 2024. Consumer research on cultivated meat is therefore still largely about intentions towards products that respondents have never tasted — keep this limitation in mind when you read survey results, and see Lesson 14.1 for the regulatory side.

From theory to your tasting study

Everything in this lesson has a direct design consequence for the sensory study in your course project (project part: sensory study of a novel food):

  1. Choose the product format deliberately. A familiar carrier (bread, cookie, pasta) lowers neophobic rejection; decide whether your research question is about the ingredient (then keep the carrier identical and vary only the ingredient) or about acceptance of the concept (then the visible form is the treatment).
  2. Measure the panel. Administer the FNS (and FTNS for technology-based products) before tasting. Report the mean, SD and Cronbach's α, and compare with published references.
  3. Separate blind and informed conditions. Information about the ingredient (“contains cricket flour”, “grown hydroponically”) activates disgust, naturalness and neophobia. A blind tasting measures sensory liking; an informed tasting measures liking plus expectation. Both are legitimate — but they answer different questions (see Lesson 13.3 on expectation bias).
  4. Mind the social setting. Tasting in a group with visible peers changes behaviour (Jensen & Lieberoth, 2019). Use individual booths if you want individual judgements.
  5. Interpret first-exposure liking honestly. A single session underestimates the liking that repeated exposure might produce.

Check your understanding

Key terms

Food choice
The process by which people select, acquire and consume foods, shaped by life-course experiences, influences and personal food systems.
Omnivore's paradox
The tension between attraction to new foods (neophilia) and fear of them (neophobia) that follows from being an omnivore (Fischler, 1988).
Food neophobia
A stable trait: reluctance to eat, or avoidance of, new foods; measured with the ten-item Food Neophobia Scale (range 10–70).
Food technology neophobia
Fear or distrust of foods produced with new technologies; measured with the 13-item FTNS (range 13–91).
Reverse-scored item
A questionnaire item worded in the opposite direction to the construct; recoded as (min + max) − x before summing.
Disgust
Revulsion at the prospect of oral incorporation of an offensive object, characterised by contamination sensitivity (Rozin & Fallon, 1987).
Contagion
The belief that brief contact transfers the properties of a disgusting or unnatural entity to another object.
Naturalness heuristic
The mental shortcut that natural foods are better, judged mainly from process history rather than composition.
Mere exposure effect
Increased liking for a stimulus caused only by repeated exposure to it (Zajonc, 1968).
Variety seeking
The tendency to switch between foods for the sake of change; measured for foods by the VARSEEK scale.
Expectancy–value attitude
An attitude computed as the sum of belief strengths multiplied by attribute evaluations, \(A = \sum b_i e_i\).
Price elasticity of demand
Percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price; typically −0.3 to −0.8 for foods.
Odds ratio
The factor by which the odds of an outcome change for a one-unit increase in a predictor in a logistic model.
