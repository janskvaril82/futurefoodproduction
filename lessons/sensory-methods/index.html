Sensory evaluation methods

A novel food succeeds or fails in the mouth. Does bread baked with 10 % cricket flour taste different from ordinary bread? Is hydroponic lettuce as crisp as soil-grown lettuce? Would students choose a seaweed snack over a potato crisp? Instruments can measure salt content, colour or breaking force, but only people can tell you what a food tastes like and whether they like it. Sensory evaluation is the discipline that turns human judges into calibrated measuring instruments. In this lesson you will learn how the senses build flavour, how to choose the right test for the question you are asking, how to set up a tasting that meets international standards, how to protect your results against the biases of human judgement — and how to run the whole study ethically and legally.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain how taste, orthonasal and retronasal smell, chemesthesis, texture, sound and colour combine into flavour, and use Weber's and Stevens' laws to reason quantitatively about perceived intensity (LO4).
  • Choose between discrimination, descriptive, affective and temporal methods for a given question, and describe the procedures of the triangle, duo–trio, 2-AFC and tetrad tests, QDA, flash profile, the 9-point hedonic scale, JAR scales, CATA, preference ranking and TDS (LO4, LO6).
  • Calculate chance performance with the binomial distribution and convert a proportion of correct answers into the signal-detection index d′ (LO4).
  • Specify a test environment and sample preparation that follow ISO 8589 and ISO 4120: booths, lighting, three-digit codes, balanced serving orders, portions, temperature and palate cleansers (LO4).
  • Recognise expectation, stimulus, halo, contrast, position and other biases, and design controls against them (LO4).
  • Plan an ethically and legally sound tasting study: informed consent, allergens, novel-food status of samples, anonymity and the GDPR, and the Swedish rules on ethical review of student projects (LO6).

People as measuring instruments

A widely quoted definition from the Institute of Food Technologists describes sensory evaluation as “a scientific discipline used to evoke, measure, analyse and interpret reactions to those characteristics of foods and materials as they are perceived by the senses of sight, smell, taste, touch and hearing” (quoted in Lawless & Heymann, 2010). Each verb in that sentence is a design decision you will make in your project. You evoke a reaction by presenting samples under controlled conditions, so that the only thing that differs between them is the thing you want to study. You measure the reaction as a number — a count of correct answers, a rating, a tick in a box, a rank. You analyse the numbers with statistics that respect how they were generated (Lesson 13.4). And you interpret them in the light of who your judges were and what they were told.

Human judges are extraordinary instruments. A trained panel can detect off-flavours at concentrations far below what many laboratory analyses can quantify, and no instrument can measure liking. But people are also noisy, easily tired, influenced by what they see and read, and different from one another. Sensory science is the craft of getting reliable numbers out of such instruments. Its central idea is simple: ask one clear question per test, and choose the test that answers exactly that question. There are three basic questions, and each has its own family of methods:

  1. Is there a perceptible difference? — discrimination tests such as the triangle test. Screened (not necessarily trained) assessors, typically 24–30 of them when testing for a difference (ISO 4120:2021).
  2. What is the difference, and how large is it? — descriptive analysis, from classical quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) with a trained panel to rapid methods such as check-all-that-apply (CATA) with consumers. Temporal methods add the dimension of time.
  3. Is it liked, or preferred? — affective (hedonic) tests with untrained consumers from the target group, such as the 9-point hedonic scale and preference tests.
Key idea — one question per test

Do not ask “Which sample is different — and which do you prefer?” in the same triangle test. Having just picked the odd sample, assessors are biased in any follow-up question, which is why ISO 4120:2021 forbids questions about preference, acceptance or degree of difference after the choice. Run a separate test for each question.

For the course project (sensory study of a novel food) you will typically combine two or three of these families: for example, a triangle test to find out whether your hydroponic lettuce is perceptibly different from supermarket soil-grown lettuce, a CATA question to describe how bread with cricket flour differs from wheat bread, and a 9-point hedonic scale with just-about-right questions to find out whether students like it and what to change. The rest of this lesson gives you the building blocks; Lesson 13.4 shows how to design and analyse the complete study.

How the senses build flavour

When you eat a slice of cricket bread you do not experience a “taste”, a “smell” and a “texture” separately. The brain fuses them into a single percept, flavour, that feels as if it were located in the mouth (Small, 2012; Spence, 2015). To design tests, however, you must know which sense carries which information, because each can be controlled, masked or biased separately.

Taste: five qualities on the tongue

Taste proper (gustation) is detected by receptor cells in taste buds on the tongue, the soft palate and the throat. There are five generally accepted basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Sweet and umami are detected by heterodimers of the T1R receptor family (T1R2 + T1R3 for sweet, T1R1 + T1R3 for umami), bitterness by a family of a few dozen T2R receptors, and appetitive saltiness by the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) (Chandrashekar et al., 2006). Experiments in mice identified the proton channel OTOP1 as the sensor for sour taste (Teng et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019). The famous “tongue map”, with sweet on the tip and bitter at the back, is a myth: all five qualities can be detected wherever there are taste buds (Chandrashekar et al., 2006).

For future foods, bitterness is the recurring enemy: many plant-protein isolates, insect flours and some seaweeds carry bitter compounds, and humans are born disliking bitterness (Lesson 13.1). Umami is often a friend: the glutamate in kombu seaweed is the classic umami stimulus. Salt is a target of reformulation, which is why the psychophysics of intensity (below) matters.

Smell: two routes to the same receptors

Humans have 339 intact odorant-receptor genes (Malnic et al., 2004), and each odorant activates a combination of receptors — a combinatorial code that lets us distinguish an enormous number of odours (Buck & Axel, 1991). Odour molecules reach the olfactory epithelium at the roof of the nasal cavity by two routes (Figure 2). Orthonasal smell is sniffing: volatiles from the outside world enter through the nostrils. Retronasal smell happens while you chew and swallow: volatiles released in the mouth are pumped up through the back of the throat (nasopharynx) to the same epithelium. Rozin (1982) called this the “duality of the olfactory sense”: the same odour can be perceived differently by the two routes, and retronasal odours are referred to the mouth and experienced as “taste”. That is why food tastes of little when you have a cold, and why most of what people call the “taste” of a food is in fact retronasal smell. Try it: pinch your nose, eat a fruit-flavoured jelly bean, and notice that you can tell it is sweet and sour — but not which fruit it is until you release your nose.

Chemesthesis: the trigeminal sense

The burn of chilli, the cooling of mint and the sting of mustard or wasabi are neither tastes nor smells. They are chemesthetic sensations carried by the trigeminal nerve, whose free endings in the mouth and nose carry ion channels of the TRP family: TRPV1 responds to capsaicin and to heat (Caterina et al., 1997), TRPM8 to menthol and to cold (McKemy et al., 2002), and TRPA1 to the isothiocyanates of mustard oil (Jordt et al., 2004). Chemesthetic sensations build up with repeated exposure and linger; a spicy insect snack served before a mild one will distort the second judgement. Plan longer rest intervals, or fewer samples per session, when products are pungent.

Texture and sound

Szczesniak (2002) insisted that texture is a sensory property: the way the structural, mechanical and surface properties of a food are perceived through vision, hearing, touch and kinaesthesis (the sense of movement of the jaw). A texture analyser measures force and deformation; only people perceive “crisp”, “chewy” or “dry”. Sound is part of texture: when Zampini and Spence (2004) played back the sound of participants biting into potato crisps louder, or amplified only the high frequencies (2–20 kHz), the same crisps were rated both crisper and fresher. For a seaweed snack or a lettuce leaf, the crunch you hear is part of the product.

Colour and appearance

Vision comes first and sets expectations. In a famous experiment, 54 oenology students described a white wine that had been coloured red with an odourless dye using the vocabulary of red wines (Morrot et al., 2001). Earlier, DuBose, Cardello and Maller (1980) showed that masking the colour of fruit-flavoured drinks dramatically reduced correct flavour identification, and that atypical colours produced incorrect flavour responses associated with the colour. A review by Spence et al. (2010) concludes that colour influences both flavour identification and perceived intensity, although the effects vary between studies. For you this has a very practical consequence: adding 10 % cricket flour darkens bread, so in a blind discrimination test assessors could pick the odd sample by eye. Standards therefore recommend masking irrelevant colour differences with coloured light or subdued illumination (ISO 8589:2007; ISO 4120:2021).

From stimulus to sensation: thresholds and psychophysics

Psychophysics describes how perceived intensity depends on the physical stimulus. Three thresholds are defined. The detection (absolute) threshold is the lowest concentration at which a stimulus can be detected; the recognition threshold is the lowest concentration at which it can be identified (“salty”); and the difference threshold or just-noticeable difference (JND) is the smallest change in concentration that can be detected. Because detection is probabilistic, thresholds are defined statistically — for example, the concentration detected on 50 % of occasions after correcting for guessing.

Weber's law and the just-noticeable difference(13.3.1)
\[ \frac{\Delta I_{\text{JND}}}{I} = k \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \Delta I_{\text{JND}} = k\,I \]

the smallest detectable change in a stimulus is a constant fraction of the stimulus itself: the stronger the stimulus, the larger the change needed before anyone notices it.

Follow the derivation — from Weber to Fechner
  1. Observation. Ernst Heinrich Weber (1834) found that the smallest detectable difference between two lifted weights was roughly proportional to the weights themselves: \(\Delta I/I\) was constant.
  2. Fechner's assumption. Gustav Fechner (1860) assumed that every JND corresponds to the same increment of sensation \(\Delta\psi\). Then \(\Delta\psi = c\,\Delta I/I\) for a constant \(c\).
  3. Integrate. Letting the steps become infinitesimal, \(\mathrm{d}\psi = c\,\mathrm{d}I/I\), so \(\psi = c\ln I + C\).
  4. Fix the constant. Sensation is zero at the absolute threshold \(I_0\): \(\psi = c\ln(I/I_0)\) — Fechner's logarithmic law.
  5. Consequence. Equal ratios of stimulus give equal steps of sensation: doubling salt from 0.5 to 1 g adds as much saltiness as doubling from 1 to 2 g.
Assumptions and validity

Weber's law is an empirical approximation that holds best in the middle of the usable range; near the absolute threshold the Weber fraction rises. Weber fractions differ between senses, between products (salt dissolved in water versus salt on a crisp) and between people, so values from the literature are only a starting point — measure the JND in your own product with a discrimination test. Fechner's “equal JNDs are equal sensations” assumption was later challenged by Stevens (Eq. 13.3.2).

\(\Delta I\) and \(I\) have the same unit, so \(k\) is dimensionless and \(\Delta I_{\text{JND}} = k I\) has the unit of \(I\) ✓

Stanley Smith Stevens replaced Fechner's indirect construction with a direct measurement: he asked people to assign numbers proportional to the perceived magnitude of stimuli (magnitude estimation) and found that sensation grows as a power of the stimulus, with an exponent characteristic of each sensory continuum.

Stevens' power law of perceived intensity(13.3.2)
\[ \psi = k\,\varphi^{\,n} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\psi_2}{\psi_1} = \left(\frac{\varphi_2}{\varphi_1}\right)^{n}, \qquad \log\psi = \log k + n\log\varphi \]

perceived intensity equals a constant times the stimulus raised to a power; the ratio of two sensations therefore equals the ratio of the two stimuli raised to that power, and on log–log axes the relationship is a straight line whose slope is the exponent.

Follow the derivation — why a power function?
  1. Stevens' empirical principle: equal stimulus ratios produce equal sensation ratios. Multiplying the stimulus by a factor \(\lambda\) multiplies the sensation by a factor \(g(\lambda)\) that does not depend on where you start: \(\psi(\lambda\varphi) = g(\lambda)\,\psi(\varphi)\).
  2. Take logarithms: with \(u = \ln\varphi\) and \(h(u) = \ln\psi\), the condition becomes \(h(u + \ln\lambda) = \ln g(\lambda) + h(u)\): shifting \(u\) by a constant shifts \(h\) by a constant.
  3. The only continuous functions with this property are straight lines, \(h(u) = n u + c\) (a form of Cauchy's functional equation).
  4. Transform back: \(\ln\psi = n\ln\varphi + c\), i.e., \(\psi = e^{c}\varphi^{n} = k\varphi^{n}\).
  5. For two stimuli, \(k\) cancels: \(\psi_2/\psi_1 = (\varphi_2/\varphi_1)^n\) — the form you need for reformulation questions.
Assumptions and validity

Exponents were measured with magnitude estimation, mostly on simple solutions (Stevens, 1957, 1975); they depend on the method, the range of stimuli and the food matrix, and differ between individuals. Near the threshold a better form is \(\psi = k(\varphi - \varphi_0)^n\). Use the law for reasoning about direction and size of effects, then confirm with a discrimination or descriptive test on the real product.

In the ratio form both sides are dimensionless ratios; \(k\) absorbs whatever units \(\varphi\) has ✓

Your group's seaweed crisps contain 1.6 g NaCl per 100 g. You would like to cut salt by 30 %. (a) Using Stevens' exponent for NaCl (\(n = 1.4\)), predict the change in perceived saltiness. (b) What is the largest cut that keeps perceived saltiness within 10 % of the original? (c) How would you check the prediction?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Stimulus ratio. A 30 % cut means \(\varphi_2/\varphi_1 = 0.70\) (1.12 g per 100 g).
  2. Sensation ratio from Eq. 13.3.2: \(\psi_2/\psi_1 = 0.70^{1.4} = e^{1.4\ln 0.70} = e^{1.4\times(-0.3567)} = e^{-0.4993} = 0.607\). Perceived saltiness falls by about 39 % — more than the 30 % cut, because the exponent is larger than 1.
  3. (b) Invert the law. We need \(\psi_2/\psi_1 \ge 0.90\), so \(\varphi_2/\varphi_1 \ge 0.90^{1/1.4} = e^{\ln 0.90/1.4} = e^{-0.07526} = 0.9275\). The largest cut is therefore about 7 % (to 1.48 g per 100 g).

Successive small reductions, each below the difference threshold, are how salt is reduced “by stealth”.
  5. Sanity check. With \(n = 1\) a 30 % cut would give exactly 30 % less saltiness; with \(n > 1\) the loss is larger, with \(n < 1\) smaller. ✓

Discrimination tests: is there a difference?

Discrimination tests answer the most basic question of product development: can people tell the products apart? They are used when a process, supplier or ingredient changes — replacing 10 % of the wheat flour with cricket flour, growing lettuce hydroponically instead of in soil, switching to a cheaper seaweed. All common discrimination tests share three features: assessors receive coded samples, they are forced to choose an answer even if they are unsure, and there is a known probability \(p_0\) of answering correctly by pure guessing. The result is simply the number of correct answers out of \(n\) assessors.

  • Triangle test (ISO 4120:2021). Three samples, two of one product and one of the other; “Which sample is different?” \(p_0 = 1/3\). The attribute that differs need not be known. The six possible arrangements (ABB, AAB, ABA, BAA, BBA, BAB) are distributed at random in groups of six among the assessors.
  • Duo–trio test (ISO 10399:2017). A marked reference and two coded samples; “Which coded sample matches the reference?” \(p_0 = 1/2\). The reference can be constant (always the standard product) or balanced (each product serves as reference for half of the assessors).
  • Paired comparison, 2-AFC (ISO 5495:2005). Two coded samples; a directional question such as “Which sample is more bitter?” \(p_0 = 1/2\). The attribute must be specified in advance.
  • 3-AFC. Three samples, one different; “Which sample is the most bitter?” \(p_0 = 1/3\). Directional, like the 2-AFC.
  • Tetrad test (ASTM E3009-24). Four samples, two of each product; “Group the samples into two pairs of similar samples.” \(p_0 = 1/3\), because only one of the three possible groupings is correct. Unspecified: the attribute need not be known.

How do you decide whether a result is more than guessing? If the products were identical, every assessor would guess, and the number of correct answers would follow the binomial distribution — the same distribution that describes the number of heads in \(n\) tosses of a (loaded) coin.

Chance performance in a forced-choice test: the binomial model(13.3.3)
\[ P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x}\,p_0^{\,x}\,(1-p_0)^{\,n-x}, \qquad E[X] = n\,p_0 \] \[ P(X \ge x) = \sum_{i=x}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\,p_0^{\,i}\,(1-p_0)^{\,n-i} \]

if every assessor is guessing, the probability of exactly x correct answers out of n is the number of ways of choosing which x assessors are correct, times the guessing probability to the power x, times the probability of a wrong guess to the power n minus x. The expected number correct is n times the guessing probability, and the probability of x or more correct answers is the sum of the probabilities from x up to n.

Follow the derivation
  1. One assessor. A guessing assessor is correct with probability \(p_0\) and wrong with probability \(1-p_0\) — a Bernoulli trial.
  2. One particular pattern. If the \(n\) assessors judge independently, a particular sequence with \(x\) correct and \(n-x\) wrong answers (e.g., correct, wrong, correct, …) has probability \(p_0^{\,x}(1-p_0)^{n-x}\) (multiply independent probabilities).
  3. Count the patterns. There are \(\binom{n}{x}\) sequences with exactly \(x\) correct answers, all equally probable. Adding them gives \(P(X=x)\).
  4. Mean. \(X\) is a sum of \(n\) Bernoulli variables, each with mean \(p_0\), so \(E[X] = np_0\); its variance is \(np_0(1-p_0)\).
  5. Tail probability. The evidence against “everyone is guessing” is the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed: \(P(X\ge x)\). This is the one-sided p-value of the exact binomial test, treated in depth in Lesson 13.4.
Assumptions and validity

Independent judgements (no talking; each assessor contributes one answer, or replicates are handled as in Lesson 13.4), a true forced choice (no “no difference” option), and correct randomisation of positions so that no position is favoured. Under the null hypothesis the model is exact, because identical products leave nothing to perceive — everyone is guessing.

All quantities are counts or probabilities — dimensionless ✓
Worked example 2Can 24 students tell cricket bread from wheat bread?

A class of 24 students performs a triangle test with wheat bread and bread containing 10 % cricket flour, served under red light so that the darker crumb cannot be seen. Thirteen students pick the odd sample. (a) How many correct answers would you expect by guessing? (b) What is the probability of 13 or more correct answers if nobody can perceive a difference? (c) What if the same 13/24 had come from a duo–trio test?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Expected by chance: \(E[X] = np_0 = 24\times\tfrac13 = 8\) correct answers, with a standard deviation of \(\sqrt{24\times\tfrac13\times\tfrac23} = 2.31\).
  2. (b) One term as an example: \(P(X=13) = \binom{24}{13}(1/3)^{13}(2/3)^{11} = 2\,496\,144\times 6.27\times10^{-7}\times 1.156\times10^{-2} = 0.0181\).
  3. Sum the tail \(x = 13, 14, \dots, 24\): \(P(X\ge 13) = 0.0181 + 0.0071 + 0.0024 + 0.0007 + 0.0002 + \dots = 0.0284\).
  4. Interpret. If nobody could perceive a difference, a result this good would occur in fewer than 3 of 100 classes. With the conventional α = 0.05 we conclude that a perceptible difference exists. (ISO 4120:2021 Table A.1 gives 13 as the minimum number of correct answers for n = 24 at α = 0.05.)
  5. (c) Duo–trio: with \(p_0 = 1/2\), 13/24 is almost exactly what guessing produces (\(E[X] = 12\)): \(P(X\ge13) = 0.419\). The same count means different things in different tests — always state the method and \(p_0\).

Why tests with the same guessing probability differ in power: signal detection theory

A significant triangle test tells you that a difference exists, but the proportion of correct answers is a poor measure of how large it is, because it depends on the test. Signal detection theory, rooted in Thurstone's (1927) law of comparative judgement, provides a test-independent measure. Each time an assessor tastes a sample, the perceived intensity of the relevant attribute is a random variable: tasting the same bread twice does not produce exactly the same sensation. Model the perceptions of product A as a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1 and those of product B as a normal distribution with mean \(d'\) and the same standard deviation. The distance \(d'\) (“d-prime”), measured in units of the perceptual standard deviation, is the size of the sensory difference (Macmillan & Creelman, 2004).

Signal detection: d′ from hit and false-alarm rates, and from a 2-AFC test(13.3.4)
\[ d' = z(H) - z(F) \] \[ P_c^{\text{2-AFC}} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{\sqrt{2}}\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d' = \sqrt{2}\;\Phi^{-1}\!\left(P_c\right) \]

in a yes/no task, d-prime is the z-score of the hit rate minus the z-score of the false-alarm rate. In a two-alternative forced-choice test, the probability of a correct answer is the standard normal cumulative probability of d-prime divided by the square root of two; turning this around, d-prime equals the square root of two times the inverse normal of the proportion correct.

Follow the derivation for the 2-AFC
  1. The assessor tastes A and B and perceives \(a \sim N(0, 1)\) and \(b \sim N(d', 1)\).
  2. Asked “which is more bitter?”, the rational decision is to choose the sample with the larger perception. The answer is correct if \(b - a > 0\).
  3. The difference of two independent normal variables is normal: \(b - a \sim N(d',\, 1 + 1) = N(d', 2)\), with standard deviation \(\sqrt2\).
  4. Standardise: \(P(b - a > 0) = P\!\left(Z > \dfrac{0 - d'}{\sqrt2}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{d'}{\sqrt2}\right)\).
  5. Invert: \(d' = \sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(P_c)\). At \(P_c = 0.5\) (pure guessing) \(d' = 0\); at \(P_c = 0.76\), \(d' \approx 1\).
Assumptions and validity

Equal-variance normal perceptions, independent tastings, and an assessor who uses the optimal decision rule for the question asked. The same \(d'\) predicts different proportions correct for different tests because the decision rules differ; the formulas for the triangle, duo–trio and tetrad are more complex (Ennis, 1993; Ennis et al., 1998) and are used in Lesson 13.4. \(d'\) is a property of the products and the population of assessors: a trained panel has smaller perceptual noise and therefore larger \(d'\) for the same products.

\(\Phi^{-1}\) returns a z-score (dimensionless), so \(d'\) is in units of standard deviations ✓
Worked example 3The same students, the same breads, two different tests

In a 2-AFC test, 24 of 32 students correctly identify the cricket bread as “more bitter”. (a) Estimate d′. (b) If the same 32 students had done a triangle test on the same breads, how many correct answers would you expect, and would that be significant at α = 0.05?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(P_c = 24/32 = 0.75\). \(\Phi^{-1}(0.75) = 0.6745\), so \(d' = \sqrt2\times0.6745 = 0.954\). The 2-AFC result itself is highly significant: \(P(X\ge24\mid n=32,\,p_0=\tfrac12) = 0.0035\).
  2. (b) Triangle. Inserting \(d' = 0.954\) in the triangle psychometric function (Figure 4; Eq. 13.4.4) gives \(P_c = 0.411\): about \(0.411\times32 = 13\) correct answers.
  3. The critical number for \(n = 32\) at α = 0.05 is 16 (ISO 4120:2021 Table A.1). With 13 correct, \(P(X\ge13\mid n=32,\,p_0=\tfrac13) = 0.24\): not significant. The probability that a triangle test with 32 assessors detects this difference at all (its power) is only about 0.20.
  4. Interpret. The 2-AFC is more powerful because the question directs attention to the attribute that differs, and because comparing two samples is less noisy than finding the odd one of three. This is Frijters' (1979) “paradox of discriminatory non-discriminators”: assessors who fail a triangle test may discriminate perfectly well in a 2-AFC.

Common misconception

“The triangle test was not significant, so the products are the same.” A non-significant difference test only means that you have not shown a difference — perhaps because the difference is small, or because your panel was too small to detect it (Worked example 3). To claim that two products are similar, you must run a similarity test with a pre-specified tolerable difference and usually about twice as many assessors (ISO 4120:2021; Lesson 13.4).

Choosing a discrimination test for your project

If you know which attribute differs (salt reduction → saltiness), a directional 2-AFC or 3-AFC is the most powerful choice. If you do not (hydroponic versus soil lettuce), use a tetrad or triangle test. The tetrad is theoretically much more powerful than the triangle; Ennis (2012) showed that it remains more powerful as long as the extra fourth sample does not reduce the effective d′ by more than about one third. For products that cause strong fatigue, adaptation or carry-over — pungent, fatty or intensely flavoured samples — ASTM E3009-24 notes that methods with fewer samples may be preferable.

Descriptive analysis: what is different, and by how much?

A discrimination test ends with a yes or a no. Product developers usually need more: which attributes changed, in which direction and by how much. Descriptive analysis answers these questions by treating a small, trained panel as a multi-channel measuring instrument that outputs an intensity for every attribute of every product — a sensory profile (ISO 13299:2016).

Quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA)

The QDA method of Stone, Sidel, Oliver, Woolsey and Singleton (1974) remains the template. Its steps are worth learning because every later method modifies one of them:

  1. Screening. Candidates are recruited and screened for their ability to discriminate among products of the relevant type (selection and training are described in ISO 8586:2023).
  2. Language development. In group sessions led by a panel leader, the assessors taste the products and generate the words that describe them; they agree on a consensus vocabulary with a written definition and, where possible, a physical reference for each attribute (“earthy: the aroma of freshly dug soil — reference: a sniff of damp potting soil”).
  3. Scaling. Each assessor, alone in a booth, rates every attribute of every coded sample on an unstructured line scale (in QDA a 15-cm line anchored near each end), and the mark is converted to a number.
  4. Replication and analysis. Each product is evaluated several times, so that the analysis of variance can separate products, assessors, replicates and their interactions, and panel performance (discrimination, repeatability, agreement) can be checked.

The result is often shown as a spider (radar) plot. Typical descriptive panels have 8–12 trained assessors; in a simulation study based on real panel variability, Gacula and Rutenbeck (2006) found that a minimum of about five judges was needed to detect typical differences of one unit on a 15-point scale. Training takes time — commonly many hours of sessions — which is why other methods were invented.

Rapid methods: flash profile, CATA and friends

Since about 2000 a family of faster methods has blurred the line between trained-panel and consumer research (Varela & Ares, 2012). In a flash profile (Dairou & Sieffermann, 2002; Delarue & Sieffermann, 2004) each assessor chooses his or her own attributes and then simply ranks all products on each attribute. Because the vocabularies differ between assessors, the individual rankings are combined with multivariate methods such as generalised Procrustes analysis into a consensus product map. The method needs little training and gives maps comparable to conventional profiles, but it does not produce attribute intensities that can be compared between studies.

In check-all-that-apply (CATA) questions, consumers receive a list of words (for bread: soft, dry, nutty, roasted, earthy, bitter, sweet, off-flavour …) and tick every word they find appropriate for the sample. The data are frequencies — how many consumers ticked “earthy” for each sample — and they are analysed with Cochran's Q test and correspondence analysis (Meyners et al., 2013; Lesson 13.4). The order of the words matters: consumers tick words near the top more often, so the order should be varied between consumers (Ares & Jaeger, 2013). Variants include RATA (rate-all-that-apply, in which ticked words are also rated for intensity), sorting and projective mapping (“napping”) (Varela & Ares, 2012).

For your project

A full QDA is beyond a course project, but two cheaper options work well. (1) A “mini-profile”: six to eight students agree on eight to ten attributes with references in one or two sessions and then rate the samples twice. (2) A CATA question in your consumer test: it costs each consumer about one minute and tells you why a sample is liked or disliked. Choose the words from a pilot tasting, not from your imagination.

Affective tests: is it liked, and which is preferred?

Affective tests measure the hedonic response — liking, preference, acceptance — of people who represent the intended consumers. They are the tests that decide whether a novel food will be eaten, and they are the ones most often run badly: with too few consumers, the wrong consumers, or questions that bias one another.

The 9-point hedonic scale

The 9-point hedonic scale was developed in the 1950s at the US Army Quartermaster Food and Container Institute to measure soldiers' food preferences (Peryam & Pilgrim, 1957). It has nine verbal categories, symmetrical around a neutral point, which are converted to the numbers 1–9 for analysis (Figure 6). Seventy years later it is still the most widely used scale for measuring food liking (Schutz & Cardello, 2001; Lim, 2011).

The scale is not perfect. The psychological distances between the labels are unequal — the step from “like slightly” to “like moderately” is not the same size as the step from “like very much” to “like extremely” — and consumers under-use the end categories, which compresses differences among very well-liked products (Schutz & Cardello, 2001). Wichchukit and O'Mahony (2015) add that verbal labels and purely numerical 9-point scales are not interchangeable, question the routine use of parametric statistics on the category numbers, and advocate ranking with a signal-detection “R-index” as an alternative. The labelled affective magnitude (LAM) scale, a line scale with the hedonic labels placed at empirically measured positions, gave equal reliability and somewhat better discrimination among highly liked foods (Schutz & Cardello, 2001). In practice, the 9-point scale's familiarity, comparability with decades of data and robustness make it the sensible default for a student project — as long as you remember that the means are averages of ordered categories.

In a typical hedonic test, samples are presented one at a time (sequential monadic presentation) in a balanced order, each consumer rates overall liking first — before any attribute question can colour the overall judgement — and every consumer tastes every sample. That last feature makes each consumer a block, the key to the analysis in Lesson 13.4. For a single sample, the mean liking and its confidence interval summarise the data.

Just-about-right scales

Liking tells you whether a product works, not what to change. Just-about-right (JAR) scales ask about the level of a specific attribute relative to the consumer's ideal: “The bitterness of this bread is: much too weak – somewhat too weak – just about right – somewhat too strong – much too strong.” The standard format has five points with “just about right” in the middle (Rothman & Parker, 2009). Combined with liking, JAR data show which deviations from the ideal actually cost liking — the penalty analysis of Lesson 13.4. Three cautions: asking attribute questions can itself change how consumers think about a product; consumers may not know what the “right” level is for a food they have never eaten, which is exactly the situation with novel foods; and JAR questions only make sense for attributes that have an ideal level (“off-flavour” does not).

Preference tests and ranking

Preference tests ask consumers to choose. In a paired preference test, a consumer tastes two coded samples and answers “Which do you prefer?”. Under the null hypothesis of no preference, each sample is chosen with probability 1/2, but — unlike in a 2-AFC difference test — either sample could win, so the test is two-sided (Lesson 13.4). With more than two samples, consumers can rank them from most to least preferred (ranking is standardised in ISO 8587:2006). Ranking is easy for consumers and avoids the scale-use problems of rating, but it says nothing about absolute liking: a consumer who dislikes all three samples still ranks one of them first. Rank data from complete blocks are analysed with the Friedman test (Friedman, 1937).

Friedman rank-sum test for preference ranking(13.3.6)
\[ F_r = \frac{12}{b\,t\,(t+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{t}R_j^{2} \;-\; 3\,b\,(t+1) \] \[ F_r = \frac{12}{b\,t\,(t+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\left(R_j - \frac{b(t+1)}{2}\right)^{2} \;\;\dot\sim\;\; \chi^2_{t-1} \]

the Friedman statistic is twelve divided by the number of panellists times the number of samples times the number of samples plus one, times the sum of the squared rank sums, minus three times the number of panellists times the number of samples plus one. Equivalently, it measures how far the rank sums lie from the value expected if all samples were equally preferred. Under the null hypothesis it approximately follows a chi-square distribution with t minus one degrees of freedom.

Complete blocks (every panellist ranks every sample), independent panellists and no ties (a tie correction exists). Consumers are not a trained panel — and vice versa

Do not ask consumers to rate “earthy intensity” on a 15-cm line scale; they have no shared definition of “earthy” and no training in scale use. Do not ask trained assessors whether they like the product; training changes how people taste, and a dozen experts are not a sample of any consumer population. And do not ask your own group members to rate their own product: they are neither blind nor representative.

Temporal methods: flavour as a story

Flavour unfolds in time. The first bite of cricket bread may be sweet and yeasty, then roasted and nutty, and it may end with an earthy, slightly bitter aftertaste that lingers after swallowing. A static profile averages this story away. Two families of methods record it.

In time–intensity (TI) evaluation, an assessor continuously rates the intensity of one attribute — say bitterness — from the moment of tasting until it disappears, typically by moving a cursor on a screen. The resulting curve is summarised by parameters such as maximum intensity, time to maximum, duration and area under the curve. TI is precise but slow, because every attribute requires a separate evaluation.

Temporal dominance of sensations (TDS) records many attributes at once (Pineau et al., 2009; Schlich, 2017). The assessor sees a list of eight to ten attributes on a screen and, while eating, clicks whichever attribute is currently dominant — the one that catches the attention — changing the selection whenever another sensation takes over. For each attribute and each moment, the dominance rate is the proportion of evaluations (panellists × replicates) in which that attribute was selected. Plotting dominance rates against time gives TDS curves. Two reference lines help reading them: the chance level, and a significance level above which an attribute is dominant more often than chance would allow.

TDS chance level and significance level(13.3.7)
\[ P_0 = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad P_s = P_0 + 1.645\,\sqrt{\frac{P_0\,(1-P_0)}{n}} \]

the chance level is one divided by the number of attributes on the list; the significance level is the chance level plus 1.645 standard errors of a proportion, the standard error being the square root of the chance level times one minus the chance level, divided by the number of evaluations.

Who tastes? Panels and panel size

The choice of assessors follows from the question. Trained panels are recruited, screened for sensory acuity and trained to use a vocabulary and a scale consistently (ISO 8586:2023); they are used for descriptive analysis and for demanding discrimination work. Consumer panels are untrained people from the population whose behaviour you want to predict; they are the only valid source of liking and preference data. Discrimination tests sit in between: ISO 4120:2021 asks that all assessors have the same level of qualification, chosen according to the objective, and be familiar with the mechanics of the test. A class of students is a convenient group of naïve assessors for discrimination tests and a convenience sample of young, educated consumers — not a representative sample of Swedish consumers, and usually less neophobic than the general population (Lesson 13.1).

Screening a student panel

Before the session, ask every volunteer (on the consent form, not in the data file) about food allergies and intolerances, dietary restrictions (a vegan cannot taste cricket bread), and anything that temporarily impairs taste or smell, such as a cold. A common rule of thumb is to ask participants not to eat, drink coffee or smoke for about 30 minutes before tasting. Remind them that they can stop at any time.

The test environment and sample preparation

Everything that differs between two samples, other than the variable you are studying, is a potential explanation of your result. The test environment and the preparation of samples exist to remove those alternative explanations. Two ISO standards set the rules you should follow in the project.

The sensory booth (ISO 8589)

ISO 8589:2007 gives general guidance for the design of test rooms. Its principle is to conduct evaluations “under known and controlled conditions with a minimum of distractions” and to reduce the effects of psychological factors and physical conditions on human judgement. The minimum facility is a testing area (individual booths or a group area) and a preparation area. The key specifications are summarised in Figure 9:

  • Layout. The preparation area is close to the testing area but separate from it, to keep out odours and noise, and assessors must not enter or leave the testing area through the preparation area. Samples are passed through hatches with quietly closing sliding doors, designed so that assessors cannot see samples being prepared or coded.
  • Climate, noise and odour. The temperature is controlled and comfortable (relative humidity controllable if it affects the product); noise is kept to a minimum; the room is kept free of odours — for example with activated-carbon filtered air and a slight positive pressure — and furnishings, cleaning agents and materials must not emit odours.
  • Colours. Walls and furnishings are neutral, so that sample colours are not modified: matt off-white or light neutral grey. Booth interiors for general use are matt grey with a luminance factor of about 15 % (Munsell N4 to N5).
  • Lighting. Uniform, free from strong shadows and controllable. As an example, the standard mentions lamps with a correlated colour temperature of 6 500 K as a neutral light similar to northern daylight. To mask unwanted colour differences, dimmers, coloured light sources, coloured filters or monochromatic (sodium-vapour) light may be used; in consumer tests, lighting typical of where the product is eaten may be chosen.
  • Booth size. A working area of at least 0.9 m wide and 0.6 m deep, dividers extending at least 0.3 m beyond the counter, and — if seats cannot be moved — at least 0.35 m between seat and working surface. The number of booths depends on the space and the tests; at least one booth should accommodate a wheelchair where local rules require it. A signal system (a switch that lights a lamp on the preparation side, or a card slipped under the hatch) lets assessors call for the next sample.

Preparing and serving samples (ISO 4120)

ISO 4120:2021 lists the general conditions for a triangle test, and they are good practice for every sensory test:

  1. Prepare out of sight and identically — same apparatus, same vessels, same quantities (clause 5.3). Bake both breads on the same day in the same oven; buy both lettuces the same day and store them together.
  2. Make the samples indistinguishable except for the test variable (clause 5.4): mask irrelevant colour differences with light filters or subdued lighting, cut identical shapes (crumb only, or crust included the same way for every sample).
  3. Code every sample with a three-digit number chosen at random (clause 5.5), preferably different codes for every assessor. Codes such as 1, 2, 3 or A, B, C carry meaning (first, best, grade A) — and so do “memorable” three-digit numbers such as 123 or 999.
  4. Serve under the conditions of normal consumption (clause 5.6) — at the temperature at which the product is usually eaten — and identical for all samples of a test.
  5. Give identical portions (clause 5.7), or instruct assessors to take similar amounts.
  6. Tell assessors whether to swallow or expectorate, and ask them to do the same for all samples (clause 5.8).
  7. Give no information about identity, expected effects or individual performance until all testing is complete (clause 5.9).
  8. Balance the serving order. For the triangle test, use the six arrangements ABB, AAB, ABA, BAA, BBA and BAB equally often, distributed at random in groups of six (clause 7.1). For three or more products in a rating test, use Williams Latin squares (Lesson 13.4).

Palate cleansers. Between samples, assessors rinse with water and eat a neutral cleanser. Lucak and Delwiche (2009) compared cleansers (table water crackers, spring water, pectin solution, whole milk, chocolate and warm water) with foods chosen to be sweet, bitter, fatty, astringent, hot, cooling and non-lingering; only table water crackers prevented significant differences across replicates for all the foods. As a rule of thumb, allow a rest interval of at least a minute between samples, and longer for lingering stimuli such as chilli, bitterness or fat.

Portions. There is no universal portion size. As a rule of thumb, one or two bite-sized pieces (roughly 10–20 g of a solid, 15–30 mL of a liquid) are enough for a discrimination test, whereas a hedonic judgement benefits from enough for two or three bites. What matters most is that portions are identical within a test and large enough to allow re-tasting.

Temperature. Serving temperature changes both perception (volatility of aroma compounds, perceived sweetness, texture of fats) and liking. Warm samples cool on the tray, so the assessor who tastes last gets a different product from the assessor who tastes first. A simple heat-transfer model tells you how long you have.

Serving window of a warm sample (Newton's law of cooling)(13.3.8)
\[ T(t) = T_r + \left(T_0 - T_r\right)e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad t^{*} = \tau\,\ln\!\frac{T_0 - T_r}{T_{\lim} - T_r}, \qquad \tau = \frac{m\,c_p}{h\,A} \]

the sample temperature approaches room temperature exponentially, with a time constant tau; the time until it falls to the lowest acceptable serving temperature is tau times the natural logarithm of the initial excess temperature divided by the acceptable excess temperature. Tau equals the sample's mass times its specific heat capacity, divided by the heat-transfer coefficient times the exposed surface area.

Your group wants to serve the breads slightly warm. Slices leave the warming cabinet at 45 °C; the room is at 21 °C; you decide that the crumb must not be colder than 35 °C when tasted. In a pilot, the core temperature of a served slice fell from 45 °C to 31.4 °C in 10 minutes. (a) Estimate the time constant τ. (b) How long is the serving window? (c) What does this mean for a session with six booths?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Time constant from the pilot: \(\ln\dfrac{T - T_r}{T_0 - T_r} = -\dfrac{t}{\tau}\) with \(T - T_r = 31.4 - 21 = 10.4\) K and \(T_0 - T_r = 24\) K: \(\ln(10.4/24) = \ln 0.4333 = -0.836\), so \(\tau = 10/0.836 = 12.0\) min.
  2. (b) Serving window: \(t^* = 12.0\times\ln\dfrac{45 - 21}{35 - 21} = 12.0\times\ln 1.714 = 12.0\times0.539 = 6.5\) min.
  3. (c) Consequence. A slice must be tasted within about 6 minutes of leaving the cabinet. Serve each assessor's tray only when he or she signals readiness, keep trays in the cabinet until then, and serve all samples of one tray at the same moment so that they have the same temperature (ISO 4120:2021, clause 5.6).
  4. Sanity check. After one time constant (12 min) the excess temperature has fallen to \(e^{-1} = 37\,\%\) of 24 K, i.e., to 29.8 °C — below the limit, consistent with a window shorter than τ. ✓
τ ≈ 12 min; serving window ≈ 6.5 min

Psychological biases and how to control them

Human judgement is shaped by context: by what people see, read, expect and have just tasted. Sensory textbooks list a set of recurring “errors” (Civille & Carr, 2015; Lawless & Heymann, 2010). None of them can be eliminated by asking people to be objective; they are controlled by design. Table 4 connects each bias to a concrete control in your project.

Blind or informed? Both — but separately

Blind tests measure what the product delivers to the senses; informed tests measure what the product delivers plus what people expect from the information. For novel foods the difference between the two is often the most interesting result: it quantifies the effect of the label “contains insects” or “grown without soil” (Lesson 13.1). Run the blind condition first, or in separate sessions, so that the information cannot contaminate the blind ratings.

Ethics, safety and the law

A tasting study asks people to put something in their mouths. That makes it the most ethically and legally sensitive part of the course project, and your protocol must be approved by the teacher before you recruit anyone. The general principles of research with human participants — respect for persons, voluntary informed consent, minimising risk, confidentiality — are set out in the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025) and apply to a student tasting as much as to a clinical trial.

Informed consent

Before tasting, every participant receives written information and gives consent. The information states the purpose of the study, what participants will do and how long it takes, what the samples contain — including every novel ingredient and every allergen, any risks, that participation is voluntary, that they may withdraw at any time without giving a reason, what data are collected, who can see them, how long they are kept, and whom to contact. Participation must never affect grades, and nobody should feel pressured by friends or teachers. After the session, debrief: reveal the identity of the coded samples and answer questions.

Allergens and food safety

EU food law lists 14 allergens that must always be declared: cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, nuts, celery, mustard, sesame seeds, sulphur dioxide and sulphites, lupin and molluscs (Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex II). Novel foods can add specific warnings: the EU authorisations of dried yellow mealworm and of partially defatted house-cricket powder require labelling that the ingredient may cause allergic reactions in consumers with known allergies to crustaceans and dust mites — and, for the cricket powder, molluscs (Commission Implementing Regulations (EU) 2021/882 and 2023/5). Bread made with cricket flour therefore contains at least gluten and a crustacean/mite cross-reactivity warning; state both on the invitation and the consent form, and exclude anyone who is allergic. Exclude also people with coeliac disease or other relevant intolerances, people whose diet excludes the product (vegans and insects), and anyone under 18. Handle samples as food: clean utensils and food-grade containers, cold chain, hand washing, and a plan for what to do if someone feels unwell (in Sweden, call 112 in an emergency).

Is the sample legally food? Novel-food status

Under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, a food not used for human consumption to a significant degree within the EU before 15 May 1997 is a novel food, and only authorised novel foods included in the Union list may be placed on the market (Lesson 14.1). “Placing on the market” includes any form of transfer, whether free of charge or not (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, Art. 3(8)) — so handing out an unauthorised novel food at a tasting is not allowed, even for free. The course rule is simple: serve only foods that are legally on the EU market, bought from registered food businesses, used as intended and documented (keep the labels and receipts). Some examples:

  • Insect ingredients: use only products covered by an EU authorisation, such as dried yellow mealworm or house-cricket powder, sold by a food business with the required labelling.
  • Mycoprotein: mycoprotein from Fusarium venenatum strain A3/5, the organism used for Quorn®, is not novel: it was consumed in the EU before 1997 and has been on the UK market since 1985 (European Commission, n.d.). Novelty can be strain-specific, however: a mycoprotein from a different strain or process may be novel even though the species is the same.
  • Seaweed: many seaweeds have a history of consumption, but check the species against the EU Novel Food Status Catalogue — and check iodine. In a Norwegian survey of commercially available macroalgae products, a single portion of some kelp and seaweed products exceeded the tolerable upper intake level for iodine, and labelling of species and iodine content was often inadequate (Aakre et al., 2021). Choose products with declared iodine content and keep portions small.
  • Your own hydroponic lettuce: not a novel food, but only safe if it was grown with food-grade inputs and drinking-quality water and handled hygienically (Lesson 3.1). If in doubt, serve purchased produce.

Personal data and the GDPR

Sensory data are about people, so the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) applies whenever a participant can be identified. Personal data means any information relating to an identified or identifiable person (Art. 4(1)); anonymous information that can no longer be linked to a person falls outside the regulation (Recital 26), but pseudonymised data — responses stored under a code, with a key that links codes to names — are still personal data (Art. 4(5)). Information about allergies or intolerances is data concerning health, a special category that needs particular protection (Art. 9). Practical rules for the project:

  • Collect only what you will analyse (data minimisation, Art. 5(1)(c)); age group and gender are rarely needed, names never.
  • Record responses under panellist codes; keep signed consent forms physically separate from the response data, and do not create a code–name key unless you need one.
  • Screen for allergies without recording them: state the exclusion criteria and let people exclude themselves, so that no health data enter your files.
  • Tell participants what you collect and why (Art. 13), store data on university systems, and delete raw data according to the university's rules when the course is over.

Does a student tasting need ethical review in Sweden?

The Swedish Ethical Review Act (Lag (2003:460) om etikprövning av forskning som avser människor) requires approval by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Etikprövningsmyndigheten) before research that, for example, involves sensitive personal data such as health data, a physical intervention on participants, or methods intended to affect them physically or mentally. However, the Act's definition of research (§ 2) excludes work carried out within first- and second-cycle higher education (högskoleutbildning på grundnivå eller avancerad nivå). A tasting study in this bachelor course is therefore not subject to formal ethical review. That is not a licence to ignore ethics: the principles above still apply, your teacher approves the protocol, and your university's local guidelines for student projects must be followed. If your data are to be used in research — for example in a researcher's publication or a doctoral project — the Act applies, and approval must be obtained before the data are collected.

Check your understanding

Key terms

Sensory evaluation
The scientific discipline used to evoke, measure, analyse and interpret reactions to the characteristics of foods as perceived by the senses.
Flavour
The unified percept formed by taste, retronasal smell, chemesthesis and texture while eating.
Retronasal olfaction
Smelling of volatiles released in the mouth that reach the olfactory epithelium through the nasopharynx; perceived as “taste”.
Chemesthesis
Chemically induced sensations such as pungency, cooling and tingling, carried by the trigeminal nerve through TRP channels.
Just-noticeable difference
The smallest detectable change in a stimulus; by Weber's law a constant fraction \(k\) of the stimulus.
Stevens' power law
Perceived intensity grows as a power of stimulus intensity, \(\psi = k\varphi^{n}\), with a continuum-specific exponent.
Discrimination test
A forced-choice test (triangle, duo–trio, 2-AFC, 3-AFC, tetrad) that determines whether a perceptible difference exists; its guessing probability \(p_0\) is 1/3 (triangle, 3-AFC, tetrad) or 1/2 (duo–trio, 2-AFC).
d′ (d-prime)
The size of a sensory difference in units of the standard deviation of perception; independent of the test method.
Quantitative descriptive analysis
A descriptive method in which a trained panel develops a vocabulary and rates attribute intensities on line scales with replication.
Flash profile
A rapid descriptive method in which each assessor ranks products on his or her own attributes; results are combined by multivariate analysis.
9-point hedonic scale
The standard consumer liking scale from “dislike extremely” (1) to “like extremely” (9) (Peryam & Pilgrim, 1957).
Just-about-right (JAR) scale
A bipolar scale asking whether an attribute's level is too weak, just about right or too strong for the consumer.
CATA
Check-all-that-apply: consumers tick every word from a list that describes a sample; data are citation frequencies.
Temporal dominance of sensations
A method recording which of several attributes is dominant at each moment of eating; results are dominance-rate curves.
Expectation error
A bias in which information about a sample changes how it is perceived or rated.
