IoT architectures, networks and edge computing

At three o'clock on a January night the heating of a Swedish greenhouse fails. Will the grower hear about it at 03:05, from a message on the phone — or at 07:00, from frozen crops? The difference is not the thermometer. It is everything behind it: a microcontroller that wakes up, a radio link that must reach across 400 metres of wet glasshouse, a broker that forwards the message, a database that remembers, an alarm rule that decides, and a design that still works when the internet does not. This lesson builds that chain layer by layer, with the physics of radio links, the arithmetic of batteries, and a complete reference design for the logger you can build in the course project.

  • Calculate LoRa time on air for different spreading factors and the transmission limits set by the EU868 1 % duty cycle and network fair-use rules (LO3, LO5).

From a logger in a drawer to the Internet of Things

A data logger that stores readings on a memory card is a fine scientific instrument, but its data are only seen when somebody takes the card out. The Internet of Things (IoT) connects sensing devices to networks so that their data flow continuously to where decisions are made, and commands flow back to actuators. In controlled-environment agriculture this is not new — greenhouse climate computers have used wired sensor networks for decades — but cheap microcontrollers with built-in radios, long-range low-power networks and cloud services have made it possible to instrument every tank, bench and field plot. Reviews of IoT in agriculture describe the same recurring building blocks: sensing devices, communication technologies, data storage and processing, and applications (Tzounis et al., 2017; Wolfert et al., 2017).

It helps to think of an IoT system as a stack of layers, each with one job and a well-defined interface to the next (Figure 1). Data flow upwards, from the physical measurand to the human decision; commands, configuration and firmware updates flow downwards. The measurement chain of Lesson 9.1 lives in the bottom layer; the uncertainty, filtering and quality flags of Lesson 9.3 can be applied in any layer — and a good design decides deliberately where.

Key idea

Every layer adds latency, energy cost, failure modes and attack surface. The best IoT design is the simplest architecture that meets the requirement — and the requirement must include what happens when a layer fails. A frost alarm that depends on a cloud server is only as reliable as the internet connection of a rural greenhouse at night.

Devices: microcontrollers, single-board computers and sensor interfaces

At the bottom of the stack sits a small computer that reads the sensors. Two families dominate. A microcontroller (MCU) is a single chip with processor, memory, timers, analogue-to-digital converters and serial interfaces; it runs one program directly on the hardware (or on a tiny real-time operating system), starts in milliseconds, responds deterministically and can sleep at microamperes. A single-board computer (SBC) such as the Raspberry Pi runs a full Linux operating system with gigabytes of memory; it can host a database, a web dashboard or a camera pipeline, but it draws watts, takes tens of seconds to boot, and must be shut down properly to avoid corrupting its memory card. The two are complementary: in the reference design of this lesson, an ESP32 microcontroller measures and an SBC or server stores and displays.

The ESP32 is the workhorse of student loggers because it combines Wi-Fi, a capable processor and low sleep current at low cost (Espressif Systems, 2025). Know its two analogue weaknesses. First, its ADCs are only moderately accurate: the data sheet specifies differential and integral non-linearities of ±7 and ±12 LSB and chip-to-chip differences of about ±6 % before calibration, which is why the reference design uses an external 16-bit ADS1115 for mV-level signals (Lesson 9.1). Newer family members — ESP32-S3 with vector instructions for small neural networks, ESP32-C6 with Wi-Fi 6, BLE and an IEEE 802.15.4 radio for Thread and Zigbee — follow the same programming model.

Practical warning: voltage levels and grounds

The ESP32 is a 3.3 V device: its inputs must never see the 5 V that many Arduino-era sensor boards produce — use 3.3 V boards or level shifters. And never let two probes that sit in the same tank (pH and EC, for example) share a ground with your logger without galvanic isolation: the EC excitation current then flows through the pH reference and shifts the reading (Lesson 9.2).

Wireless options and their trade-offs

Choosing a radio is a trade-off between four quantities that physics does not let you maximise together: range, data rate, energy per message and cost (hardware, subscription, infrastructure). The root of the trade-off is the Shannon–Hartley capacity \(C = B\log_2(1 + \text{SNR})\): at a given transmit power, a receiver farther away sees a smaller signal-to-noise ratio, and the only way to keep the link working is to lower the data rate — sending each bit with more energy. Low-power wide-area networks (LPWANs) such as LoRaWAN and NB-IoT sit at the extreme of this trade-off: a few hundred bits per second to a few kilobits per second, over kilometres, from batteries that last years (Raza et al., 2017; Mekki et al., 2019).

The topology matters as much as the radio (Figure 4). Wi-Fi is a star around an access point. Zigbee, Thread and BLE mesh relay messages from node to node, so the network extends itself — but relaying nodes must stay awake and cannot run for years on a coin cell. LoRaWAN is a "star of stars": end devices transmit, any gateway in range forwards the packet over the internet to a network server, which removes duplicates, manages data rates and security keys, and hands the data to an application server (often through an MQTT interface). NB-IoT and LTE-M use the mobile operator's base stations and core network, so you rent coverage instead of building it.

Link budgets and path loss

Will a radio link close? The answer is an addition of decibels called a link budget. Radio engineers express powers in dBm (decibels relative to 1 mW: \(P_{\text{dBm}} = 10\log_{10}(P/1\ \text{mW})\), so 0 dBm = 1 mW, 14 dBm = 25 mW, 20 dBm = 100 mW, −100 dBm = 0.1 pW) and gains and losses in dB, because the multiplications of the physical chain then become additions. Every 3 dB is a factor 2 and every 10 dB a factor 10.

Link budget and link margin(9.4.1)
\[ P_{\text{rx}} = P_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{rx}} - L_{\text{c}} - L_{\text{p}}(d) - L_{\text{x}}, \qquad M = P_{\text{rx}} - S \]

the received power in dBm equals the transmitted power plus the gains of both antennas minus cable and connector losses, minus the path loss over distance d, minus any extra losses such as walls or vegetation; the link margin is the received power minus the receiver's sensitivity, and the link works reliably only if the margin exceeds the expected fading.

Follow the derivation
  1. In linear units the chain is multiplicative: the transmitter's power \(p_{\text{tx}}\) is multiplied by the antenna gain factors \(g_{\text{tx}}g_{\text{rx}}\) and divided by the loss factors \(l_{\text{c}}\,l_{\text{p}}\,l_{\text{x}}\): \(p_{\text{rx}} = p_{\text{tx}}\,g_{\text{tx}}g_{\text{rx}}/(l_{\text{c}}l_{\text{p}}l_{\text{x}})\).
  2. Take \(10\log_{10}\) of both sides. The logarithm of a product is the sum of logarithms, and of a quotient the difference: \(10\log_{10}p_{\text{rx}} = 10\log_{10}p_{\text{tx}} + 10\log_{10}g_{\text{tx}} + \dots - 10\log_{10}l_{\text{p}} - \dots\)

  4. The receiver decodes reliably if \(P_{\text{rx}} \ge S\), so the margin \(M = P_{\text{rx}} - S\) is the room left for everything the model does not capture — fading, a wet canopy, a person standing in front of the antenna.
Assumptions and validity

Antenna gains are quoted in the direction of maximum radiation; a node antenna lying flat on a tank lid or inside a metal enclosure can lose 10 dB or more. EU rules limit the radiated power, not the transmitter output: in the 868.0–868.6 MHz sub-band the limit is 25 mW effective radiated power (14 dBm ERP), so a higher-gain antenna must be paired with a lower transmitter setting. Losses in dB add only if each is defined for the same signal path.

The largest and most variable term is the path loss. Its reference case is empty space, where the only loss is geometric spreading.

Friis transmission equation and free-space path loss(9.4.2)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{P_{\text{rx}}}{P_{\text{tx}}} = G_{\text{tx}}G_{\text{rx}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^{2} \\[8pt] L_{\text{FS}} = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d f}{c}\right) = 20\log_{10}d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10}f_{\text{MHz}} + 32.45\ \text{dB} \end{gathered} \]

in free space, the fraction of transmitted power that reaches the receiver equals the product of the two antenna gains times the square of the wavelength divided by four pi times the distance; expressed in decibels, the free-space path loss grows by 20 dB for every tenfold increase of distance and of frequency.

Follow the derivation (Friis, 1946)
  1. Spreading. An isotropic antenna radiates \(P_{\text{tx}}\) equally in all directions; at distance \(d\) the power is spread over a sphere of area \(4\pi d^2\), so the power flux density is \(P_{\text{tx}}/(4\pi d^2)\) (W m⁻²). A directional antenna concentrates it by its gain: \(\Phi = G_{\text{tx}}P_{\text{tx}}/(4\pi d^2)\).
  2. Collecting. A receiving antenna behaves as if it had an effective area \(A_e\). Antenna theory (via reciprocity and thermodynamics) gives, for any antenna, \(A_e = G_{\text{rx}}\lambda^2/(4\pi)\): an isotropic antenna has an effective area of \(\lambda^2/(4\pi)\) — 95 cm² at 868 MHz but only 12 cm² at 2.44 GHz.
  3. Multiply: \(P_{\text{rx}} = \Phi A_e = \dfrac{G_{\text{tx}}P_{\text{tx}}}{4\pi d^2}\cdot\dfrac{G_{\text{rx}}\lambda^2}{4\pi} = P_{\text{tx}}G_{\text{tx}}G_{\text{rx}}\left(\dfrac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2\).
  4. In decibels the loss between isotropic antennas is \(L_{\text{FS}} = 10\log_{10}(4\pi d/\lambda)^2 = 20\log_{10}(4\pi d f/c)\).
  5. Practical constant. With \(d\) in km (×10³) and \(f\) in MHz (×10⁶): \(20\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{4\pi\times10^3\times10^6}{2.998\times10^8}\right) = 20\log_{10}(41.92) = 32.45\) dB.
Assumptions and validity — why lower frequencies reach farther

Friis assumes the far field (distance much larger than the wavelength and the antenna size), aligned polarisation, and a clear line of sight with nothing near the path — in particular, the first Fresnel zone (Eq. 9.4.5) must be free of obstacles. The frequency dependence does not come from air "absorbing" higher frequencies (at these frequencies it hardly does) but from the smaller effective area of a receiving antenna at shorter wavelength: at equal antenna gains, a 2.44 GHz link loses \(20\log_{10}(2440/868) = 9.0\) dB more than an 868 MHz link. Walls, water and leaves, on the other hand, do attenuate higher frequencies more; the ITU publishes models for attenuation in vegetation (ITU-R P.833).

\(\lambda/(4\pi d)\): m/m = dimensionless ✓; \(4\pi d f/c\): m × s⁻¹ /(m s⁻¹) = dimensionless ✓, so \(L_{\text{FS}}\) is a ratio in dB ✓

Near the ground, among crops and inside buildings, the signal is reflected, diffracted and absorbed, and the received power usually falls faster than \(1/d^2\). The standard engineering description generalises the exponent.

Log-distance path-loss model with shadowing, and the maximum range(9.4.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} L_{\text{p}}(d) = L_{\text{FS}}(d_0) + 10\,n\,\log_{10}\!\frac{d}{d_0} + X_\sigma, \qquad X_\sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) \\[8pt] d_{\max} = d_0\cdot 10^{\left(P_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{rx}} - L_{\text{c}} - L_{\text{x}} - S - z\sigma - L_{\text{FS}}(d_0)\right)/(10\,n)} \end{gathered} \]

the path loss equals the free-space loss up to a reference distance plus ten times the path-loss exponent times the logarithm of the distance ratio, plus a random shadowing term with standard deviation sigma in decibels; the maximum range is found by spending the whole link budget, after reserving a fade margin of z standard deviations, on path loss.

Follow the derivation
  1. Power law. Measurements show that the mean received power often falls as \(d^{-n}\) over a range of distances. Relative to a reference distance: \(\bar{P}_{\text{rx}}(d) = P_{\text{rx}}(d_0)\,(d/d_0)^{-n}\).
  2. In decibels: \(10\log_{10}\) turns the power law into a straight line in \(\log_{10} d\): the loss grows by \(10n\) dB per decade of distance — 20 dB per decade in free space, 30–40 dB per decade near the ground.
  3. Why \(n = 4\) near the ground: the two-ray model. Over flat ground the receiver sees the direct wave and a ground-reflected wave with nearly opposite phase. For distances beyond about \(4h_{\text{tx}}h_{\text{rx}}/\lambda\), the two almost cancel and \(P_{\text{rx}} \approx P_{\text{tx}}G_{\text{tx}}G_{\text{rx}}h_{\text{tx}}^2h_{\text{rx}}^2/d^4\): an exponent of 4, and a received power that grows with the square of each antenna height (Rappaport, 2002). For a node at 1 m and a gateway at 10 m at 868 MHz, the break point is at about 116 m — which is why \(d_0 \approx 100\) m is a sensible outdoor reference.
  4. Shadowing. Obstacles make the loss at a given distance vary from place to place; measured in dB, the variation is approximately normal with standard deviation σ. For the link to close with probability \(p\), reserve a fade margin \(z\sigma\), where \(\Phi(z) = p\).
  5. Maximum range. Set \(L_{\text{p}}(d_{\max}) = P_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{tx}} + G_{\text{rx}} - L_{\text{c}} - L_{\text{x}} - S - z\sigma\) and solve for \(d_{\max}\): the exponent in Eq. 9.4.3.
Assumptions and validity

The model is empirical: \(n\) and σ must be measured or taken from similar environments, and they change with antenna height, season (leaf area, wet foliage) and greenhouse structure. Because the range depends exponentially on the budget divided by \(10n\), a few decibels matter a lot: with \(n = 3\), every 3 dB of extra budget increases the range by a factor \(10^{3/30} = 1.26\). The cheapest decibels are usually won by raising an antenna.

\(10n\log_{10}(d/d_0)\): dimensionless × dB = dB ✓; exponent of 10 in \(d_{\max}\): dB/dB = dimensionless, so \(d_{\max}\) has the unit of \(d_0\) ✓

The last term of the budget is the receiver. How weak a signal can it decode? The floor is set by thermal noise — the random motion of electrons in every conductor — and by how much signal-to-noise ratio the modulation needs.

Thermal noise floor and receiver sensitivity(9.4.4)
\[ \begin{gathered} S = -174\ \text{dBm Hz}^{-1} + 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{B}{1\ \text{Hz}}\right) + \mathit{NF} + \text{SNR}_{\min} \\[6pt] \text{where}\quad -174\ \text{dBm Hz}^{-1} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{k_B T_0}{1\ \text{mW}}\right)\ \text{at}\ T_0 = 290\ \text{K} \end{gathered} \]

the sensitivity in dBm equals the thermal noise power per hertz at room temperature, minus 174 dBm, plus ten times the logarithm of the receiver bandwidth in hertz, plus the receiver's noise figure, plus the minimum signal-to-noise ratio the demodulator needs — which is negative for spread-spectrum systems such as LoRa.

  3. Bandwidth. Noise power grows with bandwidth: for 125 kHz add \(10\log_{10}(125\,000) = 51.0\) dB, giving a noise floor of −123 dBm; for a 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel add 73 dB, giving −101 dBm.
  4. Receiver and demodulator. A real receiver adds its noise figure NF, and the demodulator needs a minimum SNR. For LoRa SF12 in 125 kHz: \(S = -174 + 51.0 + 6 - 20 = -137\) dBm. The SX1276 data sheet specifies −136 dBm, and −123 dBm at SF7 (Semtech, 2015).

A final geometric condition decides whether the free-space and log-distance models apply at all: the space around the direct line between the antennas must be clear (Figure 5).

Radius of the first Fresnel zone(9.4.5)
\[ r_1 = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda\,d_1 d_2}{d_1 + d_2}}, \qquad r_{1,\text{mid}} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda\,d} \]

the radius of the first Fresnel zone at a point that is d1 from one antenna and d2 from the other is the square root of the wavelength times d1 times d2 divided by their sum; at the middle of a path of length d it is half the square root of the wavelength times d.

LoRa: spreading factors, time on air and the duty-cycle rule

LoRa (from "long range") is a chirp spread-spectrum modulation developed by Semtech; LoRaWAN is the network protocol on top of it, specified by the LoRa Alliance. Each LoRa symbol is a chirp — a tone whose frequency sweeps linearly across the channel bandwidth \(B\) — and the data are encoded in the starting frequency of the sweep. A symbol carries SF bits (the spreading factor, 7 to 12) using \(2^{SF}\) chips. Raising SF by one doubles the symbol duration, halves the data rate, and improves sensitivity by about 2.5 dB (Table 5). Signals with different spreading factors are nearly orthogonal, so a gateway can receive them simultaneously on the same channel.

  2. Preamble. The receiver needs a run of identical up-chirps to detect the packet and synchronise; LoRaWAN programs 8, and the radio adds a sync word and 2.25 down-chirps (the start-of-frame delimiter): 4.25 symbols.
  3. Bits to send. The payload has \(8PL\) bits, the CRC 16 bits, and the explicit header 20 bits. The first 8 symbols (sent at the most robust coding) carry \(SF - 2\) bits each after coding, i.e. \(4SF\) bits of the "8 +" term; the constant 28 accounts for fixed overhead in that block.
  4. Blocks. The remaining bits are sent in blocks of \(4(SF - 2DE)\) bits; each block is expanded by the forward error-correcting code into \(CR + 4\) symbols (5 symbols at coding rate 4/5). Rounding up gives the number of blocks, hence \(n_{\text{pay}}\) (Semtech, 2015).
  5. Example, SF7, 12-byte application payload: \(PL = 25\); \(\lceil(200 - 28 + 28 + 16)/28\rceil = \lceil7.71\rceil = 8\) blocks → \(n_{\text{pay}} = 8 + 8\times5 = 48\) symbols; \(T_{\text{air}} = (12.25 + 48)\times1.024 = 61.7\) ms.

Why limits matter: collisions in an ALOHA network

LoRaWAN end devices transmit whenever they have data, without checking whether the channel is free — the "pure ALOHA" access scheme. If packets arrive at random with an offered load G (the average number of packets overlapping one packet duration), the fraction of time used by successful packets is \(S = G\,e^{-2G}\), which peaks at only 18.4 % for G = 0.5. Long packets at high spreading factors raise G and therefore collisions; duty-cycle limits, adaptive data rate and short payloads keep a shared network usable (Adelantado et al., 2017).

MQTT, databases and the edge–cloud split

Once a message has crossed the network it must reach the programs that need it: a database, a dashboard, an alarm service, a controller. Wiring each sensor to each consumer would create a tangle of point-to-point connections. MQTT (originally MQ Telemetry Transport) solves this with publish/subscribe: devices publish messages to named topics on a central broker, and programs subscribe to the topics they are interested in (Figure 8). Publishers and subscribers never talk to each other directly and need not even be online at the same time. MQTT is an open OASIS standard — version 3.1.1 (2014, also ISO/IEC 20922) and version 5.0 (Banks et al., 2019) — and it runs over TCP, by convention on port 1883 without encryption and 8883 with TLS.

Four MQTT features matter most for monitoring. Topics are hierarchical strings separated by slashes, such as mdu/gh1/zone2/nutrient/ph; a subscription filter can use + for exactly one level and # for all remaining levels. Quality of service (QoS) is chosen per message (Figure 9): QoS 0 sends once and forgets; QoS 1 repeats until acknowledged, so a message arrives at least once but possibly twice; QoS 2 uses a four-step handshake to deliver exactly once. Retained messages are stored by the broker — the last one per topic — and delivered immediately to every new subscriber, which is ideal for states such as "vent position" or "node online". The Last Will and Testament is a message a client registers when it connects; if it disappears without a clean disconnect, the broker publishes it, so the dashboard learns that a node has died.

A consistent topic tree is the backbone of a maintainable system. A proven pattern is site / building / zone / subsystem / quantity, with devices and alarms in their own branches:

# sensor values (retained = no; QoS 1)

Good practice: lower-case names without spaces; no leading slash; the unit in the payload, not in the topic; the logical location (zone, tank) in the topic and the physical device identity in the payload or the device branch, so that a replaced sensor does not break the history. Units can follow the SenML conventions of RFC 8428 (Jennings et al., 2018), where degrees Celsius are written "Cel". Test your understanding of the wildcards with Figure 10.

Behind the broker, a time-series database stores the history. Time-series databases (for example InfluxDB, TimescaleDB or Prometheus) are optimised for append-only records of time stamp, value and tags: they compress regular sampling efficiently, apply retention policies (keep raw 1-min data for a year, hourly means forever) and downsampling, and answer queries such as "daily mean EC per tank" quickly. A small "bridge" — Telegraf, Node-RED or a few lines of Python — subscribes to the topic tree and writes each message into the database. Dashboards such as Grafana then display the data and evaluate alarm rules. For the project, a Raspberry Pi running the Mosquitto broker, InfluxDB and Grafana is a complete, self-hosted platform; keep the SD-card log on the logger as the raw (level-0) archive of Lesson 9.3.

Power budgets, batteries and energy harvesting

A mains-powered logger next to your grow system has no power problem. A node at the far end of a field, on a floating raft or in a cold store has nothing else. Its lifetime is decided by an exercise in bookkeeping: how much charge does each phase of the duty cycle consume — sleeping, waking and measuring, transmitting, listening — and how much does the battery hold?

Average current and battery life(9.4.8)
\[ \begin{gathered} \bar{I} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{k} I_k\,t_k = \frac{I_{\text{s}}t_{\text{s}} + I_{\text{tx}}t_{\text{tx}} + I_{\text{rx}}t_{\text{rx}} + I_{\text{sl}}\left(T - t_{\text{s}} - t_{\text{tx}} - t_{\text{rx}}\right)}{T} \\[8pt] L = \frac{\eta\,C}{\bar{I}} \end{gathered} \]

the average current is the charge used in one reporting cycle — each phase's current times its duration, including sleep for the rest of the cycle — divided by the cycle length; the battery life is the usable fraction of the capacity divided by the average current.

Follow the derivation
  1. Charge conservation. The charge drawn from the battery in one cycle is \(Q = \int_0^T I(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\). With piecewise-constant currents the integral becomes a sum of rectangles, \(Q = \sum_k I_kt_k\).
  2. Average current: \(\bar{I} = Q/T\) — the constant current that would drain the same charge.
  3. Life. A battery of capacity \(C\) (mAh, a charge) delivers \(\eta C\) usefully; at \(\bar{I}\) (mA) it lasts \(L = \eta C/\bar{I}\) hours.
  4. Where the charge goes. Because the sleep phase lasts almost the whole cycle, a sleep current of a few milliamperes can dominate everything else; conversely, with a 10 µA sleep, the radio phase dominates and the life is roughly proportional to the reporting interval.
Assumptions and validity — the usable fraction η

\(\eta\) collects everything that makes a battery deliver less than its label: cut-off voltage (the regulator or the radio stops working before the cell is empty), cold (alkaline and lithium-ion cells lose much of their capacity below 0 °C), high current pulses (a Wi-Fi burst of 240 mA from a small cell), self-discharge over long lifetimes, and the regulator. A linear regulator passes the same current, so \(\bar{I}\) applies directly; a switching regulator converts power, so compute in energy (mWh) with its efficiency. Measure the real currents with a USB power meter, a shunt resistor and oscilloscope, or a dedicated power profiler — data-sheet numbers describe the chip, not your board.

Security, regulation and interoperability

Connecting a greenhouse to the internet connects it to everyone on the internet. The canonical warning is the Mirai botnet of 2016, which scanned the internet for cameras, routers and other devices that still used factory-default passwords and at its peak controlled several hundred thousand of them, using them for some of the largest denial-of-service attacks recorded at the time (Antonakakis et al., 2017). The food sector is an attractive target: manipulated set points can kill a crop or a fish stock, falsified sensor records undermine traceability, and ransomware can stop a processing plant. Security therefore belongs in the first design sketch, not in the last week of the project.

Security has also become a legal requirement for products sold in the EU. The situation as of September 2026:

16 January 2023

NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555 enters into force; member states had to transpose it by 17 October 2024. "Production, processing and distribution of food" is among the "other critical sectors" of its Annex II, so medium and large food companies must manage cybersecurity risks and report significant incidents (European Parliament and Council, 2022).

1 August 2025

11 September 2026

First obligations of the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, in force since 10 December 2024) apply: manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents in products with digital elements — an early warning within 24 hours and a notification within 72 hours (European Parliament and Council, 2024).

11 December 2027

The CRA's main requirements apply to products placed on the EU market: security by design and by default, no known exploitable vulnerabilities, security updates throughout a declared support period, a software bill of materials and a conformity assessment before the CE mark (European Commission, n.d.).

Does this apply to your logger?

A logger you build for your own experiment is not "placed on the market", so the CRA and the RED do not apply to it. But if a student project becomes a product — a start-up selling nutrient-monitoring kits, say — both will. Treat Table 8 as good engineering today and as the minimum legal standard tomorrow.

The last cross-cutting concern is interoperability: the ability of devices and software from different makers to exchange data and understand it. It works at three levels. Protocol interoperability means speaking the same transport (MQTT, CoAP, HTTP). Syntactic interoperability means agreeing on the format (JSON, CBOR, SenML). Semantic interoperability means agreeing on what the numbers mean — the measurand, the unit, the location, the quality flag — for which the OGC SensorThings API and the W3C/OGC SOSA/SSN ontology provide standard vocabularies (Liang et al., 2016; Haller et al., 2019). Without the third level, two systems can exchange data flawlessly and still misinterpret every value. Closed ecosystems in which data can only be read through one vendor's app are a real risk for growers, and the Data Act is partly a response to it.

The firmware follows one principle: store first, then send. Every reading is written to the SD card before any attempt to publish, so a network failure never loses data. The outline below uses common Arduino libraries (OneWire, DallasTemperature, Adafruit SHT4x and ADS1X15, RTClib, SD, and an MQTT client that supports QoS 1, retained messages and a Last Will); check each library's documentation for exact function signatures, and keep credentials in a separate file that is never committed to Git.

// ESP32 project logger — outline for the Arduino core for ESP32 (helper functions not shown)
#include <WiFi.h>             #include <WiFiClientSecure.h>   #include <MQTT.h>
#include <OneWire.h>          #include <DallasTemperature.h>  #include <Adafruit_SHT4x.h>
#include <Adafruit_ADS1X15.h> #include <RTClib.h>             #include <SD.h>
#include "secrets.h"   // WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS, BROKER_HOST, BROKER_CA — never in Git

const uint32_t PERIOD_S = 300;        // one record every 5 min (averaged 10-s samples)
const String BASE = "mdu/gh1/zone2/"; // topic prefix: site / greenhouse / zone
OneWire ow(4);  DallasTemperature tank(&ow);
Adafruit_SHT4x sht;  Adafruit_ADS1115 ads;  RTC_DS3231 rtc;
WiFiClientSecure net;  MQTTClient mqtt(512);      // QoS 1, retained, last will
struct Cal { float a, b; };            // indication = a + b·x (Eq. 9.3.5)
Cal calTank = { 0.317f, 0.9949f };     // from your own calibration

void setup() {
  Wire.begin(21, 22);  sht.begin();  ads.begin(0x48);  rtc.begin();  tank.begin();
  SD.begin(5);                         // SPI 18/19/23, chip select 5
  WiFi.begin(WIFI_SSID, WIFI_PASS);
  configTime(0, 0, "pool.ntp.org");    // UTC; copy to the DS3231 once synced
  net.setCACert(BROKER_CA);            // verify the broker's certificate
  mqtt.begin(BROKER_HOST, 8883, net);  // MQTT over TLS
  mqtt.setWill((BASE + "nodes/esp32-07/status").c_str(), "offline", true, 1);
}

void loop() {
  mqtt.loop();
  if (!timeForNextRecord(PERIOD_S)) return;
  tank.requestTemperatures();          // ≈ 750 ms at 12-bit resolution
  float yT = tank.getTempCByIndex(0);   // raw: −127 = disconnected, 85 = reset
  float T  = (yT - calTank.a) / calTank.b;   // inverse prediction (Eq. 9.3.6)
  sensors_event_t rh, ta;  sht.getEvent(&rh, &ta);
  float vpd = vpdKPa(ta.temperature, rh.relative_humidity);   // FAO-56
  int16_t phCode = medianOfReads(9, 0);   // median of 9 readings on A0
  // QC flag (Lesson 9.3): 4 = fail, 3 = suspect, 1 = pass
  int q = (yT < -50 || yT > 84) ? 4 : (T < 5 || T > 35) ? 3 : 1;

  String ts = isoTimeUTC(rtc.now());   // e.g. 2026-09-26T10:05:00Z
  // store first: the SD card is the level-0 archive
  appendCsv("/log.csv", ts, T, q, ta.temperature, rh.relative_humidity, vpd, phCode);
  if (ensureConnected()) {              // reconnect with back-off, resend backlog
    mqtt.publish(BASE + "nutrient/water-temperature",
                 jsonValue(ts, T, "Cel", q), false, 1);
    mqtt.publish(BASE + "nodes/esp32-07/status", "online", true, 1);  // retained
  }
}

Frontier — intelligence at the edge

Microcontrollers such as the ESP32-S3 can now run small neural networks ("TinyML") that classify images or detect anomalies on the node itself, sending a few bytes instead of megabytes. Combined with LPWAN radios and energy harvesting, this points to sensor networks that are cheap enough to monitor every plant row. The limiting factors are shifting from hardware to the topics of this module: calibration, uncertainty, data quality and security — and to the governance questions of Lesson 10.5.

Key terms

Internet of Things (IoT)
Networked sensing and actuating devices that exchange data with each other and with software services without human intervention.
Edge computing
Processing data on or near the devices that produce them — on the node, a gateway or a local controller — instead of in a remote data centre.
Microcontroller
A single-chip computer with processor, memory and peripherals (ADC, timers, serial interfaces) that runs one program with deterministic timing and very low sleep current.
I²C
A two-wire (SDA, SCL) synchronous bus on which devices are selected by 7-bit addresses; used for short, board-level connections.
SDI-12
A 1200-baud, three-wire (data, 12 V, ground) bus standard for environmental sensors with up to 62 addresses.
Modbus RTU
A request–response protocol, usually over an RS-485 differential pair, used by industrial and greenhouse equipment; addresses 1–247.
Link budget
The sum, in decibels, of transmit power, antenna gains and losses along a radio path, compared with the receiver sensitivity to give the link margin.
Path-loss exponent
The exponent n in received power ∝ d−n: 2 in free space, larger near the ground, among crops and inside buildings.
Receiver sensitivity
The weakest signal a receiver can decode at a stated error rate; set by thermal noise, bandwidth, noise figure and the required SNR.
Spreading factor
In LoRa, the number of bits per chirp symbol (7–12); each step up doubles the symbol time and improves sensitivity by about 2.5 dB.
Time on air
The duration of one radio transmission; decides energy use, collision probability and compliance with duty-cycle limits.
Duty cycle
The fraction of time a transmitter is on; limited to 1 % in the main EU868 LoRaWAN sub-band.
MQTT
An open publish/subscribe messaging protocol in which clients publish messages to hierarchical topics on a broker and subscribe with topic filters.
Quality of service (QoS)
MQTT delivery guarantee per message: 0 at most once, 1 at least once, 2 exactly once.
Last Will and Testament
A message registered by an MQTT client at connection, which the broker publishes if the client disconnects unexpectedly.
Cyber Resilience Act
Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 setting mandatory cybersecurity requirements for hardware and software products with digital elements placed on the EU market.
