Inferential statistics: t-tests, ANOVA and regression

Six light compartments with white LEDs gave lettuce heads of 147.6 g on average; six compartments with added far-red gave 166.4 g. Is far-red really better, or could chance alone produce a 19 g difference between two sets of six numbers? Inferential statistics answers such questions by imagining that the experiment is repeated many times, asking how much the results would wander, and quantifying how surprising the observed result would be if there were no effect at all. This lesson builds the tools of your project report — confidence intervals, t-tests, analysis of variance and regression — derives every one of them, applies them to the lettuce data of Lessons 3.1 and 3.2, and shows the ways in which they are most often misused.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain sampling distributions, the standard error and the central limit theorem, and derive, calculate and correctly interpret t-based confidence intervals (LO4).
  • Describe the logic of a significance test, interpret a p-value in line with the ASA statement, and relate the significance level, Type I and II errors and power (LO4).
  • Choose and carry out one-sample, Welch and paired t-tests, including the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (LO4).
  • Derive the sum-of-squares decomposition, build one-way and randomised-block ANOVA tables, and follow them with Tukey HSD comparisons (LO4).
  • Check assumptions with residual plots, QQ plots and the Brown–Forsythe test, and choose transformations or rank-based tests when assumptions fail (LO4, LO5).
  • Fit and interpret a simple linear regression with slope test, R², confidence and prediction bands, and report effect sizes while controlling error rates across many tests (LO4, LO5).

From one sample to many: sampling distributions

Your project will produce one data set: eighteen compartment means, a few weeks of sensor logs, one sensory panel. Had you sown the same seeds under the same lamps a second time, every number would have come out slightly different — other seedlings, another week, a pump that ran a little warmer. Statistical inference is the discipline of reasoning from the one sample you have to the population you care about, while stating honestly how wrong you might be. The population is usually hypothetical: all the lettuce that could be grown under your protocol. Its characteristics — the true mean fresh mass \(\mu\), the true standard deviation \(\sigma\) — are parameters: fixed, but unknown. The numbers you compute from your data — \(\bar x\), \(s\) — are statistics: known, but different in every sample.

Inference rests on a thought experiment (Figure 1). Imagine repeating the whole experiment — sowing, growing, harvesting, calculating — many times. Each repetition gives a new \(\bar x\). The distribution of a statistic over these imagined repetitions is its sampling distribution, and the standard deviation of that distribution is the standard error (SE) you met in Lesson 3.2 (Eq. 3.2.4). You never observe the sampling distribution directly — you grow the crop once — but probability theory tells you its shape, and that is enough to attach an error rate to every conclusion you draw.

For the sample mean, two facts make almost everything else in this lesson work. The first is exact: the mean of \(n\) independent observations has a sampling distribution centred on \(\mu\) with standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). The second is the most remarkable theorem in elementary statistics: whatever the shape of the population, the sampling distribution of the mean approaches a normal distribution as \(n\) grows.

Sampling distribution of the mean and the central limit theorem(3.3.1)
\[ \begin{gathered} \bar X = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i:\qquad \operatorname{E}(\bar X) = \mu,\qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \\[6pt] Z_n = \frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\ \xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }\ N(0,1) \end{gathered} \]

the sample mean is, on average, equal to the population mean; its standard deviation — the standard error — is the population standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size; and the standardised mean, the distance of the sample mean from the population mean in units of the standard error, has a distribution that approaches the standard normal distribution as the sample grows, whatever the shape of the population.

Follow the derivation (a sketch of the central limit theorem)
  1. Centre and spread are exact. By linearity of expectation, \(\operatorname{E}(\bar X) = \frac1n\sum \operatorname{E}(X_i) = \mu\). For independent observations variances add, so \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \frac{1}{n^2}\,n\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n\) (Lesson 3.2, Eq. 3.2.4). No normality is needed for these two results.
  2. Standardise. Write \(Y_i = (X_i-\mu)/\sigma\), so that \(\operatorname{E}(Y_i) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = 1\). Then \(Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\).
  3. Use the moment-generating function \(M_Y(u) = \operatorname{E}(e^{uY})\), which characterises a distribution. A Taylor expansion around \(u = 0\), using \(\operatorname{E}(Y) = 0\) and \(\operatorname{E}(Y^2) = 1\), gives \(M_Y(u) = 1 + \frac{u^2}{2} + O(u^3)\).
  4. Independence turns sums into products: \(M_{Z_n}(u) = \left[M_Y\!\left(u/\sqrt n\right)\right]^n = \left[1 + \frac{u^2}{2n} + O\!\left(n^{-3/2}\right)\right]^n\).
  5. Take the limit. Because \(\left(1+\frac{a}{n}\right)^n \to e^{a}\), \(M_{Z_n}(u) \to e^{u^2/2}\) — exactly the moment-generating function of \(N(0,1)\). The shape of the original distribution has disappeared; only its mean and variance survive.
  6. Rigour. This sketch assumes that the moment-generating function exists; the general proof (Lindeberg–Lévy) uses characteristic functions and needs only independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance.
Assumptions and validity: how large must n be?

The theorem is a statement about the limit; how fast the limit is approached depends on the population. For roughly symmetric populations — fresh masses of a uniform crop — the mean of 3–5 units is already close to normal. Strong skewness (seedling masses, bacterial counts, insect counts with many zeros) or a second mode (a few diseased plants) slows convergence, and the popular "n ≥ 30" is a rule of thumb, not a law. The theorem also says nothing about dependent observations: plants sharing one tank (Lesson 3.1, Eq. 3.1.3) do not average out as \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). Use Figure 2 to see both effects.

\(\operatorname{E}(\bar X)\) and \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) are in g, like the data; \(Z_n = \text{g}/\text{g}\) is dimensionless ✓

With a between-compartment SD of 12 g and six compartments, the SE is \(12/\sqrt6 = 4.9\) g, and the probability that one experiment's mean lands within ±5 g of the true mean is \(2\Phi(5\sqrt6/12) - 1 = 2\Phi(1.02) - 1 = 0.69\). Quadrupling the replication to 24 compartments halves the SE and raises the probability to 0.96 — the square-root law that makes replication expensive.

When σ is unknown: Student's t distribution

Eq. 3.3.1 standardises with the population SD, which you never know. Replacing \(\sigma\) by the sample SD \(s\) seems harmless, but with small samples it is not: \(s\) itself varies from sample to sample and is sometimes much too small, which produces more extreme standardised values than the normal curve predicts. The exact distribution of the standardised mean with \(s\) in the denominator was derived in 1908 by William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin who worked with small samples of barley and hops.

History

Gosset published "The probable error of a mean" in Biometrika under the pseudonym Student, because his employer restricted publication by its staff (Student, 1908). He checked his mathematics with a simulation done by hand: the heights and finger lengths of 3 000 criminals were written on cards, shuffled, and dealt into 750 samples of four. Ronald Fisher later recast the result in its modern form and made the "t-test" standard practice through Statistical Methods for Research Workers (Fisher, 1925), the book that also introduced the analysis of variance to agricultural science.

The t statistic and Student's t distribution(3.3.2)
\[ \begin{gathered} T = \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt{n}} \sim t_{\nu},\qquad \nu = n-1 \\[6pt] f_{\nu}(t) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\;\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} \end{gathered} \]

if the observations come from a normal population, the distance between the sample mean and the population mean, divided by the estimated standard error, follows a t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom. Its density is bell-shaped like the normal curve but has heavier tails, which become lighter as the degrees of freedom increase.

Follow the derivation
  1. For a normal population, \(Z = (\bar X-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\) is exactly \(N(0,1)\) for every \(n\).
  2. The scaled sample variance \(V = (n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\) follows a chi-square distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom — a sum of \(n-1\) squared independent standard normal variables; one degree of freedom is used up because the deviations are taken from \(\bar X\), not from \(\mu\) (the same reason for the \(n-1\) in Bessel's correction, Lesson 3.2).
  3. For normal data \(\bar X\) and \(S^2\) are independent (Cochran's theorem).
  4. Divide: \(T = \dfrac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n} = \dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-1)}}\). A standard normal divided by the square root of an independent chi-square over its degrees of freedom is, by definition, t-distributed with \(\nu = n-1\).
  5. As \(\nu \to\infty\), \(V/\nu \to 1\) (the estimate \(S\) becomes exact), the factor \((1+t^2/\nu)^{-(\nu+1)/2} \to e^{-t^2/2}\), and \(t_\nu \to N(0,1)\).
Assumptions and validity

Exact for independent observations from a normal population. Thanks to the central limit theorem the t procedures are robust: they remain approximately valid for moderately non-normal data, especially for two-sided tests and balanced designs. They are not robust to dependence between observations (pseudo-replication) and are sensitive to strong skewness with very small \(n\) and to outliers.

Confidence intervals: estimates with honest error bars

The most useful product of an experiment is not a yes/no verdict but an estimate with its uncertainty: "far-red increased fresh mass by 19 g, and the data are compatible with increases between about 6 and 32 g". Such a statement is a confidence interval (CI), and Eq. 3.3.2 is all you need to build one.

t-based confidence interval for a population mean(3.3.3)
\[ \bar x \;\pm\; t_{1-\alpha/2,\;n-1}\;\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

the \(100(1-\alpha)\) % confidence interval for the population mean extends from the sample mean minus to the sample mean plus the critical t value times the standard error. For a 95 % interval, \(\alpha = 0.05\) and the critical value is the 97.5th percentile of the t distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.

Follow the derivation: inverting a pivot
  1. A pivot is a function of the data and the unknown parameter whose distribution does not depend on the parameter. Eq. 3.3.2 provides one: \(T = (\bar X-\mu)/(S/\sqrt n)\sim t_{n-1}\), whatever \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are.
  2. Because the t distribution is symmetric, \(P\left(-t^* \le T \le t^*\right) = 1-\alpha\) with \(t^* = t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\).
  3. Substitute \(T\) and multiply all three parts by \(S/\sqrt n > 0\): \(P\left(-t^*\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n} \le \bar X-\mu \le t^*\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\right) = 1-\alpha\).
  4. Rearrange so that \(\mu\) stands alone in the middle (subtract \(\bar X\), multiply by −1, which reverses the inequalities): \(P\left(\bar X - t^*\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n} \le \mu \le \bar X + t^*\tfrac{S}{\sqrt n}\right) = 1-\alpha\).
  5. Read the last statement carefully: the endpoints are random, \(\mu\) is fixed. Before the experiment, the procedure has a probability \(1-\alpha\) of producing an interval that covers \(\mu\). After the experiment, the numbers are fixed, and the interval either covers \(\mu\) or it does not.
Assumptions and validity

Independent observations (experimental units), and a normal population or a sample large enough for the central limit theorem. For the difference between two independent means the same logic gives \((\bar x_1-\bar x_2) \pm t^*\,\text{SE}_{\text{diff}}\), with the standard error and degrees of freedom of Welch's test (Eq. 3.3.7). A 95 % CI for a difference excludes zero exactly when the corresponding two-sided test gives \(p < 0.05\): intervals and tests are two views of the same calculation.

Common misconception

"There is a 95 % probability that the true mean lies between 157.3 and 175.5 g." Strictly, no: the 95 % belongs to the procedure, not to one interval — 95 % of intervals built this way cover the truth (Greenland et al., 2016). A safe phrasing for a report is: "the data are compatible with population means between 157.3 and 175.5 g (95 % CI)". Values near the middle of the interval are more compatible with the data than values near its ends.

Two practical consequences follow from Eq. 3.3.3. First, the width shrinks with \(1/\sqrt n\) and, for small samples, also through the falling t quantile: going from 3 to 6 units narrows a 95 % interval by 58 % (the quantile drops from 4.303 to 2.571 and \(\sqrt n\) grows), whereas going from 30 to 60 narrows it by only 31 %. Second, what matters is the number of experimental units: six compartments with four plants each give an interval based on \(n = 6\), not \(n = 24\) (Lesson 3.1).

The logic of significance tests and the meaning of p

A significance test turns the question "is there an effect?" into a calculation under the assumption that there is none. The recipe has six steps, and the first two must be completed before you look at the data:

  1. State the null and alternative hypotheses (Lesson 3.1, Eq. 3.1.1), e.g., \(H_0: \mu_{\text{FR}}-\mu_{\text{W}} = 0\) against \(H_1: \mu_{\text{FR}}-\mu_{\text{W}} \ne 0\).
  2. Choose the significance level \(\alpha\) — the Type I error rate you will tolerate, conventionally 0.05 — and the test.
  3. Compute a test statistic that measures how far the data depart from \(H_0\) in units of their standard error, such as \(t\).
  4. Derive its null distribution: the sampling distribution of the statistic if \(H_0\) and all model assumptions are true (for \(t\): Eq. 3.3.2 with \(\mu = \mu_0\)).
  5. Compute the p-value: how often the null distribution produces a result at least as extreme as the observed one.
  6. If \(p \le \alpha\), reject \(H_0\) ("statistically significant"); otherwise do not reject it. Then — more importantly — report the size of the effect and its confidence interval.
The p-value of a t-test(3.3.4)
\[ \begin{gathered} p = P\!\left(|T| \ge |t_{\text{obs}}| \;\middle|\; H_0\right) = 2\left[1-F_{\nu}\!\left(|t_{\text{obs}}|\right)\right] \\[6pt] p_{\text{one-sided}} = 1-F_{\nu}\!\left(t_{\text{obs}}\right) \end{gathered} \]

the two-sided p-value is the probability, calculated on the assumption that the null hypothesis and the model are true, of obtaining a t statistic at least as far from zero as the one observed, in either direction; it equals twice the upper-tail area of the t distribution beyond the absolute observed value. A one-sided p-value counts only the tail in the predicted direction.

Follow the derivation — and why p is uniform under H₀
  1. "At least as extreme" means at least as far from what \(H_0\) predicts (\(t = 0\)): the set \(\{|T|\ge|t_{\text{obs}}|\}\), both tails for a two-sided alternative.
  2. By the symmetry of the t density, \(P(T \ge |t_{\text{obs}}|) = P(T\le-|t_{\text{obs}}|) = 1-F_\nu(|t_{\text{obs}}|)\); the two tails together give the factor 2.
  3. The decision rule "reject when \(p\le\alpha\)" is identical to "reject when \(|t_{\text{obs}}|\ge t_{1-\alpha/2,\nu}\)": both describe the same tail area.
  4. Under \(H_0\), the p-value is itself a random variable with a uniform distribution on [0, 1]: for any \(a\), \(P(p\le a) = P\left(|T|\ge t_{1-a/2,\nu}\right) = a\). Every p-value is equally likely when there is no effect — so \(p = 0.04\) is not rare, it happens in 4 % of null experiments. Hence the Type I error rate of the rule "reject if \(p\le\alpha\)" is exactly \(\alpha\).
Assumptions and validity: p tests the whole model

The p-value is computed from a model that includes \(H_0\) and independence, the distributional assumptions, the absence of measurement bias and the absence of selective reporting. A small p signals that the data are unusual under this whole model, not necessarily that \(H_0\) is the part that failed (Greenland et al., 2016). A one-sided p is legitimate only if the direction was specified before the data were seen (Lesson 3.1).

Two kinds of error, and power

Because the decision is made under uncertainty, it can be wrong in two ways (Lesson 3.1, Table 3). A Type I error — a false positive — rejects a true \(H_0\); the rule "reject if \(p\le\alpha\)" makes its probability exactly \(\alpha\). A Type II error — a false negative — fails to reject a false \(H_0\); its probability \(\beta\) depends on the size of the true effect, the variability and \(n\). The power \(1-\beta\) is the probability of detecting an effect that really exists. Lowering \(\alpha\) from 0.05 to 0.01 protects against false positives but, for fixed \(n\), lowers power; only more (or less variable) experimental units improve both at once. Lesson 3.1 (Eq. 3.1.8 and Figure 6) shows how to choose \(n\) for a target power; Figure 5 below lets you watch both error rates emerge from simulated experiments.

What the reader of your report usually wants to know, however, is the reverse probability: given that the result is significant, how likely is it that the effect is real? That depends on something no single experiment can tell you — how often the hypotheses you test are true in the first place.

False positive risk of a significant result(3.3.5)
\[ \text{FPR} = P\!\left(H_0 \text{ true} \mid p\le\alpha\right) = \frac{\alpha\,(1-\pi)}{\alpha\,(1-\pi) + (1-\beta)\,\pi} \]

the false positive risk — the fraction of significant results that are false alarms — equals the number of false positives (alpha times the share of tested hypotheses that are null) divided by all positives (false positives plus true positives, which are power times the share of hypotheses that are true).

Assumptions and validity

A simplified model: all real effects have the same power and nothing but chance produces false positives. Selective reporting and flexible analyses (Section 3.3.8) raise the FPR further. The argument is the core of Ioannidis's (2005) "Why most published research findings are false" and of Colquhoun's (2014) warning that \(p\approx0.05\) is much weaker evidence than it seems.

What a p-value is not

In 2016 the American Statistical Association took the unprecedented step of publishing a formal statement on p-values, because their misuse had become a threat to the credibility of science (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). Its six principles are worth learning by heart:

  1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
  2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.
  3. Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.
  4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency.
  5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
  6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis.

Greenland et al. (2016) list 25 common misinterpretations. Four are especially frequent in student reports. "p = 0.03 means there is a 3 % chance that the null hypothesis is true" — no: p is computed assuming \(H_0\) is true, and the probability that \(H_0\) is true given the data is the FPR of Eq. 3.3.5, which can be far larger. "p = 0.21, so there is no effect" — no: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence (Altman & Bland, 1995); look at the CI, which may include large effects. "p < 0.001, so the effect is large" — no: with enough units, trivially small effects become significant. "Treatment A was significant (p = 0.04) and B was not (p = 0.07), so A works better than B" — no: the difference between "significant" and "not significant" is not itself statistically significant (Gelman & Stern, 2006); compare A and B directly.

Frontier: beyond "p < 0.05"

A 2019 special issue of The American Statistician recommended abandoning the phrase "statistically significant" altogether and summarised its advice as "ATOM: accept uncertainty, be thoughtful, open and modest" (Wasserstein et al., 2019); the same week, more than 800 scientists backed a call in Nature to retire dichotomous significance (Amrhein et al., 2019). Others proposed keeping thresholds but lowering them to \(p < 0.005\) for new discoveries (Benjamin et al., 2018). The course's position: fix \(\alpha\) in your preregistration, report exact p-values together with effect sizes and confidence intervals, and let the size and precision of the effect — not a threshold — drive your conclusions.

Run a few single experiments at \(d = 1.25\) and \(n = 6\) — the situation of a student project with a realistic effect — and watch the p-value jump from 0.0003 to 0.4 and back. Cumming (2008) called this the dance of the p-values: an exact replication of an experiment can give a very different p. After 1 000 runs, about half of the experiments are significant (power ≈ 0.48), and the significant ones overestimate the true effect by about a third (exaggeration ratio ≈ 1.3); for a small true effect (\(d = 0.5\)) they overestimate it almost threefold. This is the "winner's curse" of underpowered research that Lesson 3.1 warned about (Button et al., 2013): if your project finds a significant effect with \(n = 6\), your estimate is probably too optimistic, and the CI tells you by how much it might be.

To compare two treatments whose experimental units are independent — W+FR compartments versus W compartments — we need the standard error of a difference. Variances of independent quantities add, so \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_1-\bar X_2) = \sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_2^2/n_2\). The classical Student test assumes \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2\) and pools the two SDs; Welch's version does not, and it has become the recommended default because it loses almost nothing when the variances are equal and protects the error rate when they are not (Ruxton, 2006; Delacre et al., 2017).

Welch's two-sample t-test and the Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom(3.3.7)
\[ t = \frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}},\qquad \nu_{W} = \frac{\left(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^{2}}{\dfrac{\left(s_1^2/n_1\right)^{2}}{n_1-1}+\dfrac{\left(s_2^2/n_2\right)^{2}}{n_2-1}} \]

the t statistic is the difference between the two sample means divided by the square root of the sum of their squared standard errors; the approximate degrees of freedom are the squared sum of the squared standard errors divided by the sum of each squared standard error squared over its own degrees of freedom. The result lies between the smaller of n₁ − 1 and n₂ − 1 and n₁ + n₂ − 2.

Follow the derivation
  1. Standard error. The means are independent, so \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_1-\bar X_2) = \sigma_1^2/n_1+\sigma_2^2/n_2\); estimate it by \(\hat V = s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2\) and take the square root.
  2. The problem. \(t = (\bar x_1-\bar x_2)/\sqrt{\hat V}\) would be exactly t-distributed only if \(\hat V\) were a multiple of a chi-square variable. It is a weighted sum of two independent chi-squares with different weights, which is not.
  3. Satterthwaite's idea (1946): approximate \(\hat V\) by a scaled chi-square, \(\hat V \approx V\chi^2_\nu/\nu\), with \(\nu\) chosen so that mean and variance match. A variable \(V\chi^2_\nu/\nu\) has mean \(V\) and variance \(2V^2/\nu\).
  4. For normal data \(\operatorname{Var}(s_i^2) = 2\sigma_i^4/(n_i-1)\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat V) = \sum_i \dfrac{2\sigma_i^4}{n_i^2(n_i-1)}\).
  5. Equate: \(\dfrac{2V^2}{\nu} = \sum_i\dfrac{2(\sigma_i^2/n_i)^2}{n_i-1}\) ⇒ \(\nu = \dfrac{\left(\sum_i\sigma_i^2/n_i\right)^2}{\sum_i(\sigma_i^2/n_i)^2/(n_i-1)}\). Replacing \(\sigma_i^2\) by \(s_i^2\) gives \(\nu_W\) (Welch, 1947).
  6. Special cases. With equal \(n\) and equal variances, \(\nu_W = 2(n-1)\) and Welch's test coincides with Student's. When one variance dominates, \(\nu_W\) falls towards the \(n-1\) of that group — the test becomes appropriately cautious.

Paired t-test(3.3.8)
\[ \begin{gathered} d_i = x_{1i}-x_{2i},\qquad t = \frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt{n}},\qquad \nu = n-1 \\[6pt] \operatorname{Var}(d) = \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 \end{gathered} \]

compute the difference within each pair; the test statistic is the mean difference divided by its standard error, with the number of pairs minus one degrees of freedom. The variance of a difference is the sum of the two variances minus twice their covariance, so a positive correlation between the members of a pair makes the differences much less variable.

Follow the derivation
  1. Write each observation as treatment mean + pair effect + noise: \(x_{1i} = \mu_1 + b_i + e_{1i}\), \(x_{2i} = \mu_2 + b_i + e_{2i}\).

The pairing must come from the design — blocks, the same plant measured twice, the same panellist tasting two samples — and be decided before the data are seen. The differences should be approximately normal; the individual measurements need not be.

Common misconception

"We ran both the paired and the unpaired test and reported the one with the smaller p-value." The analysis must follow the design: if the design has blocks or pairs, the paired (blocked) analysis is correct, and if it does not, pairing arbitrary observations is meaningless. Choosing between analyses after seeing the results is a form of p-hacking (Section 3.3.8).

Analysis of variance: more than two treatments

The spectrum experiment has three treatments, and many projects have more (five EC levels, four substrates). Testing every pair with a t-test multiplies the opportunities for false positives: with three independent comparisons at \(\alpha = 0.05\), the probability of at least one false positive when all nulls are true approaches \(1-0.95^3 = 14\) %, and with five treatments (ten pairs) about 40 % (Eq. 3.3.19). Fisher's analysis of variance (ANOVA) instead asks one question of all groups at once — \(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k\) — by comparing two estimates of the same variance.

The model is \(y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \varepsilon_{ij}\): observation \(j\) of treatment \(i\) is the grand mean plus a treatment effect plus random error with variance \(\sigma^2\) (the one-factor version of Eq. 3.1.2). The idea is geometric. Every observation's deviation from the grand mean can be split into two pieces: how far its group mean lies from the grand mean (between-group) and how far the observation lies from its group mean (within-group). Squaring and summing preserves the split.

Sum-of-squares decomposition of one-way ANOVA(3.3.9)
\[ \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}\left(y_{ij}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2}_{\text{SS}_T,\ N-1\ \text{df}} = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i\left(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2}_{\text{SS}_B,\ k-1\ \text{df}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}\left(y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot}\right)^2}_{\text{SS}_W,\ N-k\ \text{df}} \]

the total sum of squared deviations from the grand mean equals the between-groups sum of squares — each group mean's squared deviation from the grand mean, weighted by the group size — plus the within-groups sum of squares, the squared deviations of the observations from their own group means. The degrees of freedom add up in the same way.

Follow the proof
  1. Add and subtract the group mean: \(y_{ij}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot} = (\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}) + (y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot})\). This is an identity for every observation.
  2. Square: \((y_{ij}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2 = (\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2 + (y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot})^2 + 2(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})(y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot})\).
  3. Sum over \(j\) within group \(i\). The first term does not depend on \(j\) and appears \(n_i\) times: \(n_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2\).
  4. The cross-product vanishes: \(2(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})\sum_j (y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot}) = 2(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})\times0\), because deviations from a mean always sum to zero (Eq. 3.2.1).
  5. Sum over \(i\) to obtain \(\text{SS}_T = \text{SS}_B + \text{SS}_W\). ∎
  6. Degrees of freedom. \(\text{SS}_T\) uses \(N\) deviations constrained by one mean: \(N-1\). \(\text{SS}_B\) uses \(k\) group means constrained by the grand mean: \(k-1\). \(\text{SS}_W\) uses \(N\) deviations constrained by \(k\) group means: \(N-k\). And \((k-1)+(N-k) = N-1\).
  7. Geometrically, the between and within deviation vectors are orthogonal in \(N\)-dimensional space, so the decomposition is Pythagoras' theorem — which is why squares, not absolute values, are used.
Assumptions and validity

The decomposition is pure algebra and holds for any numbers. The assumptions (independence, normal errors, equal variances) are needed only for the F test built on it (Eq. 3.3.10).

Mean squares, the F ratio and expected mean squares(3.3.10)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{MS}_B = \frac{\text{SS}_B}{k-1},\quad \text{MS}_W = \frac{\text{SS}_W}{N-k},\quad F = \frac{\text{MS}_B}{\text{MS}_W}\ \overset{H_0}{\sim}\ F_{k-1,\,N-k} \\[6pt] \operatorname{E}(\text{MS}_W) = \sigma^2,\qquad \operatorname{E}(\text{MS}_B) = \sigma^2+\frac{\sum_i n_i\tau_i^2}{k-1} \end{gathered} \]

dividing each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom gives a mean square; the within-groups mean square estimates the error variance whatever the treatment effects are, whereas the between-groups mean square estimates the error variance plus a term that grows with the treatment effects. Their ratio F is therefore close to one when the null hypothesis is true and large when it is false, and under the null hypothesis it follows an F distribution with k − 1 and N − k degrees of freedom.

  2. Between. Under the model, \(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot} = \tau_i + (\bar\varepsilon_{i\cdot}-\bar\varepsilon_{\cdot\cdot})\).   4. Distribution. For normal errors, \(\text{SS}_W/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{N-k}\) and, under \(H_0\), \(\text{SS}_B/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{k-1}\), independently. The ratio of two independent chi-squares, each divided by its degrees of freedom, has an F distribution: the unknown \(\sigma^2\) cancels.
  5. Link with t. For \(k = 2\) groups, \(F = t^2\) of the pooled t-test and \(F_{1,\nu} = t_\nu^2\) — ANOVA generalises the two-sample test.

Independent experimental units, normal errors, equal variances in all groups. The F test is fairly robust to non-normality and — with equal group sizes — to moderately unequal variances; it is not robust to dependence. A significant F says only that not all means are equal; which ones differ is the job of post-hoc comparisons (Eq. 3.3.11).

A significant F tells you that the spectra are not all equal, but not which pairs differ. Testing all \(k(k-1)/2\) pairs with ordinary t-tests would inflate the error rate again. John Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) controls the probability of at least one false positive across all pairwise comparisons at \(\alpha\), by asking how large the range of \(k\) means (largest minus smallest) could become by chance (Tukey, 1949; Kramer, 1956).

Tukey's honestly significant difference(3.3.11)
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{HSD} &= q_{1-\alpha;\,k,\,\nu_E}\,\sqrt{\frac{\text{MS}_E}{n}} \\[6pt] \text{HSD}_{ij} &= q_{1-\alpha;\,k,\,\nu_E}\sqrt{\frac{\text{MS}_E}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n_i}+\frac{1}{n_j}\right)}\quad\text{(unequal } n\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

two treatment means differ significantly if their difference exceeds the critical value of the studentised range distribution for k means and the error degrees of freedom, multiplied by the standard error of one treatment mean, the square root of the error mean square divided by the number of units per treatment.

Follow the derivation
  1. If all \(k\) population means are equal, the \(k\) sample means are \(k\) draws from a normal distribution with SD \(\sigma/\sqrt n\).
  2. The studentised range \(Q = (\bar y_{\max}-\bar y_{\min})/\sqrt{\text{MS}_E/n}\) — the range of the means in estimated standard errors — has a known distribution depending only on \(k\) and \(\nu_E\).
  3. \(P(Q\le q_{1-\alpha}) = 1-\alpha\). If the largest difference exceeds \(q_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{\text{MS}_E/n}\) with probability only \(\alpha\) under \(H_0\), then so does any difference: the error rate is controlled for the whole family of comparisons.
  4. Check with \(k = 2\): \(q_{0.95;2,10} = 3.151 = \sqrt2\times t_{0.975,10} = \sqrt2\times2.228\) — Tukey reduces to the ordinary t-test when there is only one pair.
  5. Simultaneous CIs for each difference: \((\bar y_i-\bar y_j)\pm\text{HSD}\).
Assumptions and validity

The ANOVA assumptions, with \(\text{MS}_E\) taken from the analysis that matches the design (for an RCBD, the error term after removing blocks). If you only want to compare each treatment with a control, Dunnett's (1955) procedure is more powerful; a few pre-planned contrasts can be tested with a Bonferroni or Holm correction (Eq. 3.3.19).

Checking assumptions: residuals, QQ plots and rank-based tests

Every p-value in this lesson is computed from a model, and the model's assumptions are part of the hypothesis being tested. For t-tests, ANOVA and regression they are, in order of importance:

  1. Independence of the errors — guaranteed only by the design: one value per experimental unit, randomisation, no pseudo-replication (Lesson 3.1). Violations cannot be repaired by any test afterwards; they make p-values far too small.
  2. Equal variances (homoscedasticity) across groups or along a regression line. Matters most when group sizes differ.
  3. Normality of the errors — of the residuals, not of the raw data pooled over treatments. Least important for moderate, balanced samples thanks to the central limit theorem, but outliers and strong skewness distort means and SDs.
  4. The right structure: additivity of block and treatment effects (RCBD), linearity (regression).

All are checked through the residuals \(e_{ij} = y_{ij}-\hat y_{ij}\), the observations minus the values the model predicts (group means in one-way ANOVA; grand mean + treatment + block effect in an RCBD; the fitted line in regression). Two plots do most of the work. A plot of residuals against fitted values should show a shapeless horizontal band; a funnel means that variability grows with the mean, a curve means a missing term. A normal quantile–quantile (QQ) plot places each ordered residual against the value a normal distribution would predict for its rank; normal residuals fall on a straight line, skewness bends it, heavy tails give an S shape, and an outlier sits alone at one end. Formal normality tests such as Shapiro–Wilk (Shapiro & Wilk, 1965) are less useful than they seem: with six units they rarely detect anything, and with hundreds they flag harmless deviations. Equality of variances, however, has a robust and widely used test.

Brown–Forsythe (median-centred Levene) test for equal variances(3.3.12)
\[ \begin{gathered} z_{ij} = \left|\,y_{ij}-\tilde y_{i}\,\right| \\[6pt] F_{\text{BF}} = \frac{\sum_{i} n_i\left(\bar z_{i\cdot}-\bar z_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2/(k-1)}{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\left(z_{ij}-\bar z_{i\cdot}\right)^2/(N-k)}\ \overset{H_0}{\approx}\ F_{k-1,\,N-k} \end{gathered} \]

replace every observation by its absolute distance from its own group median, then run an ordinary one-way ANOVA on these distances: if the groups are equally variable, their average distances from the median should be similar and F close to one.

Independent observations. Like all tests, it has little power with a few units per group: \(p > 0.05\) is not proof of equal variances. With equal \(n\), ANOVA tolerates variance ratios up to about 3–4; beyond that — or with unequal \(n\) — use Welch's ANOVA or a transformation.

What should you do when the diagnostics complain? First look for a reason in your notebook: a leaking jar or a diseased plant is a question of data handling, decided by a pre-specified exclusion rule (Lesson 3.2, Figure 6), not by a test. Then choose a remedy that respects the biology. Plant growth is multiplicative — treatments change mass by a percentage, and bigger plants vary more in absolute terms — so a log transformation is often the natural scale: differences of logs are log ratios, and back-transformed means are geometric means. If variances differ but the shape is fine, use Welch's ANOVA (the k-group analogue of Eq. 3.3.7). If the data are ordinal (a tipburn score from 0 to 4) or strongly non-normal with small \(n\), use a rank-based test, which replaces the values by their ranks and therefore cannot be dominated by a single extreme value.

Rank-based tests: Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H(3.3.13)
\[ \begin{gathered} U_1 = R_1-\frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2},\qquad \operatorname{E}(U) = \frac{n_1n_2}{2} \\[6pt] \operatorname{Var}(U) = \frac{n_1n_2(n_1+n_2+1)}{12} \\[6pt] H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{R_i^2}{n_i}-3(N+1)\ \overset{H_0}{\approx}\ \chi^2_{k-1} \end{gathered} \]

rank all observations together. For two groups, U is the rank sum of group 1 minus its smallest possible value; it counts how many of the n₁n₂ cross-group pairs are won by group 1, and under the null hypothesis it is centred on half of them. For k groups, H measures how far the groups' rank sums deviate from what random ranks would give and is compared with a chi-square distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(R_i\)sum of the ranks of group \(i\) in the combined ranking (ties get the average rank)–
\(U_1\)number of pairs \((x \text{ from group 1}, y \text{ from group 2})\) with \(x > y\) (ties count ½)–
\(H\)Kruskal–Wallis statistic–
\(N\), \(n_i\)total number of units, units in group \(i\)–
Follow the derivation
  1. If \(H_0\) (identical distributions) holds, every assignment of the \(N\) ranks to the groups is equally likely — the same randomisation argument as in Lesson 3.1.
  2. The smallest possible rank sum of group 1 is \(1+2+\dots+n_1 = n_1(n_1+1)/2\); every time a group-1 value beats a group-2 value, its rank rises by one, so \(U_1 = R_1-n_1(n_1+1)/2\) counts the wins.
  3. Each of the \(n_1n_2\) pairs is a win with probability ½ under \(H_0\): \(\operatorname{E}(U) = n_1n_2/2\). The variance follows from the variance of a sum of ranks drawn without replacement from \(1,\dots,N\).
  4. For small samples the exact p-value is found by counting: with \(n_1 = n_2 = 6\) there are \(\binom{12}{6} = 924\) equally likely rank assignments. For larger samples, \(z = (U-\operatorname{E}U)/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}U}\) is approximately normal.
  5. Kruskal–Wallis is one-way ANOVA applied to ranks: \(H\) is the between-groups sum of squares of the ranks divided by the variance of the ranks \(N(N+1)/12\), which is approximately \(\chi^2_{k-1}\) (Kruskal & Wallis, 1952).
Assumptions and validity

Independent observations; no normality. The tests detect a tendency for one group to take larger values (stochastic dominance) and are tests of medians only if the distributions have the same shape. For normal data they are only slightly less powerful than t and F — about 95 % asymptotic efficiency — but with very small samples the discreteness of ranks limits the smallest attainable p-value (with 3 + 3 units, the exact two-sided p cannot fall below 0.10). Tie corrections apply to the variance formulas.

Ranks and counts are dimensionless; \(H\) and \(z\) are dimensionless ✓

Least-squares estimates of the regression line(3.3.14)
\[ \begin{gathered} b_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2} \\[6pt] b_0 = \bar y - b_1\,\bar x,\qquad \hat y_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i \end{gathered} \]

  2. \(\partial S/\partial b_0 = -2\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i) = 0\) ⇒ \(\bar y = b_0 + b_1\bar x\): the line passes through \((\bar x,\bar y)\), and the residuals sum to zero.

Correlation is not causation

Pearson's \(r\) (Eq. 3.3.15) measures how closely two variables follow a straight line, in either direction and without regard to which causes which. In an experiment, the treatment was assigned at random, so a slope can be read causally: changing EC changed fresh mass. In observational data — sensor logs, survey answers, measurements across student groups — an association can arise because a third variable drives both. Figure 10 shows a typical case from a growth room: jars near the centre of the fixture receive more light, which makes the plants grow faster and warms the nutrient solution, so solution temperature correlates with fresh mass even if temperature itself had no effect. Across countries, per-capita chocolate consumption correlates strongly with the number of Nobel laureates (Messerli, 2012) — a correlation that says more about wealth than about cocoa (Altman & Krzywinski, 2015a).

Effect sizes and the multiple-testing problem

The fifth ASA principle — a p-value does not measure the size of an effect — has a practical corollary: always report the effect itself. The most useful effect sizes are unstandardised, in the units of the response: "18.9 g (95 % CI 14.9–22.8 g)" or "+12.8 %" means something to a grower. Standardised effect sizes remove the units and are needed for power calculations (Lesson 3.1, Eq. 3.1.7) and for comparing studies in a meta-analysis (Lakens, 2013).

Standardised effect sizes: Cohen's d, η² and partial η²(3.3.18)
\[ \begin{gathered} d = \frac{\bar x_1-\bar x_2}{s_p},\qquad s_p = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}} \\[6pt] \eta^2 = \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{effect}}}{\text{SS}_T},\qquad \eta_p^2 = \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{effect}}}{\text{SS}_{\text{effect}}+\text{SS}_E} \end{gathered} \]

Cohen's d is the difference between two means in units of their pooled standard deviation; eta squared is the share of the total sum of squares explained by an effect, and partial eta squared the share of the effect-plus-error variation, which ignores other effects such as blocks.

Follow the reasoning
  1. \(d\) answers "how many within-group SDs apart are the means?". Its relation to the t statistic of the pooled test is \(d = t\sqrt{1/n_1+1/n_2}\) — the same information, without the \(\sqrt n\) that makes t grow with sample size.
  2. For large samples \(d\) is approximately normal with \(\operatorname{SE}(d) \approx \sqrt{\dfrac{n_1+n_2}{n_1n_2}+\dfrac{d^2}{2(n_1+n_2)}}\) (Hedges & Olkin, 1985), which gives an approximate CI.
  3. \(\eta^2\) comes straight from the ANOVA table (Eq. 3.3.9). In a design with blocks, \(\eta^2\) is diluted by the block SS, so \(\eta_p^2\) — which compares the effect with the error only — is usually reported.
  4. Cohen's (1988) labels (d = 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) were meant for behavioural science; in controlled plant experiments \(d > 1\) is common, so interpret effects in grams and percent first.
Assumptions and validity

\(d\) assumes similar SDs in the two groups (otherwise standardise by the control SD, "Glass's Δ"). All standardised effects depend on the design: blocking reduces \(s\) and inflates \(d_z\) and \(\eta_p^2\) relative to \(d\) and \(\eta^2\), so always state which one you report.

The second threat to honest inference is multiplicity. Every test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) has a 5 % false-positive rate when its null is true; run many and some will "succeed" by chance. A project that measures fresh mass, dry mass, leaf area, SPAD, nitrate and tipburn, analyses each with and without an outlier, and compares three spectra pairwise, has performed dozens of tests. Gelman and Loken (2014) call the many possible analyses the "garden of forking paths"; choosing among them after seeing the data (p-hacking; Simmons et al., 2011; Head et al., 2015) or inventing the hypothesis afterwards (HARKing; Kerr, 1998) produces convincing-looking false positives.

Family-wise error rate, Bonferroni and Holm corrections(3.3.19)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{FWER} = 1-(1-\alpha)^m \le m\,\alpha \\[6pt] \text{Bonferroni: } p_j\le\frac{\alpha}{m},\qquad \text{Holm: } p_{(j)}\le\frac{\alpha}{m-j+1}\quad (j = 1,2,\dots) \end{gathered} \]

when m independent true null hypotheses are each tested at level alpha, the probability of at least one false positive is one minus the probability that all m tests are correctly non-significant, and it never exceeds m times alpha. Bonferroni's correction tests each hypothesis at alpha divided by m; Holm's step-down version sorts the p-values from smallest to largest and compares the j-th smallest with alpha divided by (m − j + 1), stopping at the first failure.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(m\)number of tests in the family–
FWERfamily-wise error rate: probability of one or more false positives–
\(p_{(j)}\)\(j\)-th smallest p-value–
Follow the derivation
  1. For one true null, \(P(\text{no false positive}) = 1-\alpha\). For \(m\) independent true nulls, multiply: \((1-\alpha)^m\). The complement is the FWER.
  2. Whether or not the tests are independent, the union bound (Boole's inequality) gives \(P(\text{at least one}) \le \sum_j P(p_j\le\alpha_j) = m\alpha_j\). Setting \(\alpha_j = \alpha/m\) guarantees FWER ≤ \(\alpha\): Bonferroni.
  3. Holm (1979): once the smallest p-value has been rejected, at most \(m-1\) true nulls can remain, so the next one can be tested at \(\alpha/(m-1)\), and so on. Holm controls the FWER exactly as Bonferroni does but is uniformly more powerful.
  4. With many tests (sensor data, omics), controlling the false discovery rate — the expected share of false positives among the rejections — is often more sensible (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).
Assumptions and validity

A "family" is the set of tests supporting one conclusion — for example, all secondary responses of the project. Tukey's HSD (Eq. 3.3.11) is the tailored solution for all pairwise comparisons after ANOVA. Corrections are not needed for a single, preregistered primary hypothesis — another reason to define one (Lesson 3.1).

Watch out: the garden of forking paths

None of these corrections can fix analyses chosen after looking at the data. Decide the primary response, the test, the exclusion rules and the family of secondary comparisons in your preregistration (Lesson 3.1); label everything else as exploratory; and report every analysis you ran, not just the significant ones. The Swedish Research Council counts "misusing statistics, for example to incorrectly or improperly indicate statistical significance" among deviations from good research practice (Lesson 3.4).

Frontier: beyond the classical toolbox

Three extensions are now routine in plant science. Linear mixed models handle plants nested in tanks, repeated measurements and unbalanced blocks by estimating random effects (Bates et al., 2015). Resampling methods — the bootstrap (Efron, 1979) and permutation tests, which are the randomisation argument of Lesson 3.1 turned into an algorithm — give intervals and p-values without distributional formulas. Bayesian estimation replaces p-values by posterior distributions for the effect itself, which answers the question students usually ask ("how probable is it that far-red helps?") at the price of stating prior assumptions openly (Kruschke, 2013).

Key terms

Sampling distribution
The distribution of a statistic (e.g., the sample mean) over hypothetical repetitions of the whole experiment; its SD is the standard error.
Central limit theorem
The sampling distribution of a mean of n independent observations approaches a normal distribution as n grows, whatever the shape of the population (finite variance required).
t distribution
The distribution of a standardised mean when the SD is estimated from the data; bell-shaped with heavier tails than the normal, indexed by degrees of freedom.
Confidence interval
An interval produced by a procedure that covers the true parameter in a stated fraction (e.g., 95 %) of repeated experiments; a range of parameter values compatible with the data.
p-value
The probability, computed assuming the null hypothesis and all model assumptions are true, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
Type I and Type II errors
Rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive, probability α) and failing to reject a false one (false negative, probability β).
Welch's t-test
A two-sample t-test that does not assume equal variances, using the Welch–Satterthwaite approximation for the degrees of freedom.
Paired t-test
A one-sample t-test on within-pair differences, used when the design pairs or blocks the experimental units.
Analysis of variance (ANOVA)
A test of the equality of several means that partitions the total sum of squares into between- and within-group parts and compares their mean squares with an F ratio.
Tukey's HSD
The honestly significant difference: the smallest difference between two treatment means that is significant when all pairwise comparisons are made with a family-wise error rate α.
Residual
The difference between an observation and the value predicted by the fitted model; the basis for checking model assumptions.
QQ plot
A plot of ordered residuals against the quantiles expected under a normal distribution; normal data fall on a straight line.
Coefficient of determination (R²)
The share of the total variation of the response explained by a regression model; equals r² in simple regression.
Prediction interval
An interval expected to contain a single new observation at a given predictor value; wider than the confidence interval for the mean response.
Family-wise error rate
The probability of at least one false positive among a family of tests; controlled by Bonferroni, Holm or Tukey procedures.
