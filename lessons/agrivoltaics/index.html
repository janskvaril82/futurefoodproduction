Agrivoltaics and multifunctional land use

On a ley field at Kärrbo prästgård, a few kilometres outside Västerås, rows of solar modules stand on edge like fences, facing east and west. Between them grass grows, is cut and fed to cattle; the modules deliver electricity in the morning and the evening. This is Sweden's first agrivoltaic system, studied since 2021 by researchers at Mälardalen University. Agrivoltaics asks a question that sounds like a paradox: can the same hectare produce both food and electricity — and together more of both than if we split it in two? To answer it we need photovoltaic physics, the geometry of shadows, the light-response of photosynthesis and an accounting tool, the land equivalent ratio. And because land is scarce and contested, we also need to understand the rules and the people.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the agrivoltaic concept, its origin (Goetzberger & Zastrow, 1982) and the main configurations — elevated, vertical bifacial east–west and tracking — with their advantages and drawbacks (LO1, LO7).
  • Calculate photovoltaic module output from irradiance, efficiency and cell temperature, and the bifacial gain and ground-coverage ratio of an array (LO3).
  • Compute shadow lengths from solar geometry and explain, with light-response curves, why partial shading reduces photosynthesis less than proportionally (LO2, LO3).
  • Derive and apply the land equivalent ratio (Dupraz et al., 2011), and interpret results from Heggelbach (Germany) and Kärrbo prästgård (Sweden) (LO3, LO7).
  • Discuss standards and policy (DIN SPEC 91434, the French 2023 law, the Swedish Environmental Code), land-use conflicts and social acceptance (LO1, LO6, LO7).

Food, electricity and land

Solar photovoltaics (PV) is the fastest-growing source of electricity in the world, and ground-mounted solar parks need land — on the order of one hectare or more per megawatt of peak power, depending on the design (compare Worked example 2). Farmland is attractive for developers: flat, open, accessible, close to the grid. Farmers and planners see the other side: arable land is a finite resource for food, and once it is covered with a conventional solar park it produces no crop for 25–40 years. In Sweden this conflict is written into law. The Environmental Code states that agriculture is of national importance and that brukningsvärd jordbruksmark — arable land suitable for cultivation — may be used for buildings or installations only if it is needed for essential public interests that cannot be met satisfactorily elsewhere (Miljöbalken 3 kap. 4 §).

Agrivoltaics (agri-PV, APV) combines PV and agriculture on the same land at the same time, with the agricultural use remaining primary. The idea is older than the modern solar industry. In 1982 Adolf Goetzberger, founder of the Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems, and Armin Zastrow showed that if solar collectors are raised about 2 m above the ground and the rows are spaced about three times the collector height apart, the ground receives a nearly uniform radiation, integrated over the day, of about two-thirds of the unshaded global radiation — enough for many crops (Goetzberger & Zastrow, 1982). Akira Nagashima built the first "solar sharing" prototypes in Japan in 2004, and Dupraz et al. (2011) coined the term agrivoltaic and introduced the land equivalent ratio as the yardstick for the concept. By 2020 at least 2.8 GWp of agrivoltaic capacity had been installed worldwide, with support schemes in Japan (since 2013), China, France, the United States and Korea (Fraunhofer ISE, 2020).

Why combine them? Plants use only a fraction of full sunlight efficiently: above a certain irradiance, leaf photosynthesis saturates (Section 11.5.5), so some of the light falling on a crop at midday is surplus that a solar module could use. A global analysis even found that croplands have the highest median solar power potential of all land-cover types — about 28 W m⁻² — partly because the microclimate over crops keeps panels cool (Adeh et al., 2019).

History

Goetzberger and Zastrow's 1982 paper was entirely theoretical: they derived the fraction of direct and diffuse light reaching the ground under a field of elevated collectors, for different seasons and latitudes. It took about three decades — and a dramatic fall in module prices — before the first research systems were built in France and Italy (around 2010–2011) and in Germany (Heggelbach, 2016) (Fraunhofer ISE, 2020).

Photovoltaic basics for agronomists

A photovoltaic cell converts photons into an electric current. Crystalline-silicon modules on the market today convert roughly 20–23 % of the incident solar energy into electricity at standard test conditions (STC: 1000 W m⁻² irradiance, 25 °C cell temperature, AM 1.5 spectrum). Two effects matter especially in agrivoltaics: temperature and the use of both sides of the module.

PV module power with temperature correction(11.5.1)
\[ P = \eta_{\text{ref}}\,A\,G\,\bigl[1+\beta\,(T_c-25\,{}^{\circ}\text{C})\bigr],\qquad T_c = T_a + \frac{\text{NOCT}-20\,{}^{\circ}\text{C}}{800\ \text{W m}^{-2}}\,G \]

the electrical power of a module equals its reference efficiency times its area times the irradiance in the module plane, reduced by the temperature coefficient times the amount by which the cell temperature exceeds 25 °C; the cell temperature is the air temperature plus a rise proportional to irradiance, scaled by the nominal operating cell temperature.

\(\eta_{\text{ref}}\)module efficiency at STC–0.20–0.23
\(A\)module aream²≈ 2
\(G\)irradiance in the plane of the moduleW m⁻²0–1100
\(\beta\)temperature coefficient of powerK⁻¹−0.003 to −0.0045 for c-Si
\(T_c, T_a\)cell and air temperature°C–
NOCTnominal operating cell temperature (at 800 W m⁻², 20 °C air, 1 m s⁻¹ wind)°C≈ 42–47
Follow the reasoning
  1. Without temperature effects, electrical power is efficiency × incident power: \(\eta A G\).
  2. The open-circuit voltage of a silicon cell falls as the cell warms (more intrinsic carriers, more recombination), so efficiency falls roughly linearly with temperature — the factor \(1+\beta(T_c-25)\) with \(\beta<0\) (Skoplaki & Palyvos, 2009).
  3. Absorbed sunlight that is not converted heats the module until losses by convection and radiation balance it; to first order the temperature rise above air is proportional to \(G\). The manufacturer's NOCT measures that rise under standard conditions (25 K rise at 800 W m⁻² for NOCT = 45 °C), which gives the linear model.
Assumptions and validity

The NOCT model ignores wind and mounting: modules high above a crop or standing vertically in the wind are cooler than roof-mounted ones, and transpiring crops cool the air around them — an advantage of agrivoltaics that Adeh et al. (2019) highlighted. Spectral and angle-of-incidence effects, soiling and inverter losses are lumped into a performance ratio in yield estimates.

(–) × m² × W m⁻² × [1 + K⁻¹ × K] = W ✓; \(T_c\): °C + (K)/(W m⁻²) × W m⁻² = °C ✓
Worked example 1A hot module and a Swedish module

A 1.95 m² module has an STC efficiency of 21 %, a temperature coefficient of −0.35 % K⁻¹ and a NOCT of 45 °C. Calculate its power at 900 W m⁻² when the air is 25 °C and when it is 15 °C. Compare with its STC rating.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. STC rating: \(0.21\times1.95\times1000 = 409.5\) W.
  2. Cell temperature at 25 °C air: \(T_c = 25+(45-20)/800\times900 = 25+28.1 = 53.1\) °C.
  3. Power: \(0.21\times1.95\times900\times[1-0.0035\times28.1] = 368.6\times0.902 = 332\) W.
  4. At 15 °C air: \(T_c = 43.1\) °C; \(P = 368.6\times[1-0.0035\times18.1] = 345\) W — 4 % more.
  5. Interpretation. Cool Nordic air is an advantage for PV, and a well-ventilated agrivoltaic structure over a transpiring crop adds to it.
≈ 332 W at 25 °C, ≈ 345 W at 15 °C (STC rating 409.5 W)
Bifacial modules: rear-side gain(11.5.2)
\[ G_{\text{eff}} = G_{\text{front}} + \varphi\,G_{\text{rear}},\qquad \text{BG}=\frac{\varphi\,G_{\text{rear}}}{G_{\text{front}}} \]

a bifacial module behaves as if it received the front-side irradiance plus the bifaciality factor times the rear-side irradiance; the bifacial gain is the extra fraction relative to the front side alone.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(G_{\text{front}}, G_{\text{rear}}\)irradiance on the front and rear facesW m⁻²–
\(\varphi\)bifaciality: rear efficiency ÷ front efficiency–0.7–0.9
BGbifacial gain–0.05–0.3 for tilted arrays; for vertical E–W modules both faces are "fronts"
Follow the reasoning
  1. Bifacial cells collect light through both faces; the rear side is somewhat less efficient, by the factor φ (Guerrero-Lemus et al., 2016).
  2. The rear side of a tilted module sees mainly light reflected from the ground (albedo 0.15–0.25 for vegetation, up to 0.8 for fresh snow) and diffuse sky light; high mounting and wide row spacing increase it.
  3. A vertical module facing east and west receives direct sun on its east face in the morning and on its west face in the afternoon: both faces work as fronts in turn, which produces two daily peaks.
Assumptions and validity

Rear irradiance is very non-uniform (support structures shade it), and in agrivoltaics the crop changes the ground albedo through the season. Riaz et al. (2021) showed that vertical east–west bifacial arrays can produce essentially the same energy and photosynthetically active radiation at the ground as conventional tilted monofacial arrays, provided the array density is halved or lower.

W m⁻² + (–) × W m⁻² = W m⁻² ✓; BG dimensionless ✓
Ground-coverage ratio and installed power per land area(11.5.3)
\[ \text{GCR} = \frac{W_{\text{mod}}}{d_{\text{row}}},\qquad p_{\text{land}} = \text{GCR}\;\eta_{\text{ref}}\;1000\ \text{W m}^{-2} \]

the ground-coverage ratio is the width of the module rows (their collector width) divided by the distance between rows; the peak power installed per square metre of land is the ground-coverage ratio times the module efficiency times the standard irradiance of 1000 watts per square metre.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(W_{\text{mod}}\)collector width of a row (slant width; for vertical rows, module height)m2–4

Follow the reasoning
  1. Per metre of row length, a row has \(W_{\text{mod}}\) m² of modules and occupies \(d_{\text{row}}\) m² of land: the ratio is GCR.
  2. At STC each m² of module delivers \(1000\,\eta_{\text{ref}}\) W; multiply by GCR to get watts-peak per m² of land.
  3. A higher GCR packs more power on the land but shades the ground (and the modules) more — the central trade-off of agrivoltaic design.
Assumptions and validity

For vertical modules the "collector width" is their height; GCR then describes module area per land area rather than covered ground. Energy per hectare also depends on how much each module produces, which falls with mutual shading at high GCR.

Worked example 2How much power per hectare?

Compare (a) a conventional solar park with GCR 0.45, (b) an elevated system with 3.4 m collector width and 9 m pitch, and (c) a vertical system with 2 m high module rows 10 m apart, all with 21 % modules.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(0.45\times0.21\times1000 = 94.5\) Wp m⁻² = 0.95 MWp ha⁻¹.
  2. (b) GCR \(= 3.4/9 = 0.378\) → 79 Wp m⁻² = 0.79 MWp ha⁻¹.
  3. (c) GCR \(= 2/10 = 0.20\) → 42 Wp m⁻² = 0.42 MWp ha⁻¹.
  4. Interpretation. The vertical system installs less than half the power of a solar park per hectare, but its modules see little mutual shading and its rear faces work too; per module it can produce as much energy as a tilted array (Riaz et al., 2021), while most of the land remains farmed.

Shading geometry and light at the crop

The shadow of a module row moves across the field as the sun moves. For long rows, only the component of the shadow perpendicular to the rows matters for the crop between them. Its length follows from the sun's elevation and azimuth (computed from latitude, date and time as in Lesson 4.2):

Shadow length across the rows(11.5.4)
\[ s_\perp = \frac{H\,\bigl|\sin(\psi_s-\psi_r)\bigr|}{\tan\alpha_s} \]

the length of a row's shadow measured perpendicular to the row equals the height of the top of the row times the absolute sine of the angle between the sun's azimuth and the row direction, divided by the tangent of the sun's elevation.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(s_\perp\)horizontal shadow extent perpendicular to the rowsm
\(H\)height of the top edge of the module row above the groundm
\(\alpha_s\)solar elevation angle°
\(\psi_s\)solar azimuth, clockwise from north°
\(\psi_r\)direction of the rows (0° for north–south rows, 90° for east–west rows)°
Follow the derivation
  1. A point at height \(H\) casts a shadow at horizontal distance \(H/\tan\alpha_s\) from its foot, in the direction opposite to the sun.
  2. Decompose this horizontal shadow vector into components along and across the row. The angle between the shadow direction and the row is \(\psi_s-\psi_r\) (plus 180°, which does not change the absolute sine).
  3. The cross-row component is \(H|\sin(\psi_s-\psi_r)|/\tan\alpha_s\). The along-row component only moves the shadow along the (infinitely long) row and does not change which strip of crop is shaded. ✓
  4. For north–south rows at solar noon (\(\psi_s = 180^{\circ}\)), \(\sin 180^{\circ} = 0\): the shadow falls along the row, and the crop between vertical rows is unshaded at midday.
Assumptions and validity

Flat ground, long straight rows and direct light only. Diffuse light — a large share of the total under Swedish skies — is reduced everywhere by the part of the sky hidden by the rows (the sky view factor), which Figure 2 includes. Zainali et al. (2023) give direct and diffuse shading factors for the main agrivoltaic layouts.

m × (–)/(–) = m ✓
Worked example 3Shadows of a vertical row near Västerås

A vertical bifacial row whose top edge is 2.8 m high runs north–south at 59.6° N. On 21 June the sun stands at 42.2° elevation and 118.9° azimuth at 09:00 solar time, and at 53.8° and 180° at noon. At the March equinox at 09:00 it stands at 20.7° and 130.9°. How far across the inter-row space does the shadow reach in each case?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. 21 June, 09:00: \(s_\perp = 2.8\times|\sin118.9^{\circ}|/\tan42.2^{\circ} = 2.8\times0.875/0.907 = 2.71\) m.
  2. 21 June, noon: \(\sin180^{\circ} = 0\) → the shadow lies along the row: 0 m across.
  3. Equinox, 09:00: \(s_\perp = 2.8\times|\sin130.9^{\circ}|/\tan20.7^{\circ} = 2.8\times0.756/0.378 = 5.6\) m.
  4. Consequence. With 10 m between rows, a strip up to 2.7 m wide next to each row is shaded on summer mornings and evenings, but the whole inter-row space is sunlit around midday — when irradiance is highest and photosynthesis most likely to be light-saturated. In spring and autumn the low sun makes longer shadows; at these latitudes the choice of row distance is critical (Campana et al., 2021).
≈ 2.7 m (June, 09:00), 0 m (June, noon), ≈ 5.6 m (equinox, 09:00)

Explore Figure 2 with a Swedish summer day: vertical rows 10 m apart leave the central two-thirds of the inter-row with almost full light, and give up most in a narrow band beside each row. Narrow the pitch to 5 m and the whole strip loses much more light — in line with the model study of Campana et al. (2021), in which crop yields of oats and potatoes fell by about 50 % as row distance decreased from 20 m to 5 m. Switch to the elevated system and the shade is spread more evenly but reaches the whole field; this is why the Heggelbach system was rotated about 30° away from south to distribute radiation evenly at ground level (Trommsdorff et al., 2021).

Light saturation: why crops can share the sun

If yield were proportional to light, every percent of light taken by the modules would cost a percent of yield. It is not. Leaf photosynthesis increases steeply with light at low irradiance but levels off towards a maximum at high irradiance — the light-response curve of Lesson 2.1, often described by a non-rectangular hyperbola:

Light-response of leaf photosynthesis (non-rectangular hyperbola)(11.5.5)
\[ A_n(I)=\frac{\phi I + A_{\max}-\sqrt{(\phi I + A_{\max})^2-4\theta\,\phi I A_{\max}}}{2\theta}-R_d,\qquad \text{daily }A=\int_{\text{day}} A_n\bigl((1-f_s)\,I(t)\bigr)\,dt \]

net leaf photosynthesis is the smaller root of a quadratic that blends the initial light-limited slope and the light-saturated maximum, minus dark respiration; the daily total is the integral over the day of the photosynthesis at the shaded light level, where f_s is the fraction of light removed by the modules.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(I\)PPFD at the leafµmol m⁻² s⁻¹0–2000
\(\phi\)quantum yield (initial slope)mol CO₂ (mol photons)⁻¹≈ 0.05
\(A_{\max}\)light-saturated gross photosynthesisµmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹15 (shade-tolerant) – 45 (C4)
\(\theta\)curvature (0 = rectangular hyperbola, 1 = sharp corner)–0.7
\(R_d\)dark respirationµmol CO₂ m⁻² s⁻¹≈ 1–2
\(f_s\)fraction of light removed by shading–0.2–0.4
Follow the reasoning
  1. At low light, photosynthesis is limited by photon supply: \(A\approx\phi I\).
  2. At high light, it is limited by carbon fixation capacity: \(A\to A_{\max}\).
  3. The non-rectangular hyperbola is the smaller solution of \(\theta A^2-(\phi I+A_{\max})A+\phi IA_{\max}=0\), which joins the two limits with a curvature θ.
  4. Because the curve is concave, removing a fraction \(f_s\) of the light at high irradiance reduces \(A\) by much less than \(f_s\): on a clear summer day at 59.6° N, 30 % shading reduces leaf-level gross photosynthesis by only about 12 % with \(A_{\max}=30\) (see Figure 3).
  5. On an overcast day the leaf works on the steep, almost linear part of the curve, and shading costs nearly proportionally.
Assumptions and validity

This is a leaf-level model. In a dense canopy many leaves are shaded by other leaves and work on the linear part of the curve, so canopy photosynthesis saturates less than a single leaf; real yield losses therefore lie between the leaf-level estimate and a proportional loss. Measured crop responses in the field are the final test (Section 11.5.7).

(mol mol⁻¹ × µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ + µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) = µmol m⁻² s⁻¹; θ dimensionless ✓

Microclimate under the modules

Modules change more than light. In Montpellier, Marrou et al. (2013) measured air temperature and humidity under two densities of agrivoltaic panels and found them similar to those in full sun, while mean daily soil temperature was significantly lower in the shade; crop temperature and crop growth rate of lettuce, cucumber and durum wheat were not significantly changed, and they concluded that little adaptation of cropping practice would be needed beyond managing the light reduction. At Heggelbach, solar irradiation under the system was about 30 % lower than on the reference field, soil temperature was lower in spring and summer, air temperature was identical, and in the hot, dry summer of 2018 soil moisture was higher under the modules — which helped wheat, potatoes and celeriac to out-yield the reference plots that year (Fraunhofer ISE, 2019). In drylands the benefits can be larger still: Barron-Gafford et al. (2019) reported mutual benefits for crops, water use and panel efficiency in Arizona. In a humid, cool climate such as Sweden's, light is more often limiting, and the benefits of shade appear mainly in dry years.

The land equivalent ratio

How can we judge whether combining two products on one field is better than producing them separately? Intercropping research answered this question decades ago with the land equivalent ratio (Mead & Willey, 1980), which Dupraz et al. (2011) transferred to agrivoltaics:

Land equivalent ratio of an agrivoltaic system(11.5.6)
\[ \text{LER}=\frac{Y_{\text{crop,AV}}}{Y_{\text{crop,ref}}}+\frac{E_{\text{AV}}}{E_{\text{PV,ref}}} \]

the land equivalent ratio is the crop yield under the agrivoltaic system relative to the yield of the same crop grown alone, plus the electricity produced by the agrivoltaic system relative to the electricity a conventional solar park would produce on the same area.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(Y_{\text{crop,AV}}\), \(Y_{\text{crop,ref}}\)crop yield in the agrivoltaic system and in a reference field without modulest ha⁻¹
\(E_{\text{AV}}\), \(E_{\text{PV,ref}}\)electricity from the agrivoltaic system and from an optimised ground-mounted PV plant on the same areaMWh ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
LERland equivalent ratio; > 1 means the combination uses land more efficiently than separation–
Follow the derivation
  1. Suppose one hectare of agrivoltaics produces \(Y_{\text{AV}}\) of crop and \(E_{\text{AV}}\) of electricity.
  2. To produce the same amounts separately we would need \(Y_{\text{AV}}/Y_{\text{ref}}\) ha of cropland plus \(E_{\text{AV}}/E_{\text{ref}}\) ha of solar park.
  3. The total area needed for separate production is therefore the LER (in hectares per hectare of agrivoltaics). If LER = 1.4, separate systems would need 40 % more land to produce the same outputs. ✓
Assumptions and validity

The LER adds physical ratios, not values: a 10 % loss of a high-value crop may cost more than the electricity gained. The result depends strongly on the reference: a well-designed solar park, and the crop grown in the same year and field. It does not count the land lost under supports or the quality of the crop (DIN SPEC 91434 and the French rules address these separately), and a single year can mislead — the Heggelbach LER differed markedly between 2017 and 2018.

(t ha⁻¹)/(t ha⁻¹) + (MWh ha⁻¹)/(MWh ha⁻¹) = dimensionless ✓
Worked example 4Heggelbach in the drought year 2018

In 2018 the potatoes under the Heggelbach agrivoltaic system yielded 103 % of the reference field, and the system produced 83 % of the electricity of a conventional ground-mounted PV plant on the same area (Fraunhofer ISE, 2020). Calculate the LER and interpret it. How much land would separate production of the same potatoes and electricity require per hectare of agrivoltaics?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. LER (Eq. 11.5.6): \(1.03+0.83 = 1.86\).
  2. Land for separate production: 1.03 ha of potatoes + 0.83 ha of solar park = 1.86 ha — 86 % more land than the agrivoltaic hectare.
  3. Context. 2018 was exceptionally hot and dry; shade and moister soil helped the crop. In 2017, with normal weather, potatoes, wheat and celeriac under the modules yielded 18–19 % less than the reference and clover grass 5.3 % less, and the land equivalent ratio was about 1.6 (Trommsdorff et al., 2021, report 1.56–1.70).
  4. Lesson. Agrivoltaics can raise land productivity substantially, but single-year numbers vary with weather — robust conclusions need several years and several crops.
LER = 1.86 (≈ 1.9 ha of separate land per ha of agrivoltaics)
Worked example 5A vertical system on Swedish ley (illustrative)

Suppose a vertical east–west array with rows 10 m apart leaves 0.8 m under each row unfarmed, and the ley between the rows yields 97 % of the open-field reference. Its modules produce about as much energy per module as a conventional park, but it has only half the module area per hectare. Estimate the LER, including the unfarmed strips.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Farmed fraction: \(1-0.8/10 = 0.92\) of the land.
  2. Relative crop yield per hectare of system: \(0.92\times0.97 = 0.89\).

Field results: Heggelbach and Kärrbo prästgård

Heggelbach (Germany, near Lake Constance). In the APV-RESOLA project led by Fraunhofer ISE, 720 bifacial modules with a total of 194 kWp were mounted with a clearance of 5 m over one third of a hectare of a Demeter farm in 2016 (Fraunhofer ISE, 2020; Trommsdorff et al., 2021). The design allows large machinery to work underneath; the rows face south-west to spread the shade. Winter wheat, potatoes, celeriac and clover grass were grown in rotation under the modules and on a reference field.

Kärrbo prästgård (Sweden, near Västerås). Sweden's first agrivoltaic system was installed in the early summer of 2021 on a farm outside Västerås, in a research project led by Mälardalen University with the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences (SLU), the Swedish Energy Agency and industry partners. It consists of 60 vertical bifacial modules in north–south rows (facing east and west), compared with a conventional ground-mounted bifacial reference array and an open reference field. The first two seasons were ley (grass for silage): despite a reduction of photosynthetically active radiation of about 25 % on average, the ley yield showed no statistically significant decline, the land equivalent ratio was 1.27 in 2021 and 1.39 in 2022, and the economic analysis indicated that the agrivoltaic system could produce about 30 times more net present value than a conventional Swedish crop rotation (Campana et al., 2024). In the 2023 season, barley grown between the vertical rows yielded on average more than the open-field control, although the difference was not statistically significant (Ma Lu et al., 2024).

Standards, policy and acceptance

Because "agrivoltaics" can be used as a label for solar parks with a little grass underneath, several countries have defined what counts. In Germany, DIN SPEC 91434 (2021) distinguishes category I systems, mounted with a clearance of at least 2.1 m so that farming continues underneath, and category II systems at ground level with farming between the rows. The land lost to agriculture through the installation may be at most 10 % of the project area in category I and 15 % in category II, and the agricultural yield after installation should be at least 66 % of the reference yield. France's 2023 law on accelerating renewable energy production (loi n° 2023-175 du 10 mars 2023) defined agrivoltaics as installations that provide a service to the farm — improving agronomic potential, adapting to climate change, protecting against hazards or improving animal welfare — while agriculture remains the main activity; the implementing decree of April 2024 (décret n° 2024-318) limits the yield loss compared with a control area to at most 10 % and the panel coverage of large installations (above 10 MWp) to 40 %. Japan has supported "solar sharing" on farmland since 2013 (Fraunhofer ISE, 2020).

In Sweden, the protection of arable land in the Environmental Code (3 kap. 4 §) and local planning decisions frame what is possible; a design that demonstrably keeps the land in productive agricultural use is central to the argument for agrivoltaics.

Worked example 6Would the Swedish design pass the German and French tests?

Take the vertical system of Worked example 5 (0.8 m unfarmed under each row, rows 10 m apart; ley yield 97 % between the rows). Check it against the DIN SPEC 91434 criteria for category II and against the French 10 % yield-loss limit.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Land loss: \(0.8/10 = 8\) % of the area — below the 15 % limit for category II. ✓
  2. Yield per hectare of project area: \(0.92\times0.97 = 0.89\), i.e. 89 % of the reference — above the 66 % minimum. ✓
  3. French criterion: the yield loss relative to a control area is \(1-0.89 = 11\) % if the unfarmed strips are counted, or 3 % if only the cultivated area is compared — so the answer depends on how the rule is applied, which is why definitions matter.
Passes DIN SPEC 91434 (8 % land loss, 89 % yield); borderline under a strict 10 % yield-loss rule

Acceptance. Large solar parks increasingly meet local resistance over landscape, farmland and fairness. In interviews with solar-industry professionals in the United States, Pascaris et al. (2021) found that the potential of agrivoltaics to retain agricultural interests — and thereby increase local support — was seen as its most significant opportunity, and that its growth depends on community acceptance and supportive local regulation. Open questions remain: who benefits (landowner, tenant farmer, developer, municipality)? Will the farming really continue for 30 years, or will it become a fig leaf? How does the landscape change? How do modules affect biodiversity, drainage and machinery work? Agrivoltaics is a technical solution to a land-use conflict, and like all such solutions it works only if the institutions around it are trusted.

Common misconception

"Agrivoltaics always increases crop yields because plants get too much sun." Only in hot, dry conditions or for shade-tolerant crops does shade regularly raise yields; in normal years and for light-demanding crops yields usually fall somewhat (Heggelbach 2017). The case for agrivoltaics rests on the combined output per hectare — the LER — not on higher crop yields alone.

Frontier (2024–2026)

Research is moving towards crop-aware control of trackers that give the crop light at sensitive stages, semi-transparent and spectrally selective modules for horticulture, integration with rainwater harvesting and irrigation, and integrated crop–PV models calibrated at northern latitudes (Campana et al., 2024; Zainali et al., 2023). In Sweden, the vertical systems studied at Kärrbo are being tested with different crops in the rotation, including cereals.

Key terms

Agrivoltaics
Simultaneous use of the same land for agricultural production and photovoltaic electricity, with farming remaining the primary use.
Elevated agrivoltaic system
Modules mounted high enough above the crop (≥ 2.1 m in DIN SPEC 91434 category I) for farming to continue underneath.
Vertical bifacial array
Rows of bifacial modules mounted vertically, usually facing east and west, with crops grown between the rows.
Bifaciality
The ratio of the rear-side to the front-side efficiency of a bifacial module.
Temperature coefficient
The relative change of PV power per kelvin of cell temperature, negative for crystalline silicon.
NOCT
Nominal operating cell temperature, used to estimate cell temperature from air temperature and irradiance.
Ground-coverage ratio (GCR)
The collector width of a module row divided by the row pitch.
Shadow length across rows
The cross-row component of a row's shadow, H|sin(ψₛ − ψᵣ)|/tan αₛ.
Light saturation
The irradiance above which leaf photosynthesis increases little with further light.
Land equivalent ratio (LER)
The sum of the relative yields of each product of a combined system; the land needed for separate production per unit of combined land.
DIN SPEC 91434
A German specification (2021) defining requirements for agrivoltaic systems, including limits on land loss and a minimum agricultural yield.
Brukningsvärd jordbruksmark
Arable land suitable for cultivation, protected by the Swedish Environmental Code (3 kap. 4 §) against development unless essential public interests require it.
