Plant growth analysis and crop models

A lettuce seedling of a few hundredths of a gram of dry matter becomes a 150 g head in about four weeks — more than a hundred-fold increase in fresh mass. How fast a plant grows, and why, is the question that ties this module together: photosynthesis (Lesson 2.1), water (Lesson 2.2) and nutrients (Lesson 2.3) all end up as grams of biomass. This lesson gives you the quantitative tools of growth analysis — relative growth rate and its components, leaf area index and canopy light interception, radiation-use efficiency and Monteith's equation — and turns them into a simple mechanistic crop model. It ends with the measurements you will make on your own plants in the course project, so that you can analyse their growth with exactly these equations.

What you will be able to do

  • Calculate relative growth rate (RGR) from harvest data without bias, and decompose it into net assimilation rate, leaf area ratio, specific leaf area and leaf mass ratio (RGR = NAR × LAR = NAR × SLA × LMR) (LO3).
  • Explain leaf area index and canopy light interception with the Monsi–Saeki (Beer–Lambert) law, and estimate extinction coefficients and the leaf area needed for a target interception (LO2, LO3).
  • Apply Monteith's radiation-use-efficiency framework and the harvest index to estimate biomass and yield, and convert between radiation-, light- and energy-use efficiencies (LO3, LO7).
  • Describe exponential, logistic and expolinear growth, and derive the expolinear equation from canopy light interception (LO3, LO5).
  • Explain and run a simple mechanistic lettuce model, and use it to evaluate light-use efficiency and electricity use per kilogram in vertical farms (LO3, LO5, LO7).
  • Design destructive and image-based growth measurements for your own plants and compute growth indices with their uncertainty (LO4, LO5).

Why analyse growth — and what your project will measure

A grower wants to know when the crop will be ready and how much it will weigh. An engineer wants to know how many grams of lettuce each kilowatt-hour of lighting buys. A physiologist wants to know why one cultivar, one spacing or one light recipe outgrows another. All three questions have the same starting point: a quantitative description of growth. Plant growth analysis is the classical toolbox for it. It needs only a balance, a ruler or a scanner and a calculator, it has been refined for a century, and it remains the backbone of modern crop models and digital twins (Lesson 10.5).

The toolbox works at two levels that we will connect. At the level of the individual plant, growth analysis asks how fast each gram of plant produces new grams (relative growth rate) and splits that rate into a physiological component (how productive each square metre of leaf is) and a morphological one (how much leaf area the plant builds per gram). At the level of the crop, it asks how much light the canopy intercepts and how efficiently it converts that light into dry matter and harvestable yield — the framework of John Monteith (1977) summarised in Figure 1.

Relative growth rate: the compound interest of plants

In 1919 Vernon Blackman pointed out that a plant grows like money in a savings account: new leaves make new sugars that make more new leaves, so the growth rate is proportional to the size already reached (Blackman, 1919). The natural measure of growth is therefore not grams per day but grams per gram per day — the relative growth rate (RGR). A seedling that doubles every four days and a large plant that doubles every four days have the same RGR, although the large plant adds far more grams. RGR is to plant growth what the interest rate is to capital: it makes plants of different sizes, species and treatments comparable.

Relative growth rate and doubling time(2.4.1)
\[ \text{RGR} = \frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\mathrm d\ln W}{\mathrm dt}, \qquad \overline{\text{RGR}} = \frac{\overline{\ln W_2} - \overline{\ln W_1}}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{\text{RGR}} \]

relative growth rate is the growth rate per unit of existing biomass, which equals the rate of change of the natural logarithm of biomass. Between two harvests the mean RGR is the difference of the mean natural logarithms of plant mass divided by the time between harvests. The doubling time is ln 2 divided by the RGR.

Follow the derivation
  1. Define RGR as \((1/W)\,\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt\). By the chain rule \(\mathrm d\ln W/\mathrm dt = (1/W)\,\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt\), so RGR is simply the slope of \(\ln W\) against time.
  2. Integrate between two harvests: \(\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\text{RGR}\,\mathrm dt = \ln W_2 - \ln W_1\). Dividing by \(t_2-t_1\) gives the mean RGR over the interval — exact whatever happens in between.
  3. If RGR is constant, \(W = W_1e^{\text{RGR}(t-t_1)}\) (exponential growth). The mass doubles when \(e^{\text{RGR}\,t_d} = 2\), i.e. \(t_d = \ln2/\text{RGR} = 0.693/\text{RGR}\).
  4. With destructive harvests you weigh different plants at each time. Hoffmann and Poorter (2002) showed that inserting the arithmetic mean masses, \(\ln\bar W_2 - \ln\bar W_1\), gives a biased estimate whenever the spread of \(\ln W\) changes between harvests; the unbiased estimator uses the mean of the logarithms, \(\overline{\ln W}\).
Assumptions and validity

RGR is a mean over the interval; if plants are harvested far apart it hides changes within it. RGR normally declines as plants grow (ontogenetic drift): self-shading lowers the productivity per unit leaf area, and a growing share of biomass is support and storage tissue rather than leaf. Compare RGRs only over similar size ranges or growth stages, and always say whether they are based on dry mass, fresh mass or leaf area — they differ.

\((\ln \text{g} - \ln \text{g})/\text{d} = \ln(\text{g}/\text{g})/\text{d} = \text{d}^{-1}\) ✓ — the logarithm of a ratio of masses is dimensionless
Common misconception

"The bigger plant grows faster." In grams per day it usually does — its absolute growth rate is higher. But its relative growth rate is usually lower. A lettuce seedling with an RGR of 0.3 d⁻¹ adds 30 % of its mass every day; a nearly mature head may add only 5 %. When you compare treatments in your project, compare RGR over the same period (or plants of the same size), not final masses of plants that started at different sizes.

Dissecting RGR: NAR, LAR, SLA and LMR

Why does one plant have a higher RGR than another? Classical growth analysis answers by splitting RGR into factors that each have a clear meaning. New dry matter is made by leaves, so it is natural to ask two separate questions: how much dry matter does each square metre of leaf produce per day? — the net assimilation rate (NAR, also called unit leaf rate) — and how much leaf area does the plant have per gram of its own mass? — the leaf area ratio (LAR). The leaf area ratio can itself be split into how thin the leaves are (specific leaf area, SLA: leaf area per gram of leaf) and how much of the plant's mass is leaf (leaf mass ratio, LMR).

Components of relative growth rate(2.4.2)
\[ \underbrace{\frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}}_{\text{RGR}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{A}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}}_{\text{NAR}}\times\underbrace{\frac{A}{W}}_{\text{LAR}}, \qquad \underbrace{\frac{A}{W}}_{\text{LAR}} = \underbrace{\frac{A}{W_L}}_{\text{SLA}}\times\underbrace{\frac{W_L}{W}}_{\text{LMR}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{RGR} = \text{NAR}\times\text{SLA}\times\text{LMR} \]

relative growth rate equals the net assimilation rate — dry-matter gain per unit leaf area and time — multiplied by the leaf area ratio — leaf area per unit plant mass. The leaf area ratio is the specific leaf area — leaf area per unit leaf mass — multiplied by the leaf mass ratio — the fraction of plant mass that is leaf.

Follow the derivation
  1. Multiply and divide RGR by the leaf area \(A\): \(\frac{1}{W}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{1}{A}\frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt}\cdot\frac{A}{W}\). This is an identity — nothing is assumed — but it separates how productive the leaf area is from how much leaf area there is.
  2. Multiply and divide LAR by leaf mass \(W_L\): \(\frac{A}{W} = \frac{A}{W_L}\cdot\frac{W_L}{W}\).
  3. Units: NAR (g m⁻² d⁻¹) × LAR (m² g⁻¹) = d⁻¹ ✓. Example: NAR = 4 g m⁻² d⁻¹, SLA = 0.0365 m² g⁻¹ (365 cm² g⁻¹), LMR = 0.85 → RGR = 4 × 0.0365 × 0.85 = 0.124 d⁻¹.
  4. The identity holds for instantaneous values. For means over an interval, \(\overline{\text{RGR}} = \overline{\text{NAR}}\times\overline{\text{LAR}}\) holds only approximately (exactly if LAR stays constant), which is why the estimate of NAR between harvests needs care (Eq. 2.4.3).
Assumptions and validity

Leaf area is treated as the only photosynthetic surface (stems and petioles of lettuce are negligible). Some authors use leaf mass instead of area (unit leaf rate on a mass basis). The decomposition is descriptive: a change in NAR can be caused by photosynthesis, respiration, self-shading or even by allocation to non-leaf organs that are cheap or expensive to build.

(g m⁻² d⁻¹) × (m² g⁻¹) × (g g⁻¹) = d⁻¹ ✓

NAR cannot be measured directly from two harvests, because leaf area changes during the interval. The standard estimate, due to Williams (1946), assumes that plant mass and leaf area are linearly related over the interval:

Mean net assimilation rate between two harvests(2.4.3)
\[ \overline{\text{NAR}} = \frac{W_2 - W_1}{t_2 - t_1}\cdot\frac{\ln A_2 - \ln A_1}{A_2 - A_1} \]

the mean net assimilation rate is the absolute gain in dry mass per day, divided by a mean leaf area — the logarithmic mean of the leaf areas at the two harvests, (A₂ − A₁)/(ln A₂ − ln A₁).

Follow the derivation
  1. By definition \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = \text{NAR}\cdot A\). Integrating over the interval with a constant mean NAR: \(W_2 - W_1 = \overline{\text{NAR}}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}A\,\mathrm dt\).
  2. We need the time integral of leaf area, but only know its end values. Assume \(W\) and \(A\) are linearly related (\(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dA\) constant over the interval) and that leaf area grows exponentially between harvests, \(A = A_1e^{r(t-t_1)}\) with \(r = (\ln A_2-\ln A_1)/(t_2-t_1)\).
  3. Then \(\int A\,\mathrm dt = (A_2 - A_1)/r = (A_2-A_1)(t_2-t_1)/(\ln A_2 - \ln A_1)\): the logarithmic mean leaf area times the interval.

Assumptions and validity

Valid when mass and leaf area are roughly proportional over the interval (true for leafy crops in the vegetative phase with short intervals). If the plant starts storing reserves or making fruits, W and A diverge and the formula becomes biased; harvest more often. If \(A_2 = A_1\) the formula reduces to \((W_2-W_1)/[(t_2-t_1)A]\).

Leaf area index and light interception by the canopy

Once plants stand close together, the productivity of a leaf depends on its neighbours. D. J. Watson (1947) introduced the quantity that describes a crop's leafiness: the leaf area index (LAI), the one-sided leaf area per unit ground area (m² m⁻²). An LAI of 3 means that, on average, three layers of leaves lie above each point of the ground. In a vertical farm with isolated plants on a tray, LAI is simply the plant density times the leaf area per plant: 56 plants m⁻² × 0.05 m² = 2.8.

How much light does a canopy with a given LAI intercept? In 1953 Masami Monsi and Toshiro Saeki treated a plant stand like a turbid medium and applied the Beer–Lambert law of light absorption (Monsi & Saeki, 2005, English translation) — the same law that describes light absorption by a gas in an NDIR CO₂ sensor (Lesson 9.1) and by algae in a photobioreactor.

Light attenuation in a canopy (Monsi–Saeki, Beer–Lambert)(2.4.4)
\[ I(L) = I_0\,e^{-k\,L}, \qquad f_{\text{int}} = 1 - e^{-k\,\text{LAI}}, \qquad \text{LAI}_{f} = \frac{-\ln(1-f)}{k}, \qquad k = \frac{G(\theta)}{\cos\theta} \]

the photon flux density at a depth where L units of leaf area lie above falls exponentially from its value I₀ at the top of the canopy, with the extinction coefficient k as the rate constant. The fraction of light intercepted by the whole canopy is one minus e to the power of −k times the leaf area index. Inverting it gives the LAI needed to intercept a chosen fraction f. The extinction coefficient equals the average projection of unit leaf area in the direction of the light, G, divided by the cosine of the zenith angle of the light.

Follow the derivation
  1. Slice the canopy into thin layers, each with leaf area \(\mathrm dL\) per unit ground. A layer casts a shadow of area \(G\,\mathrm dL\) on a plane perpendicular to the beam; on the horizontal ground this shadow is stretched by \(1/\cos\theta\). The fraction of the light reaching the layer that it intercepts is therefore \(k\,\mathrm dL\) with \(k = G/\cos\theta\).
  2. Hence \(\mathrm dI = -k\,I\,\mathrm dL\). Separate the variables and integrate from the top (\(L = 0\), \(I = I_0\)): \(\ln(I/I_0) = -kL\), i.e. \(I = I_0e^{-kL}\).
  3. At the bottom of the canopy (\(L = \text{LAI}\)) the transmitted fraction is \(e^{-k\,\text{LAI}}\); the intercepted fraction is its complement, \(f_{\text{int}} = 1-e^{-k\,\text{LAI}}\).
  4. Solve for LAI: \(\text{LAI}_f = -\ln(1-f)/k\). For 90 % interception, \(\ln 10/k = 2.30/k\); with k = 0.8 an LAI of 2.9 is needed, with k = 0.4 as much as 5.8.
  5. A probabilistic view gives the same result: if leaves are randomly scattered, the number of leaves a ray meets follows a Poisson distribution with mean \(kL\), and the probability of meeting none (a gap) is \(e^{-kL}\).
  6. Leaf angle: for horizontal leaves \(G = \cos\theta\) and \(k = 1\); for a spherical distribution \(G = 0.5\) and \(k = 0.5/\cos\theta\); for vertical leaves and overhead light \(k \to 0\). Campbell (1986) gave a general formula for ellipsoidal leaf-angle distributions, used to compute Table 1.

Two consequences matter in controlled environments. First, flat-leaved crops such as lettuce under overhead LEDs have high extinction coefficients (the lettuce model of van Henten uses k = 0.9), so they close their canopy at a modest LAI of about 3. Second, light falling between young plants is wasted: just after transplanting at 56 plants m⁻², a lettuce seedling with 19 cm² of leaf gives an LAI of 0.1, and the canopy intercepts only about 8 % of the photons (Worked example 2). The lamps light the tray, not the crop. This is why spacing strategies matter so much for light-use efficiency (Section 2.4.8).

Radiation-use efficiency and Monteith's equation

In 1977 John Monteith analysed the growth of barley, potatoes, sugar beet and apples in Britain and found a strikingly simple pattern: total dry matter was strongly correlated with the amount of solar radiation the crops had intercepted, at about 1.4 g of carbohydrate per megajoule of solar energy — an efficiency of about 2.4 % (Monteith, 1977). He also noted that the major arable crops of Britain intercepted only about 40 % of the annual solar radiation, because for much of the year the fields were bare or the canopies were not closed. Crop growth could thus be analysed as the product of two factors — how much light is intercepted and how efficiently it is used — an idea that underlies nearly all modern crop models (Sinclair & Muchow, 1999).

Monteith's equation and the harvest index(2.4.5)
\[ W = \varepsilon\sum_{\text{days}} f_{\text{int},d}\;S_d, \qquad Y = \text{HI}\times W \]

the dry matter a crop accumulates equals its light-use (or radiation-use) efficiency multiplied by the sum over all days of the fraction of light intercepted times the incident light. The harvestable yield is the harvest index times that dry matter.

Follow the derivation
  1. The canopy can only use light it intercepts: on day d it intercepts \(f_{\text{int},d}S_d\).
  2. Assume the dry matter produced per unit of intercepted light, ε, is approximately constant — Monteith's empirical finding for crops that are not short of water or nutrients. Then the daily growth is \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = \varepsilon f_{\text{int}}S\).
  3. Sum (integrate) over the season: \(W = \varepsilon\sum f_{\text{int},d}S_d\).
  4. Yield is the harvested part: \(Y = \text{HI}\cdot W\). The famous gains in cereal yield potential during the twentieth century came largely from raising HI (Hay, 1995); further gains must come from raising interception or ε.
  5. Why is ε roughly constant, although single leaves saturate with light? Because in a closed canopy most leaves are shaded and work on the linear, efficient part of their light response (Figure 5). ε does fall at very high PPFD, which is why photoperiod and PPFD matter in vertical farms (Section 2.4.8).
Assumptions and validity

ε is not a physical constant: it rises with CO₂, falls at high PPFD, under stress and at unfavourable temperatures, and it differs between C3 and C4 crops (Sinclair & Muchow, 1999). Always state the basis: total solar radiation or PAR; energy or photons; incident, intercepted or absorbed light; above-ground or total dry matter. Values on different bases can differ by a factor of two or more (Eq. 2.4.6).

From exponential to linear: growth curves with a mechanism

Plot the dry mass of a lettuce crop against time and you see an S-shaped or, if you harvest before maturity, a J-shaped curve that straightens into a line. Many empirical functions can describe such curves; the statistics of fitting them — least squares, the Gompertz and Richards functions, AIC and cross-validation — are the subject of Lesson 10.1. Here we ask a different question: which curve does the physiology predict?

Exponential and logistic growth(2.4.7)
\[ \frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = r\,W \;\Rightarrow\; W = W_0\,e^{r t}, \qquad\qquad \frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = r\,W\Bigl(1-\frac{W}{K}\Bigr) \;\Rightarrow\; W = \frac{K}{1+e^{-r\,(t-t_i)}} \]

if every gram of plant produces new mass at the same relative rate r, mass grows exponentially. If the relative growth rate declines in proportion to how close the mass is to a maximum K, growth follows the logistic curve, which is steepest at its inflection time ti, when W = K/2.

Follow the derivation
  1. Exponential. \(\mathrm dW/W = r\,\mathrm dt\) integrates to \(\ln W = \ln W_0 + rt\): a straight line on a log scale, the phase in which RGR is constant — young plants whose leaves do not yet shade each other.
  2. Logistic. Here \(\text{RGR} = r(1-W/K)\) falls linearly with mass. Separate: \(\dfrac{\mathrm dW}{W(1-W/K)} = r\,\mathrm dt\). With partial fractions, \(\dfrac{1}{W(1-W/K)} = \dfrac{1}{W} + \dfrac{1/K}{1-W/K}\), so \(\ln\dfrac{W}{K-W} = r(t-t_i)\), which rearranges to the formula above.
  3. The absolute growth rate \(rW(1-W/K)\) is largest at \(W = K/2\); there \(\mathrm dW/\mathrm dt = rK/4\).
  4. A useful test for your own data: plot RGR against W. The logistic model predicts a straight line falling from \(r\) at \(W \to 0\) to zero at \(W = K\) (Lesson 10.1, Eq. 10.1.5).
Assumptions and validity

Both are descriptive. The exponential model only fits the earliest phase. The logistic model is symmetric around its inflection, which crops rarely are, and its K is a statistical parameter, not a real "maximum" for a crop that is harvested long before it stops growing. Neither model says why RGR falls — for that we need the light-interception argument of Eq. 2.4.8.

Jan Goudriaan and John Monteith (1990) derived a curve from the mechanism instead. They assumed that when light is limiting, crop growth rate is proportional to intercepted radiation and therefore to an exponential function of leaf area, and they called the result the expolinear growth equation: growth is exponential while the canopy is open and becomes linear once it closes. They showed that the ratio of its two rate parameters is the product of the extinction coefficient, the specific leaf area and the leaf mass ratio, and that the time "lost" before the canopy closes depends on the logarithm of the fraction of light intercepted at the start (Goudriaan & Monteith, 1990). The derivation below uses exactly the ingredients of Sections 2.4.3–2.4.5.

Expolinear growth from canopy light interception(2.4.8)
\[ \frac{\mathrm dW}{\mathrm dt} = c_m\Bigl[1 - e^{-k\,s\,p_1\,W}\Bigr] \;\Rightarrow\; W(t) = \frac{c_m}{r_m}\ln\!\Bigl[1 + e^{\,r_m\,(t-t_b)}\Bigr], \qquad c_m = \varepsilon\,S, \qquad r_m = k\,s\,p_1\,c_m, \qquad t_b = -\frac{1}{r_m}\ln\!\bigl(e^{k s p_1 W_0} - 1\bigr) \]

if the crop grows at a rate equal to its maximum crop growth rate cm times the fraction of light it intercepts, and if its leaf area index is proportional to its dry mass, then mass follows the expolinear curve. The maximum crop growth rate is the light-use efficiency times the daily light; the maximum relative growth rate rm is the product of the extinction coefficient, specific leaf area, leaf mass ratio and cm; the "lost time" tb depends on how much light the crop intercepted at the start.

A simple mechanistic lettuce model

Empirical curves describe the growth you have measured; a mechanistic model predicts growth under conditions you have not yet tried — another light intensity, a longer photoperiod, CO₂ enrichment, a different plant density. That is what a grower or an engineer designing a vertical farm needs. Crop models range from very detailed (layered canopies, organ-level carbon allocation, as in the SUCROS family; Goudriaan & van Laar, 1994) to deliberately compact. A classic compact model for lettuce is that of van Henten, which tracks structural and non-structural dry matter driven by light, CO₂ and temperature and was built for optimal greenhouse climate control (van Henten & van Straten, 1994); it drives the lettuce digital-twin laboratory.

Here we assemble an even simpler model from the pieces of this module, so that every equation is one you have already met. It has a single state variable, the dry mass per plant W, and six processes (Figure 6): (1) leaf area follows from mass (Eq. 2.4.2); (2) the canopy intercepts light according to Beer–Lambert (Eq. 2.4.4); (3) intercepted light drives canopy photosynthesis with a light-response curve whose initial slope responds to CO₂ and temperature as in the Farquhar model of Lesson 2.1; (4) maintenance respiration consumes a fixed fraction of the mass per day, rising with temperature; (5) the remaining sugars are converted into new dry matter with a growth efficiency; (6) fresh mass follows from the dry-matter content.

  • Plant density and re-spacing raise early interception. Moving plants from a dense nursery to their final spacing — manually, with mobile gutters or with robots — keeps the canopy closed for more of the cycle.
  • Leaf expansion. Adding far-red light (52 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ on top of 218 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ red–blue) increased lettuce leaf area by 58–75 % and LUE on incident light by 46–77 %, but LUE on intercepted light by only 8–23 %: most of the gain came from faster leaf expansion and better light interception, not from more efficient photosynthesis (Jin et al., 2021).
  • Photoperiod. Spreading the same DLI over more hours lowers the PPFD and raises conversion efficiency. At a fixed DLI of 15.6 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, lower PPFD with a longer photoperiod produced more biomass (Kelly et al., 2020); continuous light at 16.9 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ raised fresh and dry mass by 6–23 %, light interception by 10 % in one cultivar and LUE by 7–11 % compared with an 18 h photoperiod, without tipburn or chlorosis (Núñez Ocaña et al., 2026).

Research frontier (2026)

Light-use efficiency is now being optimised dynamically rather than statically: light intensity, spectrum (including far-red) and photoperiod are changed during the cycle as the canopy develops, plants are re-spaced as they grow, and top-view cameras measure the interception achieved every day (Núñez Ocaña et al., 2026). Combined with growth models like the one above and with model predictive control (Lesson 10.4), such "light recipes" aim to close the gap between the average and the best light-use efficiencies of Table 2.

Measuring growth in your project

Growth analysis is only as good as the measurements behind it. You will use two complementary kinds.

Destructive harvests give the full truth for a few plants: fresh and dry mass of shoots and roots, leaf area, and hence every index of Eqs. 2.4.1–2.4.3. Good practice (see also Pérez-Harguindeguy et al., 2013, for standardised protocols):

  • Harvest at the same time of day (plant water content changes during the day) and several plants per treatment — four to six is a practical minimum for an RGR with a useful standard error (Eq. 2.4.10). Choose plants at random, not the nicest ones, and avoid the edges of the tray (Lesson 3.1).
  • Separate shoots and roots, rinse and gently blot the roots, and weigh fresh mass immediately.
  • Measure leaf area before drying — with a leaf-area meter, or by scanning the leaves flat on a flatbed scanner next to a scale and counting leaf pixels.
  • Dry the material in paper bags in a ventilated oven at 60–70 °C to constant mass (typically at least 72 h; Pérez-Harguindeguy et al., 2013), let it cool in a dry place and weigh on a balance with 1 mg resolution for seedlings.
  • Record everything per plant, not only means: the unbiased RGR needs the mean of the logarithms (Eq. 2.4.1).

Non-destructive imaging follows the same plants through time. A camera mounted at a fixed height above the tray photographs the plants every day; green plant pixels are separated from the background with a colour index such as the excess-green index ExG = 2G − R − B (Woebbecke et al., 1995) and a threshold, and the pixel count times the area per pixel gives the projected leaf area (PLA). Image-based growth analysis of rosette plants is well established in phenotyping (Leister et al., 1999; Walter et al., 2007), convolutional neural networks can estimate lettuce fresh mass, dry mass and leaf area from images (Zhang et al., 2020), and daily top-view images are now used to quantify the light interception of lettuce canopies in vertical-farm experiments (Núñez Ocaña et al., 2026). The plant-vision laboratory lets you segment canopy images yourself.

Projected leaf area has well-known limitations: it sees only the top layer, so it stops increasing in proportion to mass once leaves overlap, curl upwards or form a head; neighbouring plants merge in the image once the canopy closes; and the relation between PLA and mass differs between cultivars (Zhang et al. (2020) found better estimates for flat-type than for curled lettuce cultivars). The remedy is calibration against destructive harvests, and the natural form of the calibration is a power law.

Allometric calibration of images and the uncertainty of RGR(2.4.10)
\[ \text{FW} = a\,\text{PLA}^{\,b} \;\Leftrightarrow\; \ln\text{FW} = \ln a + b\ln\text{PLA} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{RGR}_{\text{FW}} = b\cdot\text{RGR}_{\text{PLA}}, \qquad \text{SE}(\overline{\text{RGR}}) = \frac{\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}}{t_2 - t_1} \]

fresh mass is modelled as a power function of projected leaf area, which becomes a straight line on log–log axes; its slope b converts a growth rate of projected area into a growth rate of mass. The standard error of a mean RGR from two destructive harvests is the square root of the sum of the variances of ln W divided by the sample sizes, divided by the time between the harvests.

Follow the derivation
  1. Many plant traits scale as power laws of each other (allometry). Taking logarithms linearises the power law, and the parameters follow from ordinary least squares on (ln PLA, ln FW) (Eq. 10.1.2).
  2. Differentiate \(\ln\text{FW} = \ln a + b\ln\text{PLA}\) with respect to time: \(\frac{\mathrm d\ln\text{FW}}{\mathrm dt} = b\,\frac{\mathrm d\ln\text{PLA}}{\mathrm dt}\). The intercept \(a\) drops out — RGR of mass is b times RGR of projected area.
  3. For destructive harvests, \(\overline{\text{RGR}} = (\overline{\ln W_2} - \overline{\ln W_1})/\Delta t\). The two means come from independent groups of plants, so the variance of their difference is the sum of the variances of the means, \(s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2\). Divide the square root by \(\Delta t\).
  4. A 95 % confidence interval is \(\overline{\text{RGR}}\pm t_{0.975,\nu}\,\text{SE}\), with ν ≈ n₁ + n₂ − 2 degrees of freedom (Lesson 3.2). Doubling the number of plants reduces the SE by a factor of √2; lengthening the interval reduces it proportionally — but hides changes of RGR within the interval.
Assumptions and validity

The calibration is valid only within the range of sizes, the cultivar and the camera set-up for which it was made; re-calibrate if leaves start to overlap or heads form. The SE formula assumes plants at the two harvests are independent random samples; with image data on the same plants, regress ln FW on time per plant and average the slopes instead.

Key terms

Relative growth rate (RGR)
Growth per unit of existing biomass, (1/W) dW/dt = d ln W/dt, in d⁻¹; estimated between harvests from the means of ln W.
Absolute (crop) growth rate
Increase in mass per unit time (g d⁻¹ per plant, or g m⁻² d⁻¹ per unit ground area for a crop).
Net assimilation rate (NAR)
Dry-matter gain per unit leaf area and time (g m⁻² d⁻¹); also called unit leaf rate.
Leaf area ratio (LAR)
Leaf area per unit plant dry mass (m² g⁻¹); LAR = SLA × LMR.
Specific leaf area (SLA)
Leaf area per unit leaf dry mass (m² g⁻¹ or cm² g⁻¹); high values mean thin leaves.
Leaf mass ratio (LMR)
Fraction of plant dry mass in leaves (g g⁻¹); also called leaf weight ratio.
Leaf area index (LAI)
One-sided leaf area per unit ground area (m² m⁻²).
Extinction coefficient (k)
Rate constant of exponential light attenuation per unit leaf area index; depends on leaf angles and light direction.
Monsi–Saeki law
Beer–Lambert description of light in a canopy, I = I₀e−kL; fraction intercepted 1 − e−k·LAI.
Light-use efficiency (LUE)
Dry matter produced per mole of PAR photons, on an incident or intercepted basis (g mol⁻¹).
Radiation-use efficiency (RUE)
Dry matter produced per unit of intercepted radiant energy (g MJ⁻¹); Monteith's ≈ 1.4 g MJ⁻¹ of solar energy for C3 crops.
Harvest index (HI)
Fraction of total dry matter that is harvested (0.4–0.6 for modern grain crops, ≈ 0.85 for lettuce).
Expolinear growth
Growth that is exponential while the canopy is open and linear once it intercepts nearly all light (Goudriaan & Monteith, 1990).
Projected leaf area (PLA)
Leaf area visible in a top-view image; a non-destructive growth measure that must be calibrated against harvests.
Mechanistic crop model
Model that predicts growth from process equations (interception, photosynthesis, respiration) with physically interpretable parameters.
