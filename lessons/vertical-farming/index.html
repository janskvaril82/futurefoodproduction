Vertical farms and plant factories with artificial lighting

In a former warehouse, lettuce grows on ten or more shelves stacked to the ceiling under magenta light — without sunlight, soil, pesticides or seasons. Almost all of the water is recycled, and each square metre of floor yields tens of times more than a field. Yet between 2022 and 2025 many of the best-funded vertical-farming companies in the world closed or went through bankruptcy. Both facts follow from the same handful of balances — for photons, heat, water, CO₂ and money. This lesson derives them one by one, so that you can judge for yourself where a plant factory makes sense and where it does not.

  • Build a cost-per-kilogram model with capital, electricity, labour and consumables, and use it to explain the 2022–2025 wave of failures and the business models that survived (LO1, LO7).
  • Judge where vertical farming makes sense, considering climate, the electricity system, land and water scarcity and food-security policy, including Nordic cases (LO1, LO7).

What is a vertical farm?

The phrase "vertical farm" was popularised by the ecologist Dickson Despommier, who imagined skyscrapers in which each floor would be a field (Despommier, 2010). The farms that were actually built look different: they are mostly single-storey warehouses, shipping containers or basement rooms in which plants grow on many shelves, one above the other, under electric light. A recent review in Nature Food therefore defines vertical farming systems (VFS) simply as multi-layer indoor crop production systems with artificial lights, in which growth conditions are precisely controlled — and notes that there is no consensus on terminology (van Delden et al., 2021).

The most precise engineering definition comes from Japan, where commercial indoor farming started earliest. Toyoki Kozai describes a plant factory with artificial lighting (PFAL) as a closed plant production system consisting of six principal components: a thermally insulated and airtight structure, a multi-tier system with lighting devices, air conditioners and fans, a CO₂ supply unit, a nutrient-solution supply unit and an environment control unit (Kozai, 2013). The purpose of such a closed plant production system (CPPS) is to maximise plant growth with the minimum input of light energy, water, CO₂ and fertiliser, which Kozai quantifies as the resource use efficiency (RUE): the amount of a resource fixed or held in the plants divided by the amount supplied to the system. In this lesson "vertical farm" and "plant factory" are used interchangeably for such fully enclosed, artificially lit, multi-tier systems.

What grows well in a PFAL? Kozai (2013) gives four practical criteria: plants that can be grown at high planting density (50–1000 plants per m²), under relatively low light (PPFD 100–300 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), within a short cultivation period (30–60 days) and that stay shorter than about 30 cm, because the multi-tier racks are what make the land productivity high. Leafy greens, herbs, microgreens, baby leaf and transplants (seedlings) meet these criteria; wheat, rice, maize and potatoes do not. Kozai states plainly that the CPPS "is not for production of staple food plants", and van Delden et al. (2021) conclude that vertical farms are currently economically unsuitable for staple foods in practically all markets. Keep this in mind whenever you read that vertical farming will "feed the world".

Japan has the longest commercial experience. The number of commercially operating PFALs there increased from 64 in 2011 to 197 in 2017 and then stagnated; by 2021 the total cultivated area was still below 60 ha, and nearly 80 % of the PFALs that existed around 2012 had disappeared within a decade, replaced by roughly the same number of new ones (Zhuang et al., 2022). This high turnover — a busy "graveyard" alongside a stable total — is the first hint that the economics of plant factories are fragile, a theme we return to in Section 8.

Key idea

A vertical farm replaces the Sun with electricity. That single decision buys total control over climate, hygiene and timing — and makes electricity (for light and for removing the heat that light becomes) the dominant resource, cost and climate burden. Almost every equation in this lesson is a consequence of it.

Anatomy of a plant factory

A PFAL is a building-services engineering project with plants inside. Figure 2 shows the components you would find in a typical lettuce factory. The design logic is easiest to understand by following the three things that flow through it: light and heat, water and nutrients, and air with CO₂.

Envelope and hygiene. The building is insulated and nearly airtight: Kozai (2013) recommends an air-exchange rate below about 0.02 h⁻¹, both to keep the enriched CO₂ inside and to keep insects, pathogens and dust out. People enter through an anteroom with gowning and often an air shower. Because pests cannot get in, no pesticides are needed; because the envelope isolates the room thermally, a well-insulated factory needs no heating even on cold nights — it is enough to switch on a share of the lamps (Kozai, 2013).

Racks and tiers. Plants grow in trays or channels on 5–20 tiers. The tier spacing is set by crop height plus the fixture and the air gap needed for air circulation, which is why short crops are preferred. Racks can be static (workers or lifts reach the tiers from aisles) or mobile, with trays conveyed automatically to a transplanting or harvesting station. Plants are often re-spaced as they grow so that the canopy covers the tray at every stage — an important lever for light capture (Eq. 7.2.3).

Lighting. Light-emitting diodes are now universal. The best horticultural fixtures in 2020 converted electricity to photosynthetic photons at about 2.5–3.0 µmol J⁻¹, with a projected practical maximum of roughly 3.4–4.1 µmol J⁻¹ depending on spectrum (Kusuma et al., 2020; Lesson 4.3). Fixtures sit 10–40 cm above the canopy. Because the lamps must be cooled and the crop dehumidified at all hours, the tiers are often divided into two or three groups that are lit in rotation, 12–16 hours each, so that some lamps are always on and the air-conditioners run continuously (Kozai, 2013).

HVAC. Heating, ventilation and air-conditioning units — in practice heat pumps used for cooling — hold the air at about 20–25 °C and 70–80 % relative humidity, and fans drive a horizontal air current of about 0.3–0.5 m s⁻¹ through the canopy to thin the leaf boundary layers and keep conditions uniform (Kozai, 2013; Lesson 2.2). The cooling coil is kept below the dew point of the room air, so that it condenses the transpired water — the plant factory's built-in water recovery (Eq. 7.2.6).

CO₂. Pure CO₂ from tanks is dosed to set points of 1000–2000 µmol mol⁻¹ — higher than the 700–1000 µmol mol⁻¹ common in greenhouses, because the airtight room loses little of it (Kozai, 2013; Lesson 8.3).

Nutrient delivery. Most factories use recirculating hydroponics — deep-flow or nutrient-film channels (Lesson 5.1) — and a few use aeroponics (Lesson 7.1). EC and pH are controlled automatically (Lesson 5.2), and the returning solution is filtered and disinfected, often with UV light, because recirculation can spread root pathogens through the whole farm (Lesson 5.3).

Automation and robotics. Labour constitutes a large part of total production costs — 25–30 % according to van Delden et al. (2021) — so seeding, transplanting, re-spacing, tray transport and harvesting are increasingly automated. When Spread built its Techno Farm Keihanna near Kyoto, the company announced that automated cultivation would cut labour costs by 50 % compared with its older, largely manual Kameoka plant (Fruitnet, 2017). Fully robotic rooms have further advantages: no aisles, no disease brought in by people, and the freedom to use high CO₂ or UV that would be unsafe for workers (van Delden et al., 2021). Their disadvantage is capital cost — the other large item in the cost per kilogram (Section 7).

Space-use efficiency and yield per floor area

The most spectacular claim for vertical farms is their land productivity. It comes from four multipliers: stacking, short cycles, year-round operation and high density. The first of them is pure geometry.

Cultivation-area ratio (space-use efficiency)(7.2.1)
\[ \text{CAR} = \frac{A_{\text{cult}}}{A_{\text{floor}}} = f_{\text{grow}}\; f_{\text{rack}}\; N_{\text{t}} \]

the cultivation-area ratio is the total area of growing trays divided by the floor area of the building; it equals the fraction of the floor used for grow rooms, times the fraction of the grow-room floor covered by racks, times the number of tiers in each rack.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(\text{CAR}\)cultivation-area ratio: m² of growing area per m² of floor–2–8 (building), 4–10 (grow room)
\(A_{\text{cult}}\)total tray (cultivation) area on all tiersm²
\(A_{\text{floor}}\)floor area of the building (or of the grow room)m²
\(f_{\text{grow}}\)share of the building floor used by grow rooms (rest: nursery, packing, cold store, plant rooms, corridors)–0.5–0.8
\(f_{\text{rack}}\)share of grow-room floor covered by racks (rest: aisles)–0.4–0.8
\(N_{\text{t}}\)number of growing tiers per rack–5–20
Follow the derivation
  1. The grow rooms occupy \(f_{\text{grow}}A_{\text{floor}}\) of the floor.
  2. Racks cover \(f_{\text{rack}}\) of that area, so the rack footprint is \(f_{\text{grow}}f_{\text{rack}}A_{\text{floor}}\).
  3. Each square metre of rack footprint carries one tray per tier, so \(A_{\text{cult}} = N_{\text{t}}\,f_{\text{grow}}f_{\text{rack}}A_{\text{floor}}\). Dividing by \(A_{\text{floor}}\) gives Eq. 7.2.1.
  4. Some authors call CAR the space-use efficiency; others express space use per unit of building volume (m² of trays per m³). Always state which floor area is in the denominator: that of the grow room alone or of the whole building.
Assumptions and validity

Assumes all tiers have the same tray area and are fully planted. Mobile racks (which close the aisles) and robotic systems can push \(f_{\text{rack}}\) towards 0.8–0.9; tall ceilings allow more tiers but increase the cost of lifts, fire protection and air distribution.

Geometry alone is not yield. What reaches the market in a year depends on how many plants each square metre of tray carries, how heavy they are at harvest, and how many cycles fit into a year.

Annual marketable yield per floor area(7.2.2)
\[ Y_{\text{floor}} = \text{CAR}\cdot Y_{\text{cult}},\qquad Y_{\text{cult}} = \rho_{p}\; m_{h}\;\frac{365}{t_c + t_{\text{turn}}}\;(1 - f_{\text{loss}}) \]

the yield per square metre of floor equals the cultivation-area ratio times the yield per square metre of tray; the latter is the number of plants per square metre times the marketable mass of each plant times the number of cycles per year — 365 days divided by the growing period plus the turnaround time — reduced by the fraction lost.

  2. A tray position is occupied for \(t_c + t_{\text{turn}}\) days per cycle, so it completes \(365/(t_c+t_{\text{turn}})\) cycles per year.

Assumptions and validity

Continuous, staggered production (every day some trays are harvested and replanted). Seasonal price effects, downtime for maintenance and disease outbreaks are lumped into \(f_{\text{loss}}\). Yield per plant and cycle length are not independent of light: more light shortens \(t_c\) but costs electricity (Eq. 7.2.3).

Worked example 1A lettuce factory in a Västerås warehouse

A company converts a 2000 m² warehouse in Västerås into a lettuce factory. Grow rooms will occupy 60 % of the floor; inside them, racks cover 50 % of the floor and carry 10 tiers. In the final growth stage there are 30 plants m⁻², harvested after 21 days at 120 g each; turnaround takes 1 day and 5 % of plants are lost. Calculate the cultivation area, the CAR, the annual yield per m² of tray and per m² of floor, the annual output, and the daily number of heads. How does the floor yield compare with an open field producing an assumed 4 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Cultivation area (Eq. 7.2.1): \(A_{\text{cult}} = 2000\times0.60\times0.50\times10 = 6000\) m². CAR = 6000/2000 = 3.0 for the building (and 0.5 × 10 = 5.0 for the grow rooms alone).
  2. Cycles per year: \(365/(21+1) = 16.6\).
  3. Yield per tray area (Eq. 7.2.2): \(30\times0.120\times16.6\times0.95 = 56.7\) kg m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  4. Yield per floor area: \(3.0\times56.7 = 170\) kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (building) or \(5.0\times56.7 = 284\) kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (grow room).
  5. Annual output: \(6000\times56.7 = 340\,000\) kg = 340 t of lettuce; per day \(30\times6000/22\times0.95 \approx 7800\) heads.
  6. Comparison with a field: \(170/4 \approx 43\) times more per m² of building than the assumed field yield — high, but well short of the "hundredfold" often quoted, because 40 % of the building and half of each grow room are not racks.

Kozai (2013) decomposed the land productivity of a 10-tier PFAL relative to an open field into five factors: ×10 from 10 tiers; ×2 from a shorter growing period under optimal conditions (germination and seedling production elsewhere); ×2 from year-round production with no time lost between harvest and replanting; ×2 from higher planting density thanks to controlled air speed and VPD; and ×1.2 from the absence of weather and pest damage — together 10 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 1.2 = 96, "nearly 100-fold". Worked example 1 shows why real buildings deliver less: the tier factor applies to rack footprint, not to the whole building. Conversely, Jin et al. (2023), using the highest light-use efficiency ever measured for lettuce, estimated that a single layer could in principle produce up to 700 kg m⁻² per year at 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of continuous light — a theoretical ceiling, not a business plan, because the electricity bill grows in proportion to the light.

The kilowatt-hours in a kilogram of lettuce

A plant factory buys photons with electricity. How many kilowatt-hours does one kilogram of lettuce contain? The question sounds complicated, but it decomposes into three efficiencies that you can look up, measure and improve one at a time:

  • Photon efficacy \(\eta_{\text{ph}}\): how many micromoles of photosynthetic photons a fixture emits per joule of electricity (Lesson 4.3).
  • Photon capture \(f_{\text{cap}}\): the fraction of the emitted photons that land on the cultivation area rather than on walls, aisles, rack frames or empty tray between young plants. Kozai (2013) reports 0.63–0.71 for older fluorescent installations; well-designed LED racks close to a closed canopy do better.
  • Photon-use efficiency PUE: grams of marketable fresh produce per mole of photons incident on the cultivation area — the fresh-weight version of the light-use efficiency of Lesson 2.1. Across 53 vertical-farm studies, Jin et al. (2023) found an average light-use efficiency for lettuce of 0.55 g of shoot dry weight per mole of incident photons (greenhouses 0.39, open field 0.23), a maximum of 1.63 g mol⁻¹, and a theoretical maximum of 1.26–1.81 g mol⁻¹.
Lighting electricity per kilogram: the photon decomposition(7.2.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} e_{\text{light}} = \frac{\overbrace{\text{DLI}\;t_c\,/\,(\eta_{\text{ph}}\,f_{\text{cap}})}^{\text{electricity per m}^2\text{ per cycle}}}{\underbrace{\text{PUE}\;\text{DLI}\;t_c}_{\text{yield per m}^2\text{ per cycle}}} = \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{ph}}\;f_{\text{cap}}\;\text{PUE}} \\[6pt] e_{\text{light}}\ [\text{kWh kg}^{-1}] = \frac{277.8}{\eta_{\text{ph}}\;f_{\text{cap}}\;\text{PUE}},\qquad \text{PUE} = \frac{\text{LUE}_{\text{DW}}}{x_{\text{DM}}} \end{gathered} \]

the lighting electricity per kilogram equals the electricity used per square metre over a crop cycle — the photons that reach the canopy, divided by the photon efficacy and by the capture fraction — divided by the yield per square metre, which is the photon-use efficiency times the same photons. The daily light integral and the cycle length cancel, leaving 277.8 divided by the product of photon efficacy, photon capture and photon-use efficiency, in practical units. The fresh-weight photon-use efficiency is the dry-weight light-use efficiency divided by the dry-matter content.

Follow the derivation
  1. Photons at the canopy. Over one cycle each m² of tray receives \(\text{DLI}\cdot t_c\) mol of photons.
  2. Photons emitted. Only the fraction \(f_{\text{cap}}\) of what the fixtures emit arrives there, so the fixtures must emit \(\text{DLI}\,t_c/f_{\text{cap}}\) mol.
  3. Electricity. A fixture emits \(\eta_{\text{ph}}\) µmol per joule, so the electrical energy is \(\text{DLI}\,t_c\times10^{6}/(\eta_{\text{ph}}f_{\text{cap}})\) J per m², or that divided by \(3.6\times10^{6}\) J kWh⁻¹ in kWh.
  4. Yield. By the definition of PUE, the same m² yields \(\text{PUE}\cdot\text{DLI}\cdot t_c\) grams, i.e. \(\text{PUE}\,\text{DLI}\,t_c/1000\) kg.
  5. Divide. \(\dfrac{\text{DLI}\,t_c\,10^{6}/(3.6\times10^{6}\,\eta_{\text{ph}}f_{\text{cap}})}{\text{PUE}\,\text{DLI}\,t_c/1000} = \dfrac{10^{6}\times1000}{3.6\times10^{6}}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\eta_{\text{ph}}f_{\text{cap}}\text{PUE}} = \dfrac{277.8}{\eta_{\text{ph}}f_{\text{cap}}\text{PUE}}\).
  6. Interpretation. DLI and cycle length cancel: at a given photon-use efficiency, giving more light only makes the crop faster, not cheaper per kilogram. They matter indirectly, because PUE itself falls when the light is so strong that leaves saturate, and rises when the canopy closes quickly.
Assumptions and validity

PUE is defined on incident photons on the cultivation area, as in Jin et al. (2023); converting dry weight to fresh weight with a fixed dry-matter content ignores that lettuce grown under high light is often drier. Photon efficacy is that of the complete fixture (driver, optics, thermal losses), not of the bare LED chip. The equation counts only lighting; pumps, fans and the HVAC come on top (Eq. 7.2.5).

Where does the electricity go? Almost none of it is stored. The chemical energy in the harvested dry matter is only a few per cent of the electricity, and everything else becomes heat that the HVAC system must remove.

Fraction of lighting electricity stored in biomass(7.2.4)
\[ \varepsilon_{\text{chem}} = \frac{\text{LUE}_{\text{DW}}\;q_{\text{DM}}}{10^{6}/(\eta_{\text{ph}}\,f_{\text{cap}})} = \text{LUE}_{\text{DW}}\;q_{\text{DM}}\;\eta_{\text{ph}}\;f_{\text{cap}}\times10^{-6} \]

the fraction of the lighting electricity that ends up as chemical energy in the plants equals the dry mass produced per mole of photons at the canopy times the energy content of dry matter, divided by the electricity needed to deliver that mole of photons to the canopy.

Every watt becomes heat: HVAC, dehumidification and water recovery

Draw a boundary around a sealed plant factory. Electricity enters; lettuce, a little CO₂-enriched air and heat leave. Because the envelope is insulated and airtight and no sunlight enters, "the cooling load of the CPPS is almost equal to the heat generated by the lamps and other electric equipment" (Kozai, 2013). Only the few per cent stored as chemical energy (Eq. 7.2.4) escape being heat. The air-conditioners — heat pumps working in cooling mode — must pump all that heat out, and they need electricity to do it. Lesson 8.1 treats the full energy balance; here we need its bottom line.

Total electricity per kilogram, including HVAC(7.2.5)
\[ e_{\text{tot}} = e_{\text{light}}\,(1 + f_{\text{aux}})\left(1 + \frac{1-\varepsilon_{\text{chem}}}{\text{COP}_c}\right) \;\approx\; e_{\text{light}}\,(1 + f_{\text{aux}})\left(1 + \frac{1}{\text{COP}_c}\right) \]

the total electricity per kilogram equals the lighting electricity, increased by the auxiliary share for pumps, fans and controls, and increased again by the electricity the heat pumps need to remove all this energy as heat — the heat divided by the cooling coefficient of performance. Because the stored fraction is only a few per cent, it can usually be neglected.

Using outdoor air directly for cooling ("free cooling") is possible in cold climates but costs CO₂ and humidity control, and weakens the hygiene barrier.

The price of removing heat depends on the COP, and the COP depends on the outdoor temperature. Water, CO₂ and nutrients: closing the loops

If electricity is the weakness of plant factories, water and CO₂ are their strengths. Kozai (2013) defines the water-use efficiency of a production system as the water held in the harvested plants divided by the water supplied. In a closed plant factory it is 0.95–0.98, because transpired water is condensed and reused; in a greenhouse with open vents it is only 0.02–0.03, because the transpired vapour escapes with the ventilation air — a difference of 30–50 times. Van Delden et al. (2021) estimate that closed vertical farms can reduce water use by about 90 % compared with greenhouses and by about 99 % compared with open fields, while noting that closed greenhouses with active cooling can recover water in the same way. In the modelling of Graamans et al. (2018), plant factories achieved higher productivity for water, CO₂ and land than greenhouses in all three climates studied.

What does 0.95 mean per kilogram? A kilogram of lettuce contains about 0.95 kg of water. If the water-use efficiency is 0.95, the farm supplies \(0.95/0.95 = 1.0\) L per kilogram; at 0.025 (open greenhouse) it would be \(0.95/0.025 = 38\) L. Worked example 3 showed that the crop still circulates about 11 L per kilogram — but through the plant, the air, the coil and back to the tank.

CO₂ tells a similar story. The crop fixes carbon into its dry matter: lettuce dry matter is about 40 % carbon (Anderson et al., 2018), so each kilogram of fresh lettuce with 5 % dry matter contains \(0.05\times0.40\times44/12 = 0.073\) kg of CO₂-equivalent carbon. Whatever is dosed beyond that leaks out.

in steady state the CO₂ that must be supplied equals the net uptake by the crop plus the CO₂ lost with leaking air — the air-exchange rate times the room volume times the excess concentration inside, converted from micromoles per mole to kilograms per cubic metre with the ideal-gas law. The CO₂-use efficiency is the fraction of the supplied CO₂ fixed by the crop.

Cost per kilogram: capital, electricity and labour

A plant factory is an expensive machine that turns electricity, labour and capital into a cheap product. To see whether it can pay, we need all three on the same basis — euros per kilogram. Operating costs are straightforward; capital needs one extra step: spreading the investment over the years of the building's life, including the interest the investors expect.

Levelised production cost per kilogram(7.2.9)
\[ c = \frac{\text{CRF}\cdot I}{Y_{\text{cult}}} + e_{\text{tot}}\,p_{\text{el}} + c_{\text{lab}} + c_{\text{cons}} + c_{\text{oth}},\qquad \text{CRF} = \frac{r\,(1+r)^{n}}{(1+r)^{n}-1} \]

the cost of one kilogram equals the annualised capital cost per square metre of tray — the investment times the capital recovery factor — divided by the annual yield per square metre, plus the electricity per kilogram times its price, plus labour, consumables and other costs per kilogram. The capital recovery factor turns a one-off investment into equal annual payments over \(n\) years at interest rate \(r\).

How large is labour? Kozai (2013) gives a revealing data point: a commercial PFAL in Kashiwa, Japan, with a culture-room floor of 338 m², had an annual capacity of one million lettuce heads, run by one manager and ten part-time workers working seven hours a day. That is about \(10\times7\times365 = 25\,550\) hours per million heads, or 1.5 minutes per head — at a Swedish labour cost of, say, €30 per hour, about €0.77 per head, or several euros per kilogram of small heads. Hence the push for automation. For Japanese PFALs, Zhuang et al. (2022) estimated operating costs for lettuce of US$700 per m² per year, of which labour was 40 %, electricity 24 %, packaging and logistics 22 % and seeds and nutrients 12 %, against revenue of about US$1200 m⁻² yr⁻¹ (115 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ at US$11 kg⁻¹). They warned that "a 30 % decline either in the yield or the price of lettuce would easily bring PFALs bankruptcy", and found that construction cost per m² falls only slowly with scale (elasticity −0.17: a tenfold larger farm is about a third cheaper per m²).

Why did so many fail? The equations of this lesson give a coherent answer, and it is not that plants refuse to grow indoors.

  1. Capital intensity meets the end of cheap money. Capital was the largest cost item in Worked example 5. When interest rates rose from 2022, the cost of capital in Eq. 7.2.9 rose with them, and investors who had funded rapid expansion stopped funding losses. Bowery's US$150 million loan "weighed heavily" on the company (Agriculture Dive, 2024).
  2. An electricity shock. In 2022 European electricity prices spiked. With 10–20 kWh per kilogram, a price rise of €0.20 kWh⁻¹ adds €2–4 per kilogram — more than the whole margin. Infarm's closures in Europe followed (Vertical Farm Daily, 2023).
  3. A cheap competitor. Lettuce and herbs are sold against field and greenhouse produce that uses free sunlight (Table 3). A "premium" story (local, pesticide-free, consistent) supports a higher price only up to a point, and demand for premium produce wavered (Agriculture Dive, 2024).
  4. Scaling before the unit economics worked. Several companies built multiple large farms before a single farm had shown a stable profit, so every inefficiency was multiplied. Zhuang et al. (2022) found only weak economies of scale in PFAL construction, so building bigger did not rescue the arithmetic.
  5. Biological and operational risk. A root-disease outbreak in a recirculating system, or a failed HVAC unit, can empty a whole farm; thin margins leave no buffer (Lesson 5.3).
Common misconception

"The bankruptcies prove that vertical farming doesn't work." The agronomy works: indoor lettuce grows fast, uniformly and cleanly, and the best light-use efficiencies approach the theoretical maximum (Jin et al., 2023). What failed were business models that sold a low-priced commodity from very capital-intensive, electricity-hungry buildings financed on optimistic growth assumptions. The distinction matters, because it tells you which conditions would change the verdict: cheaper capital, cheaper clean electricity, higher photon-use efficiency, automation, higher-value crops or public food-security goals.

What do the survivors look like? Several patterns emerge.

  • Patient operators with modest, standardised farms. Japan's Spread made its non-automated Kameoka plant profitable in 2013 (HortiDaily, 2020a) and then built the automated Techno Farm Keihanna, which reported a 99 % operating rate, more than 90 % water recycling and supply to 3600 stores in early 2021 (Vertical Farm Daily, 2021). Kozai (2013) concluded that a PFAL, "if properly designed and operated, can contribute to the production of value-added plants at high yield with efficient resource consumption".
  • High-value, short-cycle products. Microgreens (the post-2026 AeroFarms), herbs and baby leaf have short cycles and high prices per kilogram, which divides the capital charge by more euros.
  • Transplants. Most facilities that produce only seedlings and plantlets are designed as closed plant production systems (Kozai, 2013): a few days of perfect conditions give uniform, disease-free young plants for greenhouses and fields, and the value per square metre is high.
  • Medicinal and other speciality plants. Van Delden et al. (2021) point to pharmaceutical crops and forgotten crops as niche markets for which total control is worth its price. Ljusgårda's Tibro factory now grows more than ten medicinal species, including tulsi, ribwort plantain, borage and Artemisia annua, in batches of 14 000 plants (Vertical Farm Daily, 2026).
  • Fruit — with caution. Plenty's Richmond farm is designed to grow more than 4 million pounds (about 1800 t) of strawberries a year on vertical towers (Plenty, n.d.). Yet Zhuang et al. (2022) calculated that strawberry production in PFALs was "still premature", because the break-even scale was far larger than any existing farm. Whether indoor strawberries can pay is one of the open questions of 2026.
  • Small, embedded systems. In-store farms such as Swegreen's cabinets in Swedish ICA supermarkets sell freshness and visibility rather than tonnes; new installations were commissioned in 2026 (Swegreen, 2026).
  • Farms that are also energy-system components. Iceland's VAXA grows microalgae in sealed, LED-lit modules integrated with a large geothermal power plant, using its renewable electricity, hot and cold water and CO₂ emissions (designboom, 2022) — the opposite of a stand-alone warehouse on an expensive grid.

Among large leafy-green growers, 80 Acres Farms, GoodLeaf Farms (Canada) and GrowUp Farms (UK) were still operating and raising capital in early 2025 (AgFunderNews, 2025).

Where does vertical farming make sense?

The comparison with greenhouses is the right benchmark, because greenhouses supply most of the same crops. Graamans et al. (2018) modelled lettuce production in a plant factory and in greenhouses in the Netherlands, the United Arab Emirates and northern Sweden (Kiruna, 68° N). The plant factory was the most efficient system in total energy (1411 MJ kg⁻¹ DW, against 1699 MJ kg⁻¹ DW for the best greenhouse) and in water, CO₂ and land use — but it needed the most purchased energy: 247 kWh of electricity per kg DW against 70 kWh in the Netherlands, 111 in the UAE and 182 (with supplementary lighting) or 211 kWh (without) in Kiruna. Notice the pattern: in the mild, bright Netherlands the plant factory needs 3.5 times more purchased energy than a greenhouse; in Kiruna, where a greenhouse must be heated and lit through a long dark winter, only 17–36 % more. "The local scarcity of resources determines the suitability of production systems" (Graamans et al., 2018).

The Nordic picture. The Nordic countries combine favourable conditions — low-carbon electricity, a cold climate that makes cooling cheap (Figure 7), district-heating networks and strong demand for local produce — with unfavourable ones: high labour costs, small markets and long, dark winters that make any year-round production energy-hungry. The companies reflect this mixed verdict. In Sweden, Grönska (Stockholm) went bankrupt in 2024; Ljusgårda, which had converted a 7000 m² former wood factory in Tibro into a lettuce farm (HortiDaily, 2020b) and raised about US$50 million (Impact Loop, 2026), stopped lettuce in 2026 and turned to medicinal plants; Swegreen continues to install in-store farms in ICA supermarkets. In Denmark, Nordic Harvest opened one of Europe's largest vertical farms near Copenhagen in 2020; its operating status in 2026 could not be verified for this lesson. Iceland's VAXA shows the energy-integrated alternative. None of these cases contradicts the equations; each is a different point in the space spanned by Eqs. 7.2.3–7.2.9.

Key terms

Vertical farming system (VFS)
Multi-layer indoor crop production with artificial light in which growth conditions are precisely controlled.
Plant factory with artificial lighting (PFAL)
A thermally insulated, airtight building with multi-tier lighting, air conditioners and fans, CO₂ supply, nutrient supply and an environment control unit (Kozai).
Closed plant production system (CPPS)
The design concept behind a PFAL: a closed system operated to maximise the use efficiencies of light, water, CO₂ and fertiliser.
Resource use efficiency (RUE)
The amount of a resource fixed or held in the plants divided by the amount supplied to the production system.
Cultivation-area ratio (CAR)
Growing-tray area divided by floor area; the space-use efficiency of a vertical farm.
Photon efficacy
Photosynthetic photons emitted per joule of electricity by a fixture (µmol J⁻¹).
Photon capture
The fraction of emitted photons that reach the cultivation area rather than walls, aisles or empty trays.
Photon-use efficiency (PUE)
Marketable fresh mass produced per mole of photons incident on the crop (g mol⁻¹); the fresh-weight light-use efficiency.
Coefficient of performance (COP)
Heat removed by a cooling machine per unit of electricity; depends on the temperature lift and hence on the outdoor climate.
Latent heat load
The part of the cooling load that condenses transpired water vapour on the coil; in a sealed factory equal to λ times the transpiration.
CO₂-use efficiency (CUE)
CO₂ fixed by the crop divided by CO₂ supplied; 0.87–0.89 in airtight plant factories.
Capital recovery factor (CRF)
The factor \(r(1+r)^n/((1+r)^n-1)\) that converts an investment into equal annual payments over n years at interest r.
Levelised cost of food
The cost per kilogram that covers annualised capital and all operating costs over the life of a production system.
