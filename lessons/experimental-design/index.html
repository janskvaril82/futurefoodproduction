Designing plant-growth experiments

Two student groups grow lettuce under white LEDs with and without extra far-red light. One group reports a 25 % yield increase, the other finds nothing. Both measured carefully; both used a t-test. The difference lies not in the statistics but in decisions taken weeks earlier — where the jars stood on the shelf, what counted as a replicate, how many plants were grown. This lesson teaches you to take those decisions deliberately, so that your course-project experiment can answer the question you actually asked.

What you will be able to do

  • Turn a vague idea into a testable research question with a null and an alternative hypothesis, and decide whether the hypothesis should be directional (LO4).
  • Classify independent, dependent, controlled and confounding variables, and build single-factor and factorial treatment structures with suitable controls, including the interpretation of interactions (LO4).
  • Identify the experimental unit in a hydroponic set-up, recognise pseudo-replication and state the correct sample size \(n\) (LO4).
  • Protect an experiment against gradients in a growth room using randomisation, randomised complete blocks and Latin squares, and quantify the gain from blocking (LO4).
  • Estimate the number of plants needed for adequate statistical power from an expected effect size and coefficient of variation (LO4, LO5).
  • Write a measurement plan, a tidy data sheet and a preregistration, and address the safety and ethical aspects of a growing and tasting project (LO4).

Why design comes before data

An experiment is a question put to nature in a form that nature can answer unambiguously. Everything else in this module — descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, the laboratory report — works on the answers. If the question was badly put, no amount of arithmetic will repair it. The statistician Ronald A. Fisher, who developed most of the ideas in this lesson while analysing crop trials at Rothamsted Experimental Station in England, put it bluntly: "To consult the statistician after an experiment is finished is often merely to ask him to conduct a post mortem examination. He can perhaps say what the experiment died of" (Fisher, 1938).

A plant-growth experiment is especially vulnerable because plants integrate everything that happens to them. A lettuce plant that sits 30 cm closer to the edge of an LED panel, next to a warm pump, or in a jar that happened to receive 5 % more nutrient solution will differ from its neighbour for reasons that have nothing to do with your treatment. If those reasons line up with your treatment, you will see an "effect" that is not there — or hide one that is. The job of experimental design is to make sure that the only thing that systematically differs between your treatment groups is the treatment.

Fisher's answer, formulated in The Design of Experiments (1935), rests on three principles that we will develop one by one:

  1. Replication — apply each treatment to several independent experimental units, so that you can measure how much units vary without any treatment difference, and so that treatment means become precise.
  2. Randomisation — let chance, not you, decide which unit gets which treatment, so that unknown sources of variation cannot line up with treatments except by (quantifiable) accident.
  3. Local control (blocking) — group units that are alike (same shelf position, same day, same tank) and compare treatments within these groups, so that known sources of variation are removed from the comparison.
Key idea

Design decides what you can conclude; analysis only decides how precisely. A well-designed experiment with a simple analysis beats a badly designed experiment with a sophisticated analysis every time. A widely quoted rule from the textbook of Box, Hunter and Hunter (2005) summarises the strategy: block what you can, randomise what you cannot.

The scientific method that frames all of this is not a straight line but a cycle (Figure 1). You start from existing knowledge and a question, formulate a hypothesis, design an experiment to test it, collect data, analyse them, and draw conclusions that feed back into new questions. Your course project will go around this cycle once; research programmes go around it hundreds of times. Notice that the cycle contains two places where honesty is essential: the design step (did you decide your analysis before seeing the data?) and the conclusion step (do your conclusions go further than your data allow?). We return to both in the sections on preregistration and in Lesson 3.4.

From question to testable hypothesis

Most projects begin with a vague interest: "We want to see whether LED colour matters for lettuce." This is a topic, not a question. A good research question names the organism and system, the manipulated factor and its levels, the response you will measure, and when you will measure it. A useful template for growth experiments is:

Question template

In [crop and cultivar] grown in [system and conditions], does [treatment] compared with [control] change [response variable, unit] at [time]?

Example: In butterhead lettuce grown in Kratky jars at 22 °C and a PPFD of 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 16 h d⁻¹, does replacing part of the white LED light with far-red light (700–750 nm) compared with white light alone change shoot fresh mass (g plant⁻¹) 35 days after transplanting?

The question already contains the design: one factor (spectrum) with two levels, one response, one time point, and a list of things held constant. The hypothesis is a tentative answer that could turn out to be wrong. Good hypotheses are grounded in a mechanism. Here the mechanism is well documented: far-red photons lower the phytochrome photostationary state, which triggers shade-avoidance responses such as leaf expansion; larger leaves intercept more light, and the plant grows faster (Meng et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2021; see Lesson 4.4). So we expect more fresh mass with far-red.

Statistical testing (Lesson 3.3) needs this expectation phrased as a pair of statements about populations — about what would happen if you grew infinitely many plants under each treatment:

Null and alternative hypotheses for a two-treatment comparison(3.1.1)
\[ H_0:\ \mu_{\text{FR}} - \mu_{\text{W}} = 0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\ \mu_{\text{FR}} - \mu_{\text{W}} \neq 0 \quad\left(\text{directional: } H_1:\ \mu_{\text{FR}} - \mu_{\text{W}} > 0\right) \]

the null hypothesis says that the population mean fresh mass under white-plus-far-red light equals that under white light; the (two-sided) alternative says that they differ in either direction; a directional (one-sided) alternative says specifically that far-red gives the larger mean.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mu_{\text{FR}}\)population mean shoot fresh mass under white + far-red lightg plant⁻¹
\(\mu_{\text{W}}\)population mean shoot fresh mass under white light (control)g plant⁻¹
\(H_0\)null hypothesis: "no effect"; the statement the test tries to reject–
\(H_1\)alternative hypothesis: the effect you expect or want to detect–
Why test the null rather than our own hypothesis?
  1. A general statement ("far-red increases growth") can never be proven by a finite number of observations, but it can be contradicted. This asymmetry, emphasised by the philosopher Karl Popper, is why science advances by trying to falsify claims.
  2. The null hypothesis is a precise statement (difference = 0). Precise statements allow us to calculate how probable our data would be if it were true — that is what a p-value is (Lesson 3.3).
  3. If the data are very improbable under \(H_0\), we reject \(H_0\) in favour of \(H_1\). If they are not, we fail to reject \(H_0\) — which is not the same as proving that there is no effect.
When is a directional hypothesis justified?

Only when (1) theory and previous evidence predict the direction before you see the data, and (2) an effect in the opposite direction would be treated exactly like no effect. A one-sided test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) puts all 5 % in one tail, making it easier to detect an effect in the expected direction — which is exactly why choosing it after seeing the data is a form of cheating. In growth experiments, surprising effects in the "wrong" direction are often the most interesting results, so the two-sided alternative is the safe default. If you do choose a directional test, write it in your preregistration.

Both sides of each statement are in g plant⁻¹; the hypotheses compare quantities of the same unit ✓

Three properties separate useful hypotheses from useless ones. They must be falsifiable (some possible outcome would contradict them), operationalised (every term maps to a measurement: "grow better" becomes "higher shoot fresh mass at day 35"), and relevant (the answer matters to someone — a grower deciding whether to buy far-red LEDs, or a scientist testing the shade-avoidance mechanism).

Worked example 1From an idea to hypotheses

A group writes in their project plan: "We think a higher nutrient strength is better for basil, so we will test it." Rewrite this as a research question and a pair of statistical hypotheses. Identify what makes the original statement untestable.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Diagnose the problems. "Higher" is not a level; "nutrient strength" is not a measured quantity; "better" is not a response variable; no time point is given. The phrase "we think … is better" also reveals a bias: the group expects a particular result.
  2. Operationalise the factor. Nutrient strength is conveniently expressed as electrical conductivity (EC) of the solution (Lesson 5.2). Choose levels that span a realistic range, e.g., EC 1.0, 1.6 and 2.2 mS cm⁻¹, maintained by topping up.
  3. Operationalise the response. For a leafy herb, shoot fresh mass per plant at harvest is the commercially relevant response; leaf dry-matter content and a sensory attribute could be secondary responses.
  4. Write the question. In sweet basil grown in deep-water culture at 24 °C and DLI 15 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, does the EC of the nutrient solution (1.0, 1.6 or 2.2 mS cm⁻¹) affect shoot fresh mass 35 days after transplanting?
  5. Write the hypotheses. With three levels, \(H_0: \mu_{1.0} = \mu_{1.6} = \mu_{2.2}\); \(H_1\): at least one mean differs. This is the structure of a one-way ANOVA (Lesson 3.3). A directional prediction ("fresh mass increases with EC") would be better tested as a regression on EC.

Common misconception

"The aim of our experiment is to prove that far-red increases yield." An experiment cannot prove a hypothesis, and aiming to confirm one invites unconscious bias in how plants are handled, measured and excluded. Write aims neutrally — "to test whether…", "to estimate how much…" — and let the data decide.

Variables, treatments and factorial designs

Every experiment involves four kinds of variables. Getting them straight is the fastest way to find the weaknesses in a design.

Table 1. Kinds of variables, illustrated with the far-red lettuce experiment.

Controlled variables deserve more attention than students usually give them. Consider container volume. A meta-analysis of pot-size experiments found that doubling the rooting volume increased plant biomass by 43 % on average (Poorter, Bühler et al., 2012). If your Kratky jars for one treatment are 2 L and the others 1 L because you ran out of jars, container size is confounded with treatment: you can no longer tell which of the two caused any difference. The same applies to seed batch, sowing date, and even the person doing the measuring. Poorter, Fiorani et al. (2012) give an excellent practical checklist for growing plants for experiments.

Controls

A control is the treatment level that serves as the reference for the others. In the spectrum experiment, the white LED is the control because it represents the standard practice against which a grower would judge far-red. Two further kinds are useful: a negative control is expected to show no response (e.g., plants handled exactly like the treated ones but without the active ingredient of a biostimulant), and a positive control is known to produce a response, and so demonstrates that your system is capable of detecting one (e.g., a nutrient-deficient solution that must produce visibly smaller plants). If the positive control shows nothing, the problem lies with your measurement, not with your treatment.

Factorial designs and interactions

When you have two factors — say spectrum (W or W+FR) and nutrient strength (EC 1.2 or 2.0 mS cm⁻¹) — the tempting approach is "one factor at a time": vary spectrum at one EC, then vary EC under one spectrum. A factorial design instead combines every level of every factor: 2 × 2 = 4 treatment combinations. It is more efficient (every plant contributes information about both factors) and it is the only way to discover an interaction: the situation in which the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other.

Linear model for a two-factor factorial experiment(3.1.2)
\[ y_{ijk} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j + (\alpha\beta)_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ijk}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ijk}\sim N(0,\sigma^2) \]

the response of replicate \(k\) receiving level \(i\) of factor A and level \(j\) of factor B equals the overall mean, plus the main effect of A, plus the main effect of B, plus an interaction term specific to that combination, plus random unit-to-unit variation with mean zero and variance sigma squared.

\(y_{ijk}\)response of unit \(k\) in cell \((i,j)\)g plant⁻¹fresh mass of jar 3 under W+FR at EC 2.0
\(\mu\)overall (grand) meang plant⁻¹150
\(\alpha_i\)main effect of level \(i\) of factor A (spectrum); \(\sum_i\alpha_i = 0\)g plant⁻¹±10
\(\beta_j\)main effect of level \(j\) of factor B (EC); \(\sum_j\beta_j = 0\)g plant⁻¹±5
\((\alpha\beta)_{ij}\)interaction: extra deviation of cell \((i,j)\) not explained by the two main effectsg plant⁻¹±3
\(\varepsilon_{ijk}\)residual (error): unit-to-unit variationg plant⁻¹SD ≈ 10–15
Follow the derivation: main effects and the interaction from four cell means
  1. In a 2 × 2 design, write the four population cell means as \(\mu_{11}, \mu_{12}, \mu_{21}, \mu_{22}\) (first index: spectrum W = 1, W+FR = 2; second index: EC 1.2 = 1, EC 2.0 = 2).
  2. The simple effect of far-red at low EC is \(\mu_{21}-\mu_{11}\); at high EC it is \(\mu_{22}-\mu_{12}\).
  3. The main effect of far-red is the average of the two simple effects: \(A = \tfrac12\left[(\mu_{21}-\mu_{11}) + (\mu_{22}-\mu_{12})\right]\). In the model this equals \(\alpha_2-\alpha_1\).
  4. The interaction is the difference between the simple effects: \(AB = (\mu_{22}-\mu_{12}) - (\mu_{21}-\mu_{11})\) — a "difference of differences". It is zero exactly when the far-red effect is the same at both EC levels.
  5. Rearranging shows that the interaction is symmetrical: \(AB = (\mu_{22}-\mu_{21}) - (\mu_{12}-\mu_{11})\), i.e., also the change in the EC effect caused by far-red.
  6. The constraints \(\sum\alpha_i = \sum\beta_j = 0\) (and the same for the interaction terms in every row and column) make the parameters unique; without them, a constant could be shifted freely between \(\mu\) and the effects.
Assumptions and validity

Effects are additive on the scale of measurement. Growth responses are often multiplicative (far-red adds 12 %, not 12 g), in which case an interaction on the gram scale may disappear on a logarithmic scale. The residuals are assumed independent, normally distributed and of equal variance in all cells — the assumptions checked in Lesson 3.3.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Simple effects of far-red. At EC 1.2: \(151-138 = 13\) g. At EC 2.0: \(183-152 = 31\) g.
  2. Main effect of far-red (average of the simple effects): \((13+31)/2 = 22\) g.

Experimental units, replication and pseudo-replication

The single most important question in any design is: what is the experimental unit? The experimental unit is the smallest entity to which a treatment is independently applied. The observational unit (or sampling unit) is the entity on which you measure. They are often the same — one lettuce in its own Kratky jar, with its own spectrum assignment — but frequently they are not.

Imagine you test two nutrient solutions in deep-water culture using two tanks, one per solution, each holding 12 lettuces. You harvest 24 plants and run a t-test with \(n = 12\) per group. The p-value is tiny. But the treatment (solution A or B) was applied to the tank, not to individual plants: all 12 plants in tank A share the same water temperature, the same dissolved oxygen, the same air stone, the same position under the lamp, and the same random history (an algal bloom, a pH drift, a Pythium infection). The 12 plants are subsamples of one experimental unit, and the correct sample size is \(n = 1\) per treatment — with which no statistical test is possible. Treating subsamples as independent replicates is called pseudo-replication (Hurlbert, 1984).

History

Stuart Hurlbert coined the term pseudoreplication in 1984 after examining 176 published ecological field experiments: 27 % of them — and 48 % of those that applied inferential statistics — had tested treatment effects with subsamples or non-independent replicates (Hurlbert, 1984). Decades later the problem persists in other disciplines, from neuroscience (Lazic, 2010) to cell culture and animal studies (Lazic et al., 2018). In controlled-environment research, the classic case is "one growth chamber per treatment": two chambers set to the same conditions can still produce different plants (Potvin & Tardif, 1988; Porter et al., 2015).

Why does the choice of unit matter so much? Because the p-value of a test depends on how much the treatment means are expected to wander by chance, and that depends on the variance between experimental units, not on the variance between plants inside one unit. The following equation makes this precise for the common case of \(m\) plants (subsamples) in each of \(n\) tanks per treatment.

Variance of a treatment mean with subsampling(3.1.3)
\[ \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\bar y\right) = \frac{\sigma_u^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma_e^2}{n\,m}, \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\bar y) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_u^2}{n} + \frac{\sigma_e^2}{n\,m}} \]

the variance of a treatment mean equals the between-unit variance divided by the number of experimental units, plus the within-unit (plant-to-plant) variance divided by the total number of plants. Adding plants per tank shrinks only the second term; only more tanks shrink the first.

\(\bar y\)treatment mean over all \(n m\) plantsg plant⁻¹150
\(\sigma_u^2\)variance between experimental units (tanks) receiving the same treatmentg²(10 g)²
\(\sigma_e^2\)variance between plants within a unitg²(15 g)²
\(n\)number of experimental units per treatment–3–8
\(m\)number of observational units (plants) per experimental unit–1–12
Follow the derivation
  1. Model plant \(k\) in tank \(j\) as \(y_{jk} = \mu + u_j + e_{jk}\), where the tank effect \(u_j\) has variance \(\sigma_u^2\) and the plant effect \(e_{jk}\) has variance \(\sigma_e^2\); all effects are independent.
  2. The mean of tank \(j\) is \(\bar y_j = \mu + u_j + \frac1m\sum_k e_{jk}\). Because the \(m\) plant effects are independent, \(\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac1m\sum_k e_{jk}\right) = \frac{m\sigma_e^2}{m^2} = \frac{\sigma_e^2}{m}\). Hence \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar y_j) = \sigma_u^2 + \sigma_e^2/m\).
  3. The treatment mean averages \(n\) independent tank means: \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar y) = \frac{1}{n}\left(\sigma_u^2 + \frac{\sigma_e^2}{m}\right) = \frac{\sigma_u^2}{n}+\frac{\sigma_e^2}{nm}\).
  4. As \(m\to\infty\) the variance approaches \(\sigma_u^2/n\), never zero. With \(n = 1\) tank we cannot even estimate \(\sigma_u^2\), because it is inseparable from the treatment effect.
  5. Pseudo-replication treats all \(nm\) plants as independent and estimates the variance as \((\sigma_u^2+\sigma_e^2)/(nm)\), which is smaller than the truth whenever \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\) and \(m > 1\). Standard errors are too small, confidence intervals too narrow, p-values too small.
Assumptions and validity

Balanced design (same \(m\) in every unit), independent random effects, constant variances. The correct analysis for this design either averages the plants within each tank and analyses the tank means (simple and always valid when balanced), or fits a mixed model with tank as a random effect.

Reveal the reasoning

Randomisation: protecting against what you cannot see

Suppose you have 18 Kratky jars on one shelf under one fixture, and three nutrient recipes to test — your standard recipe (S) and two alternatives (A and B) — six jars each. (A nutrient recipe can be given to each jar separately, so here the jar really is the experimental unit. A light spectrum cannot: see the complete design at the end of this lesson.) How should you decide which jar goes where? The natural instinct is to be tidy: S on the left, A in the middle, B on the right. Unfortunately, shelves are never uniform. PPFD is usually highest under the centre of a fixture and falls towards the edges; air near a door or fan is cooler; jars at the front are handled more often. If any of these gradients runs along the shelf, it is now perfectly confounded with recipe.

Randomisation assigns treatments to units by a chance mechanism, so that every possible assignment is equally likely. It achieves two things. First, it makes confounding with any unknown variable unlikely and, crucially, quantifiable: the probability that a gradient happens to favour one treatment is exactly what the p-value accounts for. Second, it justifies the statistical test itself — Fisher showed that randomisation creates the reference distribution against which the observed result is compared. How many assignments are there to choose from?

Number of distinct randomisations of a completely randomised design(3.1.4)
\[ R = \frac{N!}{\left(n!\right)^{t}}, \qquad N = t\,n \]

the number of different ways to allocate \(t\) treatments, each replicated \(n\) times, to \(N\) distinguishable positions equals \(N\) factorial divided by \(n\) factorial raised to the power \(t\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(R\)number of distinct treatment layouts–
\(N\)total number of experimental units (positions)–
\(t\)number of treatments–
\(n\)replicates per treatment–
Follow the derivation
  1. Label every jar with its treatment and line the \(N\) jars up: there are \(N!\) orderings of \(N\) distinguishable objects.
  2. Jars with the same treatment are interchangeable: swapping two S jars gives the same layout. Within each treatment there are \(n!\) such swaps.
  3. Divide by \(n!\) once per treatment: \(R = N!/(n!)^t\) (the multinomial coefficient).
  4. Example: \(t = 3\), \(n = 6\): \(R = 18!/(6!)^3 = 6.40\times10^{15}/3.73\times10^{8} = 17\,153\,136\). Your "tidy" layout is exactly one of them.
How to randomise in practice

Number the positions 1–18. Write the treatment list S, S, S, S, S, S, A, …, B (18 entries) and shuffle it with a random number generator — in a spreadsheet use =RAND() in a helper column and sort by it; in Python random.shuffle(); in R sample(). Record the seed or save the list. Haphazard is not random: "I just put them where it felt random" reliably produces layouts with human patterns (e.g., avoiding placing identical treatments side by side).

Figure 4 lets you test this on a virtual shelf. Eighteen jars stand in three rows and six columns under a fixture whose PPFD falls from about 290 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at the left end to about 200 at the right, with the front and back rows slightly darker still. You cannot see the gradient until you reveal it — exactly as in a real growth room before you measure a PPFD map. Choose a layout, run one experiment, then run 1000 and compare the distribution of the estimated recipe effect (B − S) with the truth.

Run the simulator with each design and you will find three things. The systematic layout is biased: because recipe B sits in the darkest columns, its estimated advantage over S is much smaller than the truth, and with no true effect at all it declares a "significant" recipe effect in most experiments — a false-positive rate far above the nominal 5 %. The completely randomised layout is unbiased on average, and its false-positive rate is about 5 %, but individual experiments scatter widely because the gradient adds to the random error. The blocked layout is unbiased and precise. That is the next idea.

Common misconception

"Randomisation is unnecessary because our growth room is uniform." No growth room is uniform, and you do not know the gradients you have not measured. Randomisation costs nothing and insures you against all of them — including the ones that appear during the experiment, such as a failing LED driver or a leaking jar.

Blocking, randomised complete blocks and Latin squares

Randomisation removes bias but not noise: if the shelf has a strong gradient, a randomised experiment is fair but imprecise. When you know (or suspect) where the variation comes from, you can do better. Blocking groups experimental units that are similar to one another — the three jars in one column of the shelf, the plants harvested on the same day, the compartments on the same rack level — into blocks, and then compares treatments within blocks. In a randomised complete block design (RCBD) every block contains every treatment exactly once, and the treatments are randomised separately within each block.

Typical blocking factors in controlled-environment experiments are:

  • Light gradients: PPFD falls towards the edges of a bench and between fixtures. Measure a PPFD map before you start (Lesson 4.1) and block along the gradient; the grow-room lighting lab shows how large edge effects can be.
  • Temperature and humidity gradients: upper shelves of a multi-tier rack are warmer because heat from the lamps below rises; air near a door, window, fan or dehumidifier is cooler or drier.
  • Edge effects: plants at the edge of a group receive more side light and have more space, so they often grow differently from plants in the middle. Guard plants (unmeasured plants around the edge) or blocking by edge/centre deal with this.
  • Time: sowing batches, repeated runs of the experiment, or harvest days. If harvesting takes two afternoons, harvest one complete block per session — never all of one treatment on day 1.
  • People and instruments: if two students measure, let each measure complete blocks, not complete treatments.
Randomised complete block model and sum-of-squares partition(3.1.5)
\[ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \underbrace{\sum_{i,j}(y_{ij}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\text{SS}_{T}} = \underbrace{b\sum_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\text{SS}_{\text{trt}}} + \underbrace{t\sum_j(\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2}_{\text{SS}_{\text{blocks}}} + \text{SS}_{E} \]

the response of treatment \(i\) in block \(j\) equals the grand mean plus a treatment effect plus a block effect plus random error; correspondingly, the total variation splits into a part due to treatments, a part due to blocks and a residual part. Blocking moves the block-to-block variation out of the error term.

SymbolMeaningUnit / df
\(y_{ij}\)response of the unit receiving treatment \(i\) in block \(j\)g plant⁻¹
\(\tau_i\), \(\beta_j\)treatment effect (\(\sum\tau_i=0\)) and block effect (\(\sum\beta_j=0\))g plant⁻¹

Follow the derivation of the partition
  1. Write each deviation from the grand mean as the sum of three pieces: \(y_{ij}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot} = (\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}) + (\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}) + (y_{ij}-\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot j}+\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})\). Expanding the right-hand side confirms the identity.
  2. Square both sides and sum over all \(i\) and \(j\). The squared pieces give \(b\sum_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2\) (each treatment deviation occurs in \(b\) blocks), \(t\sum_j(\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})^2\), and the residual sum of squares \(\text{SS}_E\).
  3. All three cross-products vanish because deviations from a mean sum to zero; e.g., \(\sum_{i,j}(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})(\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}) = \sum_i(\bar y_{i\cdot}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot})\cdot\sum_j(\bar y_{\cdot j}-\bar y_{\cdot\cdot}) = 0\cdot0\).
  4. Degrees of freedom add up in the same way: \(tb-1 = (t-1)+(b-1)+(t-1)(b-1)\).
  5. The treatment effect is tested with \(F = \text{MS}_{\text{trt}}/\text{MS}_E\) where \(\text{MS} = \text{SS}/\text{df}\) (the logic of \(F\) is developed in Lesson 3.3). Because block variation has been removed from \(\text{MS}_E\), the test is more sensitive.
Assumptions and validity

Treatment and block effects are additive (no treatment × block interaction): a treatment that is better in bright blocks is also better, by the same amount, in dark blocks. Blocks must be defined before treatments are assigned, from information that the treatment cannot affect. Blocking on something the treatment changes (e.g., "block by plant size at day 14") destroys the logic of the design.

Every SS is a sum of squared masses, g²; the partition adds quantities in the same unit ✓

How much does blocking gain? We can estimate what the error variance would have been if the same units had been used in a completely randomised design, and compare it with the error variance actually achieved.

Relative efficiency of blocking(3.1.6)
\[ \text{RE} = \frac{\hat\sigma^2_{\text{CRD}}}{\text{MS}_E} = \frac{(b-1)\,\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}} + b\,(t-1)\,\text{MS}_E}{(bt-1)\,\text{MS}_E} \]

the relative efficiency is the estimated error variance of an unblocked design divided by the error mean square of the blocked design; it tells you how many times more replicates a completely randomised design would need to be as precise.

SymbolMeaningUnit
RErelative efficiency of the RCBD versus a CRD–
\(\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}}\), \(\text{MS}_E\)block and error mean squares from the RCBD ANOVA tableg²

Follow the derivation
  1. Imagine a uniformity trial: the same units, no treatment effects. Then the treatment mean square would itself be an estimate of the error variance, so in a CRD the error sum of squares would contain the block SS, the error SS and \((t-1)\) degrees of freedom' worth of \(\text{MS}_E\).
  2. So \(\hat\sigma^2_{\text{CRD}} = \dfrac{\text{SS}_{\text{blocks}} + \text{SS}_E + (t-1)\text{MS}_E}{(b-1)+(b-1)(t-1)+(t-1)}\).
  3. Substitute \(\text{SS}_{\text{blocks}} = (b-1)\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}}\) and \(\text{SS}_E = (b-1)(t-1)\text{MS}_E\); the numerator becomes \((b-1)\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}} + b(t-1)\text{MS}_E\) and the denominator \(bt-1\).
  4. Divide by \(\text{MS}_E\). If blocks do not differ (\(\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}} \approx \text{MS}_E\)), RE ≈ 1: nothing gained, a few error degrees of freedom lost.
Assumptions and validity

Cochran and Cox's classical estimator. Some texts multiply RE by a small correction for the error degrees of freedom lost to blocks; with 10 or more error df the correction is below 5 %.

Worked example 4Was blocking by shelf worth it?

The spectrum experiment described at the end of this lesson was analysed as an RCBD with \(t = 3\) spectra and \(b = 6\) blocks (three rack shelves in each of two runs). The ANOVA table (Lesson 3.3) gives \(\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}} = 185.9\) g² with 5 df and \(\text{MS}_E = 40.3\) g² with 10 df. Calculate the relative efficiency and interpret it.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Numerator. \((b-1)\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}} + b(t-1)\text{MS}_E = 5\times185.9 + 6\times2\times40.3 = 929.5 + 483.6 = 1413.1\) g².
  2. Denominator. \((bt-1)\,\text{MS}_E = 17\times40.3 = 685.1\) g².
  3. Ratio. \(\text{RE} = 1413.1/685.1 = 2.06\).
  4. Interpret. Ignoring the shelves, the error variance would have been about \(1413.1/17 = 83\) g² instead of 40 g². A completely randomised design would have needed about 2.06 × 6 ≈ 12 compartments per spectrum to reach the same precision — blocking doubled the value of every compartment.
  5. Sanity check. \(\text{MS}_{\text{blocks}}\) is 4.6 times \(\text{MS}_E\), so the shelves genuinely differed (upper shelves warmer). If they had not, RE would be close to 1.

Latin squares: two gradients at once

Sometimes there are two crossing gradients — for example, temperature increases from the bottom to the top shelf of a rack, and light decreases from the wall side to the door side. A Latin square arranges \(t\) treatments in a \(t\times t\) grid so that every treatment appears exactly once in every row and every column (Figure 5). Row and column effects are both removed from the error. The price is rigidity: you need exactly \(t^2\) units, and a single 3 × 3 square leaves only \((t-1)(t-2) = 2\) error degrees of freedom — far too few. In practice, use several squares (e.g., one per run) or combine a Latin-square layout with RCBD analysis.

Watch out

Do not "block" on a variable that the treatment can change, and do not use a post-hoc block to rescue a disappointing result. Blocks are part of the design, decided and written down before the first seed is sown.

How many plants? Effect size, power and sample size

Every statistical test can go wrong in two ways (Table 3). A Type I error (false positive) rejects a true null hypothesis; its probability is \(\alpha\), the significance level you choose, conventionally 0.05. A Type II error (false negative) fails to reject a false null hypothesis; its probability is \(\beta\). The power of an experiment, \(1-\beta\), is the probability that it detects an effect of a given size if that effect really exists. A common target is 0.8 (80 %).

Table 3. The four possible outcomes of a hypothesis test.
\(H_0\) true (no real effect)\(H_0\) false (real effect exists)
Reject \(H_0\) ("significant")Type I error — probability \(\alpha\)Correct detection — probability \(1-\beta\) (power)
Do not reject \(H_0\)Correct — probability \(1-\alpha\)Type II error — probability \(\beta\)

Power depends on four quantities: the true size of the effect, the variability between experimental units, the number of units, and \(\alpha\). The first two are conveniently combined into a standardised effect size.

Standardised effect size (Cohen's d) from a relative difference and the CV(3.1.7)
\[ d = \frac{\mu_1-\mu_0}{\sigma} \;\approx\; \frac{\Delta_{\%}}{\text{CV}_{\%}}, \qquad \Delta_{\%} = 100\,\frac{\mu_1-\mu_0}{\mu_0},\quad \text{CV}_{\%} = 100\,\frac{\sigma}{\mu_0} \]

Cohen's d is the difference between two population means divided by the common standard deviation; if the difference is expressed as a percentage of the control mean and the variability as a coefficient of variation (also in percent), d is simply their ratio.

\(d\)standardised effect size–0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large (Cohen, 1988)

Where do the numbers come from?

The difference \(\mu_1-\mu_0\) should be the smallest effect of practical interest, not the effect you hope for. For a grower, a far-red fixture might only pay off if it increases yield by at least 10–15 %. The standard deviation comes from a pilot experiment, from your own previous runs, or from published experiments with similar systems — look for reported SDs (not SEs!) or convert SEs with \(\text{SD} = \text{SE}\sqrt{n}\) (Lesson 3.2). Cohen's labels "small, medium, large" were proposed for behavioural sciences; in well-controlled plant experiments, effects of \(d > 1\) are common.

\(\dfrac{\text{g}}{\text{g}}\) or \(\dfrac{\%}{\%}\) — dimensionless ✓
Sample size per group for a two-group comparison (normal approximation)(3.1.8)
\[ n \;\approx\; \frac{2\left(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\right)^2}{d^2} \]

the number of experimental units needed in each group is about two times the square of the sum of two standard normal quantiles — one set by the significance level, one by the desired power — divided by the square of the standardised effect size. Halving the effect size quadruples the sample size.

SymbolMeaningValue
\(n\)experimental units per groupround up
\(z_{1-\alpha/2}\)standard normal quantile for a two-sided test1.960 for \(\alpha = 0.05\); 2.576 for 0.01

\(d\)standardised effect size (Eq. 3.1.7)–
Follow the derivation
  1. With \(n\) units per group and SD \(\sigma\), the difference of the two sample means, \(D = \bar y_1-\bar y_0\), is approximately normal with mean \(\Delta = \mu_1-\mu_0\) and standard error \(\sigma\sqrt{2/n}\) (variances of independent means add).
  2. A two-sided test rejects \(H_0\) when \(|D|/(\sigma\sqrt{2/n}) > z_{1-\alpha/2}\).
  3. If the true difference is \(\Delta>0\), the statistic \(Z = D/(\sigma\sqrt{2/n})\) is normal with mean \(\delta = \Delta/(\sigma\sqrt{2/n}) = d\sqrt{n/2}\) and SD 1. Ignoring the negligible chance of rejecting in the wrong tail, power \(= P(Z > z_{1-\alpha/2}) = \Phi(\delta - z_{1-\alpha/2})\).
  4. Require power \(=1-\beta\): \(\delta - z_{1-\alpha/2} = z_{1-\beta}\), so \(d\sqrt{n/2} = z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\).
  5. Square and solve: \(n = 2(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2/d^2\).
  6. Because \(\sigma\) must be estimated from the data, the real test uses the \(t\) distribution, whose quantiles are larger. The exact calculation (noncentral \(t\), as in G*Power; Faul et al., 2007) therefore needs one or two more units than this formula; a handy correction is to add \(z_{1-\alpha/2}^2/4 \approx 1\).
Assumptions and validity

Two groups of equal size, a two-sided test, normally distributed responses with equal SDs, independent experimental units. For more than two groups (one-way ANOVA), the effect size is expressed as Cohen's \(f\) and the required \(n\) per group is somewhat larger; use the power tool in the statistics workbench or G*Power. Sample size calculations are only as good as the SD you put in — treat the result as an order of magnitude and add a margin for plant losses (10–20 %).

Quantiles and \(d\) are dimensionless, so \(n\) is a pure number ✓
Worked example 5How many Kratky jars do we need?

A group wants to compare two nutrient recipes in Kratky jars (one plant per jar). A 15 % difference in shoot fresh mass would matter to them. In last year's project, lettuce fresh mass had a coefficient of variation of 12 % between jars. Using \(\alpha = 0.05\) (two-sided) and a power of 0.80, how many jars per recipe are needed? What power would their planned 6 jars per recipe give?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Effect size (Eq. 3.1.7): \(d = 15/12 = 1.25\).
  2. Quantiles. \(z_{0.975} = 1.960\), \(z_{0.80} = 0.842\); their sum is 2.802 and its square 7.850.
  3. Normal approximation (Eq. 3.1.8): \(n = 2\times7.850/1.25^2 = 15.70/1.5625 = 10.05\).
  4. t correction. Add \(z_{0.975}^2/4 = 0.96\): \(n \approx 11.0\). The exact noncentral-t calculation (Figure 6) gives \(n = 12\) jars per recipe (power 0.83). Allowing ~10 % losses, plant 13–14 per recipe.
  5. Power with 6 jars. From Figure 6 (or G*Power), a two-sample t-test with \(n = 6\) per group and \(d = 1.25\) has power ≈ 0.50 — a coin flip. If the true effect is only 12 % (\(d = 1\)), power drops to 0.35.
  6. Sanity check. Halving the effect to 7.5 % (\(d = 0.63\)) would require about four times as many jars (≈ 41 per recipe) — the \(1/d^2\) law.
≈ 12 jars per recipe for 80 % power; with 6 jars the power is only about 50 %

Why worry about power, if a non-significant result can simply be reported as such? Because underpowered experiments are worse than they look. Most obviously, they miss real effects. Less obviously, the effects they do declare significant are systematically exaggerated: with low power, only the samples that happened to overestimate the effect cross the significance threshold (the "winner's curse"). Button et al. (2013) estimated that the median statistical power of studies in neuroscience was only about 21 %, and showed that this lowers the probability that a significant finding reflects a true effect. Plant science has the same incentives and the same risks (Kramer et al., 2016).

Common misconception

"A power of 0.8 means there is an 80 % probability that the null hypothesis is false." No — power is a property of the design assuming a specific true effect. It is the long-run fraction of such experiments that would reject \(H_0\). It says nothing about whether the effect exists.

The laboratory notebook

Your notebook is the legal and scientific record of what you did. Good practice, whether on paper or in an electronic lab notebook: date every entry (ISO format, 2026-10-02); write in ink and never erase — strike through errors so they stay readable; record what you did as you do it, including deviations from the plan, failures and surprises; note instrument IDs, calibration results and solution batch numbers; sketch the layout; and link every data file by name. The Swedish Research Council lists "You shall keep your research organised, for example through documentation and filing" among its basic rules of good research practice (Swedish Research Council, 2017).

Preregistration

A preregistration is a time-stamped document, written before data collection, that states the hypotheses, the design, the sample size and its justification, the primary response variable, the exclusion criteria and the analysis plan. It separates confirmatory tests (planned) from exploratory analyses (discovered in the data), both of which are legitimate as long as they are labelled honestly (Nosek et al., 2018). Researchers preregister on public registries such as the Open Science Framework; in this course, the one-page experimental plan you hand in during week 2 plays the same role. A preregistration for the project should answer:

  1. What is the research question, and what are \(H_0\) and \(H_1\)? One- or two-sided?
  2. What are the factor(s) and levels, the experimental unit, the blocks and the randomisation procedure?
  3. How many experimental units per treatment, and why (power calculation or practical limit, stated honestly)?
  4. What is the one primary response variable? Which are secondary?
  5. Which plants will be excluded, on what criteria (e.g., "plants that die before DAT 7 are replaced; later losses are recorded and excluded")?
  6. Which statistical analysis will test the hypothesis (e.g., RCBD ANOVA followed by Tukey HSD, \(\alpha = 0.05\))?

Safety and ethics

A student growing experiment combines mains electricity, water, intense light, chemicals, food and — in the sensory study — people. Each deserves a short risk assessment in your plan.

  • Electricity and water. Plug pumps, air pumps and LED drivers into an outlet protected by a residual-current device (RCD, jordfelsbrytare, 30 mA). Keep power strips above the water level, use drip loops on cables, prefer low-voltage (12/24 V) pumps, and never modify mains wiring yourself.
  • Light. Horticultural LEDs are far brighter than room lighting, and their blue component poses a photochemical "blue-light hazard" to the retina. Lamps are classified by the photobiological safety standard IEC 62471 into risk groups (exempt, RG1 low, RG2 moderate, RG3 high). Do not look directly into fixtures; dim or switch off lamps while working close to them, and remember that far-red and UV are barely visible even when intense.
  • Chemicals. Fertiliser concentrates, acids used to lower pH (phosphoric or nitric acid) and bases (potassium hydroxide) are irritants or corrosive. Read the safety data sheet, wear gloves and eye protection, add acid to water (never the reverse), and label every container with contents and date.
  • Food safety. If you will taste your produce, use food-grade containers and substrates, drinking-quality water and only food-grade inputs; wash hands and tools, and keep harvested plants cool. Plants from the experiment that received non-approved substances must not be eaten.
  • People in the sensory study. Participation must be voluntary and based on informed consent, participants may withdraw at any time without giving a reason, allergies and dietary restrictions must be asked about before tasting (some novel foods, for example insect-based ones, can cause reactions in people allergic to crustaceans or house-dust mites), and data must be anonymised and minimised in line with the GDPR. Under the Swedish Ethical Review Act (2003:460), work carried out by students within first- or second-cycle education is not defined as research and needs no formal ethical review — but the ethical principles still apply, and your teacher must approve the protocol. See Lesson 13.3.
  • Environment. Spent nutrient solution is a fertiliser: dilute it and use it on plants or dispose of it as instructed locally; never pour concentrates or acids down the drain.
Research integrity starts in the design

The Swedish Research Council's eight basic rules of good research practice begin with "You shall tell the truth about your research" and include openly accounting for methods and results and keeping research organised (Swedish Research Council, 2017; revised guidance: Swedish Research Council, 2025). Recording every excluded plant and every deviation from the plan is how you live up to them. Lesson 3.4 covers research integrity and the Swedish legal framework in detail.

A complete design: does far-red make lettuce grow better?

We now assemble everything into a design that a student group could run in the course project. The main complication is instructive: a light spectrum is applied to a whole light compartment, not to a single jar. All jars under one fixture share the same spectrum — and the same fixture, the same local temperature and the same position — so the compartment, not the jar, is the experimental unit (Section 3.1.4). Twelve jars under one white fixture and twelve under one far-red fixture would be the tank problem of Figure 3 again.

Worked example 6The full experimental plan

Write a complete, defensible design for testing whether far-red light increases lettuce fresh mass, using a rack of three shelves that can be divided into three compartments each, and time for two successive runs of 35 days. Justify each choice.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Question and hypotheses. In butterhead lettuce in Kratky jars at PAR PPFD 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, 16 h d⁻¹ (DLI 14.4 mol m⁻² d⁻¹), does the spectrum (W, RB or W+FR) affect shoot fresh mass at DAT 35? \(H_0: \mu_{\text{W}} = \mu_{\text{RB}} = \mu_{\text{FR}}\); \(H_1\): not all equal. Planned comparison of main interest: W+FR versus W.
  2. Treatments. Three spectra at equal PAR PPFD. W+FR adds about 50 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of far-red on top of the white light. Design decision: this means W+FR plants receive more photons in total (400–750 nm). The alternative — keeping 400–750 nm photons equal by replacing some white light with far-red — tests a different question (spectral quality at equal photon flux). Both are legitimate; the plan must say which one, because the answer may differ (Zhen & Bugbee's extended-PAR concept, Lesson 4.1).
  3. Experimental unit. The compartment (its own fixture). The 4 jars in it are subsamples; their mean fresh mass is the response value of the compartment (Eq. 3.1.3). 4 jars rather than 1 make each compartment value more precise and provide spares if a plant dies.
  4. Blocks. Shelf level within run — upper shelves are warmer, and the second run may differ in seed batch and season. \(b = 6\) blocks, each containing all three spectra: an RCBD with \(n = 6\) compartments per spectrum.
  5. Randomisation. For every shelf of every run, randomly permute (W, RB, FR) over the left, middle and right positions with a random number generator (seed recorded). Randomly assign seedlings (of similar size) to jars and jars to compartments.
  6. Power. With between-compartment SD ≈ 10 g for compartment means (pilot guess) and a smallest interesting difference of 22 g (15 % of 148 g), \(d \approx 2.2\). With \(n = 6\) per spectrum, the power of a two-group comparison is ≈ 0.93; with only one run (\(n = 3\)) it would be ≈ 0.53. Two runs are therefore essential.
  7. Controlled variables. Cultivar and seed lot, jar volume (1 L), one nutrient solution batch per run, EC 1.6 mS cm⁻¹ and pH 5.8 at start, photoperiod, PAR PPFD (adjusted by dimming after the PPFD map), air temperature target 21 °C.
  8. Measurements as in Table 4; fresh mass is the single primary response.
  9. Analysis plan. RCBD ANOVA on compartment means (spectrum + block), followed by Tukey HSD; residual diagnostics; effect sizes with 95 % confidence intervals. These are exactly the analyses of Lesson 3.3, which uses data from this design.
  10. If resources are smaller. With only two grow tents, run three or more successive runs, rotating spectra between tents (tent and run become blocks), or choose a factor that can be applied per jar (EC, substrate, cultivar) so that the jar is the experimental unit.

Key terms

Null hypothesis (H₀)
A precise statement of "no effect" (e.g., equal population means) that a statistical test attempts to reject.
Alternative hypothesis (H₁)
The statement accepted if H₀ is rejected; two-sided (a difference in either direction) or directional (one-sided).
Factor and level
A factor is a deliberately manipulated independent variable; its levels are the values it takes (e.g., spectrum: W, RB, W+FR).
Confounding
A situation in which an uncontrolled variable varies together with the treatment, so that their effects cannot be separated.
Factorial design
A design in which every level of each factor is combined with every level of every other factor, allowing main effects and interactions to be estimated.
Interaction
The effect of one factor depends on the level of another; in a 2 × 2 design, the difference between the two simple effects.
Experimental unit
The smallest entity to which a treatment is independently applied; the basis of the correct sample size n.
Observational unit
The entity on which a measurement is made (e.g., one plant or one leaf); may be a subsample of the experimental unit.
Pseudo-replication
Testing treatment effects using subsamples or non-independent observations as if they were independent experimental units (Hurlbert, 1984).
Randomisation
Assigning treatments to experimental units by a chance mechanism so that every allocation is equally likely.
Randomised complete block design (RCBD)
A design in which units are grouped into homogeneous blocks, each block contains every treatment once, and treatments are randomised within blocks.
Latin square
A t × t arrangement in which each treatment appears once in each row and once in each column, controlling two crossing gradients.
Statistical power
The probability that a test rejects H₀ when a specified effect truly exists (1 − β).
Cohen's d
A standardised effect size: the difference between two means divided by the standard deviation.
Preregistration
A time-stamped plan of hypotheses, design, sample size and analysis written before data collection.
