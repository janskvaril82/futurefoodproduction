Writing the laboratory report

An experiment is finished only when someone else can understand it, judge it and repeat it. The report is where 35 days of growing, a few hundred measurements and an afternoon of statistics become a scientific argument: this is what we asked, this is exactly what we did, this is what we found, and this is how far the evidence goes. This lesson shows how scientists build that argument — the IMRaD structure, figures and tables that communicate, statistics reported so that readers can check them — and how to do it honestly: referencing, plagiarism, generative AI, open data and the Swedish framework for research integrity. It ends with an annotated report of the far-red experiment of Lessons 3.1–3.3 that you can use as a template for your own.

What you will be able to do

  • Structure a laboratory report in IMRaD form and write an informative title, a structured abstract and an introduction that ends in a testable aim (LO4).
  • Write a methods section from which another group could repeat a controlled-environment plant experiment, including design, environment and statistical analysis (LO4, LO5).
  • Present results in figures and tables that pass a quality checklist, with correct SI units, sensible precision and self-contained captions (LO4).
  • Report statistical results in APA style with effect sizes and confidence intervals, and check reported statistics for internal consistency (LO4).
  • Write a discussion that compares your results quantitatively with the literature and states limitations honestly (LO4).
  • Reference sources in APA 7 style, avoid plagiarism, use generative AI transparently, share data according to the FAIR principles and act within the Swedish framework for good research practice (LO4, LO5).

The report as a scientific argument: IMRaD

A laboratory report is not a diary of what happened in the growth room; it is an argument from evidence, written for a reader who was not there. Your examiner is one such reader, but so are the group that will repeat your experiment next year, a grower wondering whether far-red LEDs pay off, and you yourself six months from now. What all of them need is the same: the question, enough detail to judge and repeat the work, the results with their uncertainty, and an honest account of what the results mean. The report closes the empirical cycle of Lesson 3.1 (Figure 1 there): conclusions that are never communicated, or communicated so vaguely that nobody can check them, add nothing to knowledge.

This is not a formality. In a survey of 1 576 researchers, more than 70 % had tried and failed to reproduce another scientist's experiment, and more than half had failed to reproduce one of their own (Baker, 2016). Chalmers and Glasziou (2009) estimated that about 85 % of the investment in biomedical research is wasted — through poorly chosen questions, inadequate designs, results that are never published and reports too incomplete to be used. Good reporting is the cheapest of all remedies.

Almost every empirical paper in the natural sciences follows the same four-part structure, IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion. It answers four questions in the order a sceptical reader asks them — Why? How? What? So what? In leading medical journals the format appeared in the 1940s, was used by 80 % of research articles in the 1970s and had become the only structure for original papers by the 1980s (Sollaci & Pereira, 2004). Its strength is predictability: a reader who wants the sample size looks in Methods, one who wants the effect looks in Results, and neither has to read the whole report to find it.

Write in the order that makes writing easy

Few scientists write a report from the first word to the last. A practical order is: figures and tables first (they are your results), then Methods (you know exactly what you did), Results (describe the figures), Discussion, Introduction (now you know what needs introducing), and finally the abstract and title. Mensh and Kording (2017) summarise good structure as "context–content–conclusion" at every scale: the whole report, each section and each paragraph opens by saying why the reader should care, delivers its content and closes with its message. Within sentences, Gopen and Swan (1990) showed that readers expect the subject and its verb to stand close together, familiar information at the start of a sentence and the new, important information at the end — the "stress position".

Clear writing is also measurable, if crudely. The most widely used readability index counts only sentence length and word length.

Flesch reading ease(3.4.1)
\[ \text{RE} = 206.835 - 1.015\,\frac{N_{\text{words}}}{N_{\text{sentences}}} - 84.6\,\frac{N_{\text{syllables}}}{N_{\text{words}}} \]

the reading-ease score starts at about 207 and loses about one point for every extra word per sentence and about 85 points for every extra syllable per word; higher scores mean easier text.

Where does the formula come from?
  1. Flesch (1948) asked readers to answer comprehension questions on graded reading passages and recorded which texts were understood by which school grades.
  2. He then regressed the comprehension scores on simple, countable text properties. Sentence length and word length (in syllables) explained most of the variation; the coefficients 1.015 and 84.6 and the constant 206.835 are the fitted regression coefficients, scaled so that the index runs roughly from 0 to 100.
  3. It is therefore a simple linear regression of the kind you met in Lesson 3.3 — with the same caveats: it predicts an average reader's difficulty and is only valid for the kind of text it was fitted to.
Assumptions and validity

The index knows nothing about meaning, logic or technical vocabulary: "photosynthetically active radiation" costs 13 syllables however necessary it is. Use it as a warning light for sentences that have grown too long, never as a target. Scientific text scoring 20–40 is normal; a methods paragraph scoring below 10 usually hides sentences of 40 words or more that should be split.

Both ratios are counts per count — the score is dimensionless ✓

Splitting a 28-word sentence of long words (1.75 syllables per word; RE = 30) into two sentences of 16 words and choosing slightly shorter words (1.6 syllables) raises the score to 55 — from "difficult" to "fairly difficult" — without losing a single technical term.

Title, abstract and introduction

The title and abstract are read by a hundred times more people than the rest of the report; the introduction decides whether they keep reading. All three should be written, or at least rewritten, last.

The title

A good title is specific (organism, system, factor, response), informative and short — usually 10–20 words. A descriptive title names the topic ("Effects of LED spectrum on lettuce"); a declarative title states the finding ("Adding far-red to white LED light increased lettuce fresh mass by 13 %"). In a survey of medical articles, short titles and titles that described the results were cited more often (Paiva et al., 2012). Avoid empty openings ("A study of…", "Investigation into…"), unexplained abbreviations and questions whose answer you already know.

The abstract

The abstract is a complete, stand-alone miniature of the report in 150–250 words: background (one sentence), aim, methods (design, experimental units and \(n\), key conditions), results (the main effects with numbers — estimates, uncertainty and test statistics) and conclusion (what the result means, and its main limitation). It contains no citations, no undefined abbreviations and nothing that is not in the report.

The introduction

The introduction funnels the reader from a broad context to your specific question. Swales (1990) described the typical research introduction as three "moves": establish a territory (why the topic matters, what is known — every claim supported by a citation), establish a niche (what is not known, contradictory or untested) and occupy the niche (the aim, the hypothesis and, briefly, the approach). End with the aim and hypothesis exactly as preregistered in your experimental plan (Lesson 3.1); a reader should be able to predict the structure of your Results from them.

Methods: writing for reproducibility

The Methods section passes one test: could a competent group repeat your experiment from this section alone, and would a critical reader be able to judge whether the design supports your conclusions? Plant responses depend on dozens of conditions that seem trivial until they differ between laboratories — cultivar, seed lot, container size, light history, the height at which PPFD was measured (Poorter et al., 2012). Guidelines for reporting environmental conditions in greenhouses and growth rooms therefore specify which variables to measure, where, how often and with which instruments (Both et al., 2015), and the MIAPPE standard lists the minimum information that makes plant phenotyping data reusable (Papoutsoglou et al., 2020). Write Methods in the past tense and in prose, grouped under sub-headings; either the active ("We randomised…") or the passive voice ("Spectra were randomised…") is acceptable if used consistently. Report measured values rather than set-points ("air temperature was 21.3 ± 0.8 °C", not "the room was set to 21 °C"), give derived quantities together with how you derived them (DLI from PPFD and photoperiod), and name the version of every software you used — results can change between versions. What you do not need is a click-by-click protocol: include every detail that could plausibly affect the result, and nothing that could not.

Deviations are data, not embarrassments

Plants die, loggers fail, a run is shortened by a holiday. Reporting deviations from the preregistered plan — and what you did about them — is part of good research practice; hiding them is not (Swedish Research Council, 2025). Readers can judge a transparent imperfect experiment; they cannot judge an apparently perfect one that did not happen as described.

Results: figures, tables and numbers that communicate

The Results section presents the findings in the order of the questions posed in the Introduction, main result first, without interpretation beyond what the reader needs to follow the argument. Each finding has one best vehicle. Use text for one to three numbers ("fresh mass was 18.9 g higher under W+FR"), a table when readers need exact values or many variables, and a figure when the message is a pattern — a difference, a trend, a distribution. Never present the same numbers twice, and refer to every figure and table in the text, in order.

Figures are where most reports fail. Rougier et al. (2014) formulated ten rules for better figures — know your audience and your message, adapt to the medium, avoid defaults and chartjunk, use colour deliberately, never mislead — and Weissgerber et al. (2015) showed how bar graphs of small samples hide the data that readers need to judge them (Lesson 3.2). Figure 2 turns these rules into a checklist you can test.

Captions must let a reader understand the figure without reading the text: what is shown (response, treatments, time), what the symbols and error bars represent, \(n\) as the number of experimental units, and what any letters or asterisks mean. The caption goes below a figure and above a table. Tables are built for reading down columns: one variable per column, the unit in the column header rather than in every cell, numbers right-aligned at a consistent precision, horizontal rules only (above and below the header, at the bottom) and footnotes for abbreviations and statistics (Table 3).

Numbers and units

Follow the SI conventions (BIPM, 2019): a space between number and unit (148 g, 21 °C, 95 %), upright unit symbols without plurals or full stops, products of units separated by a space (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, never "µmol/m²/s"), and the decimal point in English text even if your spreadsheet uses the Swedish decimal comma. Separate thousands with a thin space (1 576), write ranges as 12–15 g, and never begin a sentence with a numeral. Precision follows the uncertainty, not the calculator (Lesson 3.2).

How many decimal places to report(3.4.2)
\[ k = 1-\left\lfloor \log_{10} u \right\rfloor \;+\; \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if the first significant digit of } u \text{ is } 1\\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

round the uncertainty u (an SD, SE or confidence half-width) to two significant figures — three if it starts with a 1 — and report the estimate to the same number of decimal places k; the floor of the base-10 logarithm of u tells you in which decimal position its first significant digit lies.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(u\)the uncertainty you report (SD, SE or CI half-width)unit of the data
\(k\)number of decimal places for both the uncertainty and the estimate (0 if negative)–
\(\lfloor\cdot\rfloor\)floor: the largest integer not greater than the argument–
Follow the reasoning
  1. For \(u = 8.65\) g, \(\log_{10}8.65 = 0.94\) and \(\lfloor0.94\rfloor = 0\): the first significant digit is in the units position, so two significant figures need one decimal: \(k = 1\) → 8.7 g, and the mean 166.416… g becomes 166.4 g.
  2. For \(u = 0.59\) g, \(\log_{10}0.59 = -0.23\), floor −1, so \(k = 2\) → 0.59 g and a mean of 7.60 g.
  3. For \(u = 11.2\) g the first digit is 1; a two-figure rounding (11 g) is coarse — for uncertainties starting with 1 it can be off by up to 5 % — so a third figure is kept: \(k = 0 + 1 = 1\) → 11.2 g and 147.6 g.
  4. The Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement recommends at most two significant digits for uncertainties (JCGM, 2008); keeping a third for a leading 1 is a common refinement (Lesson 3.2).
Assumptions and validity

A reporting convention, not a law of nature. Keep full precision in all intermediate calculations and in the data file; round only in the report. Test statistics are usually given to two decimals (t = 3.26) and p-values to two or three (Section 3.4.5), whatever \(k\) is.

\(k\) is a count of decimal places — dimensionless; the rounded estimate keeps the unit of the data ✓

Reporting statistics so that readers can check them

A statistical result is reported completely when a reader can see what was tested, recompute the p-value from the test statistic and its degrees of freedom, and judge the size and precision of the effect. The SAMPL guidelines for basic statistical reporting ask for exactly this: the test and why it was chosen, whether its assumptions were checked, the estimate with a confidence interval, and the exact p-value — never only "p < 0.05" or "NS" (Lang & Altman, 2015). The course follows the style of the Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association, 7th edition (APA, 2020), the most widely used convention in the life and social sciences:

  • Statistical symbols in italics — t, F, p, M, SD, n, r, d — Greek letters upright (α, η²).
  • Degrees of freedom in parentheses after the symbol: t(9.40), F(2, 10), χ²(2, N = 18).
  • Exact p-values to two or three decimals (p = .033, p = .41); below .001 write p < .001. A p-value is never zero, so never write p = .000.
  • No leading zero for quantities that cannot exceed 1 (p = .009, r = .82, ηp² = .90), but a leading zero for those that can (d = 0.85, a slope of 0.35 g cm⁻²).
  • Test statistics to two decimals; confidence intervals in square brackets: 95 % CI [5.9, 31.9] g. (APA writes "95% CI"; this course keeps the SI space before the per cent sign — choose one convention and apply it throughout.)
  • Every inferential statistic comes with descriptive statistics (mean ± SD and \(n\), or means with CIs) and an effect size.

The classic example of the style is t(18) = 2.31, p = .033, d = 1.03: a t statistic on 18 degrees of freedom (two groups of ten), its exact two-sided p-value and Cohen's d. Table 4 contrasts typical mistakes with correct versions.

Because the p-value is a deterministic function of the statistic and its degrees of freedom, a reader can check whether the three numbers in a report agree. When Nuijten et al. (2016) automated this check with their statcheck software on decades of psychology articles, about half of the articles that reported significance tests contained at least one p-value inconsistent with its statistic and degrees of freedom, and about one in eight contained an inconsistency large enough to change the statistical conclusion. Most were typing and rounding errors — exactly the kind you can catch in your own report.

Checking that a reported p-value matches its statistic(3.4.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} p_{t} = 2\left[1-F_{t,\nu}\!\left(|t|\right)\right],\qquad p_{F} = 1-F_{F,\,\nu_1,\,\nu_2}(F) \\[6pt] \text{consistent if } \left|p_{\text{recomputed}}-p_{\text{reported}}\right| \le \tfrac{1}{2}\,10^{-k} \end{gathered} \]

recompute the two-sided p-value of a t statistic from the t distribution, or the p-value of an F statistic from the upper tail of the F distribution; the reported value is consistent if it equals the recomputed one after rounding to the k decimals that were reported.

Follow the reasoning
  1. For a two-sided t-test, the p-value is the area in both tails beyond \(|t|\) (Lesson 3.3, Eq. 3.3.4); for an F test, the upper-tail area beyond F.
  2. Reported statistics are rounded (t to two decimals, p to three), so exact agreement is not expected. Recompute p from the rounded statistic and accept the report if the difference is within half a unit of the last reported decimal of p (with a little extra tolerance when t itself was rounded coarsely).
  3. A report of "\(p < .001\)" is consistent if the recomputed value is below .001.
  4. A decision inconsistency — reported \(p < .05\), recomputed \(p \ge .05\) or the reverse — is the serious case, because the conclusion changes.
Assumptions and validity

The check can only detect inconsistencies among the reported numbers; it cannot detect a wrong test (for example, an unpaired test on paired data) whose three numbers agree perfectly. For one-sided tests the reported p is half the two-sided value — which must then be stated in the Methods.

Recovering spread and effect size from reported statistics(3.4.4)
\[ s = \text{SE}\,\sqrt{n},\qquad \text{SE} = \frac{U-L}{2\,t_{1-\alpha/2,\,\nu}},\qquad d = t\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}} \]

  2. A t-based CI is \(\bar x\pm t^*\,\text{SE}\), so its width is \(U-L = 2t^*\,\text{SE}\).

One more check works for any variable measured on an integer scale — hedonic scores from 1 to 9, numbers of leaves, correct answers in a triangle test. Brown and Heathers (2017) called it the GRIM test (granularity-related inconsistency of means) and found impossible means in a substantial fraction of the published articles to which it could be applied.

The GRIM test for means of integer data(3.4.5)
\[ \bar x = \frac{T}{n},\quad T\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \min_{T\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\frac{T}{n}-\bar x_{\text{reported}}\right| \le \tfrac12\,10^{-k} \]

if every observation is an integer, their sum T is an integer and the mean can only take values T divided by n; a reported mean with k decimals is possible only if some integer total, divided by n, rounds to it.

\(T\)sum of the integer observationsscale points

Follow the reasoning
  1. With 12 panellists scoring 1–9, the total \(T\) is an integer between 12 and 108, so the mean moves in steps of \(1/12 = 0.0833\).
  2. A reported mean of 6.47 implies \(T = 6.47\times12 = 77.64\) — not an integer. The attainable neighbours are \(77/12 = 6.42\) and \(78/12 = 6.50\); 6.47 is impossible.
  3. The test works only when steps are wider than the reporting precision, i.e., for \(n < 10^{k}\) (fewer than 100 observations for two decimals) — exactly the situation of a student sensory panel.
  4. An inconsistency proves an error somewhere (a typing error, a wrong n, an undisclosed exclusion); it does not prove misconduct.

Worked example 3Peer-checking a sensory result

In a draft you are asked to review, a group writes: "Twelve panellists tasted both seaweed-crisp recipes. Liking of recipe A was 6.47 ± 1.2 (mean ± SD, 9-point hedonic scale), significantly higher than recipe B (t(10) = 2.10, p = .03)." Check the report with Eqs. 3.4.3 and 3.4.5 and the design.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. GRIM: 12 integer scores give means in steps of 1/12. \(6.47\times12 = 77.64\) is not an integer; the possible neighbouring means are 6.42 and 6.50. The reported mean is impossible — a typing error, a wrong n or an undisclosed exclusion.

Discussion and conclusions: how far does the evidence go?

The Discussion turns results into knowledge. A robust structure, paragraph by paragraph, is: (1) the answer to the research question, with the effect size and its uncertainty; (2) comparison with previous studies — quantitatively, not "in agreement with the literature"; (3) mechanisms that could explain the result, separating what your data show from what you infer; (4) limitations; (5) implications for practice and research, and what should be done next. The Conclusions restate the main answer in two or three sentences and add nothing new.

Limitations deserve a precise vocabulary. Internal validity asks whether the design supports a causal conclusion within the experiment — confounding, missing randomisation, pseudo-replication (Lesson 3.1). External validity asks how far the result generalises — to other cultivars, systems, light levels, seasons. Statistical conclusion validity concerns power, the width of the CI and multiplicity (Lesson 3.3). Measurement validity concerns what your instruments really measured — fresh mass depends on the water status at harvest, and a quantum sensor has spectral errors under far-red-rich LEDs (Lesson 4.1). A good limitation paragraph says what the problem is, in which direction it could bias the result, and how large that bias could plausibly be.

Comparing your effect with the literature quantitatively requires a common scale. For yields, the natural one is the ratio of treatment to control, because growth effects are multiplicative and the ratio is independent of the size of the plants in each study.

Relative effect and its confidence interval (log-ratio method)(3.4.6)
\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta_{\%} = 100\left(\frac{\bar x_T}{\bar x_C}-1\right),\qquad \operatorname{SE}\!\left[\ln\frac{\bar x_T}{\bar x_C}\right] \approx \sqrt{\left(\frac{\text{SE}_T}{\bar x_T}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\text{SE}_C}{\bar x_C}\right)^2} \\[6pt] 95\,\%\ \text{CI:}\quad 100\left[\exp\!\left(\ln\frac{\bar x_T}{\bar x_C}\pm t^*\operatorname{SE}\right)-1\right] \end{gathered} \]

the relative effect is the treatment mean divided by the control mean, minus one, in per cent; its uncertainty is computed on the logarithmic scale, where the standard error of the log ratio is approximately the square root of the sum of the squared relative standard errors; the interval is then transformed back.

Follow the derivation
  1. Ratios have skewed sampling distributions, but their logarithms are approximately normal: \(\ln(\bar x_T/\bar x_C) = \ln\bar x_T - \ln\bar x_C\).
  2. By the delta method, \(\operatorname{Var}(\ln\bar x)\approx\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)/\bar x^2 = (\text{SE}/\bar x)^2\): the SE of a log mean is the relative SE of the mean.
  3. The two means are independent, so the variances add (as in Eq. 3.3.7), and a CI on the log scale is the estimate ± \(t^*\) SE.
  4. Back-transforming with \(\exp\) gives an interval for the ratio that is asymmetric around the point estimate — as it should be for a quantity that cannot fall below −100 %.

Positive means with small relative SEs (below about 20 %). For blocked designs, the paired or RCBD analysis gives a narrower interval; use the design-based SE when you have the raw data. When comparing studies, check that responses (fresh or dry mass, per plant or per m²) and harvest ages match.

Common misconception

"Our result was not significant, so the experiment failed." A well-designed experiment with a clear answer — even "the effect, if any, is smaller than 5 %" — is a success. Plagiarism

Plagiarism is "using other people's work or ideas without giving proper credit to the original source" (ALLEA, 2023). It includes copying text, figures, data or code without acknowledgement; patchwriting — keeping another author's sentence structure and swapping a few words; translating a source without citing it; re-using your own earlier text without saying so (self-plagiarism); and submitting text generated by someone — or something — else as your own. Universities use text-matching software, but the definition does not depend on whether software detects it.

Generative AI: a tool you remain responsible for

Large language models can polish grammar, explain a statistical concept, suggest code or summarise a paper — and they can also invent plausible references, misstate results and reproduce other people's text.   3. pay particular attention to privacy, confidentiality and intellectual property when sharing information with AI tools — for example, do not upload other people's unpublished work or personal data;

Science works only if readers can trust that what is reported was actually done, measured and found. The European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity rests this trust on four principles: reliability in the quality of research (design, methods, analysis, use of resources), honesty in developing, undertaking, reviewing, reporting and communicating research transparently, fairly, fully and without bias, respect for colleagues, research participants, animals, society, ecosystems and the environment, and accountability for research from idea to publication (ALLEA, 2023). The Swedish Research Council's Good research practice 2024 builds on these principles and explains the Swedish rules (Swedish Research Council, 2025).

Since 1 January 2020, the Swedish Act on responsibility for good research practice and the examination of research misconduct (Lag 2019:504) defines research misconduct as "a serious deviation from good research practice in the form of fabrication, falsification or plagiarism that is committed intentionally or through gross negligence when planning, conducting or reporting research" (translation from Swedish Research Council, 2025). Fabrication is making up data or results; falsification is manipulating materials, equipment or processes, or changing, omitting or suppressing data or results without justification; plagiarism is using others' work or ideas without credit (ALLEA, 2023). Suspected misconduct in research covered by the Act is investigated by the Swedish National Board for Assessment of Research Misconduct (Npof); universities handle other deviations from good research practice themselves. Student coursework is not research in the sense of the Act — deception in your report falls under the Higher Education Ordinance instead — but the principles are the same.

Outright fabrication is rare but not negligible: in a meta-analysis of surveys, about 2 % of scientists admitted to having fabricated, falsified or modified data at least once, and up to about a third admitted other questionable research practices (Fanelli, 2009). Those grey-zone practices do more damage in total, and Good research practice 2024 names many of them as deviations: re-publishing one's own text without citation, manipulating authorship, hiding the use of AI, citing selectively, withholding data without acceptable reasons, and "misusing statistics, for example to incorrectly or improperly indicate statistical significance" (Swedish Research Council, 2025) — the p-hacking of Lesson 3.3.

Open science and FAIR data

Open science makes research outputs — publications, data, code, methods — available for others to check and reuse (UNESCO, 2021). For data, the guiding standard is the FAIR principles: data should be Findable (a persistent identifier and rich metadata), Accessible (retrievable through an open protocol, with metadata available even if the data are restricted), Interoperable (formal, shared formats and vocabularies) and Reusable (a clear licence, detailed provenance and community standards) (Wilkinson et al., 2016). FAIR does not mean "public at any price": the rule is "as open as possible, as closed as necessary". Sweden's national goal is that research data from publicly funded research should be openly available, following FAIR, by 2026, within national guidelines for open science that the National Library of Sweden coordinates towards 2030 (National Library of Sweden, n.d.). Your project data are a small but real instance: the grow log and harvest data can be fully open; the sensory data contain personal data and must be anonymised or kept closed under the GDPR.

Peer review

Before a paper is published, editors send it to independent experts who judge whether the methods support the conclusions and suggest improvements. Review may be single-anonymous (reviewers know the authors), double-anonymous or open. Its value depends on reviewers who are competent, constructive, timely and fair; the Committee on Publication Ethics asks reviewers to keep manuscripts confidential, declare competing interests and judge the work, not the authors (COPE Council, 2017) — and the ERA guidelines add that a manuscript under review must not be uploaded to a generative AI tool (European Commission, 2026). In the course you will review another group's report. A useful review has four parts: a two-sentence summary of what the report claims, major issues (design, analysis or interpretation problems that affect the conclusions), minor issues (clarity, figures, references) and a recommendation. Write every comment so that the authors know what to do: "The df in the t-test do not match the paired design (12 panellists → 11 df); please re-run it as a paired test" helps, "The statistics are wrong" does not. Worked example 3 is a model of the checking half of a review.

Key idea

Integrity is not a separate chapter that begins after the science is done. The habits that make an experiment trustworthy — a written plan, complete records, reporting every result with its uncertainty, open data, declared tools and honest limitations — are the same habits that make it good science.

Key terms

IMRaD
The Introduction–Methods–Results–and–Discussion structure of empirical scientific reports, answering why, how, what and so what.
Structured abstract
A stand-alone summary of 150–250 words with background, aim, methods, results (with numbers) and conclusion.
Reproducibility
The ability of others to repeat a study from its report and obtain consistent results; depends on complete methods and available data.
Figure caption
The self-contained text below a figure that states what is shown, what symbols and error bars represent, and n as experimental units.
Reporting precision
Rounding an uncertainty to two significant figures (three if it starts with 1) and the estimate to the same decimal place.
APA style
The reporting and referencing conventions of the American Psychological Association (7th ed.), e.g., t(18) = 2.31, p = .033, d = 1.03, and author–date citations.
Statistical consistency check
Recomputing a reported p-value from its test statistic and degrees of freedom to detect reporting errors (as done by statcheck).
GRIM test
A check that a reported mean of integer data is attainable, i.e., that mean × n is (close to) an integer.
Plagiarism
Using other people's work or ideas without giving proper credit to the original source.
Patchwriting
Copying a source's sentence structure while substituting some words; a form of plagiarism even when the source is cited.
Reference manager
Software (e.g., Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote) that stores sources, inserts citations and formats reference lists.
Research misconduct
Fabrication, falsification or plagiarism committed intentionally or through gross negligence (Swedish Act 2019:504).
Questionable research practices
Grey-zone practices such as selective reporting, undisclosed flexible analyses and HARKing that bias results without being outright fabrication.
FAIR principles
Guidelines for making data Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable.
Data availability statement
A statement in a report saying where and under which conditions the underlying data, code and materials can be obtained.
