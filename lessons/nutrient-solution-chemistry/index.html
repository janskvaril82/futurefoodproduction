Nutrient-solution chemistry: EC, pH and ion balance

A hydroponic nutrient solution is a carefully balanced mixture of a dozen ions in water — and every number on a fertiliser bag, a water analysis and a meter display is a statement about those ions in a different unit. This lesson teaches you to speak all those units fluently, to check a recipe with the same electroneutrality rule a chemist uses, to predict and correct conductivity and pH, and finally to do what professional growers pay consultants for: turn a tap-water analysis into grams of salt in two stock tanks.

What you will be able to do

  • Convert concentrations between mg L⁻¹, ppm, mmol L⁻¹ and meq L⁻¹, including water-report units such as mg L⁻¹ as CaCO₃ and German degrees of hardness (LO3).
  • Calculate the mass of a fertiliser salt needed for a target concentration, using its formula and molar mass, and check the ion balance of a recipe or water analysis (LO2, LO3).
  • Estimate electrical conductivity from ion concentrations with Kohlrausch's law and the "Σcations/10" rule, and correct measured EC to 25 °C (LO2, LO3).
  • Explain pH buffering by the carbonate system and calculate the acid dose needed to neutralise bicarbonate alkalinity (LO2, LO3).
  • Explain iron-chelate stability, predict precipitation with the saturation index, and justify A/B stock tanks and concentration factors (LO2).
  • Calculate a complete nutrient recipe from a water analysis to grams per stock tank, and manage EC and pH in recirculating and Kratky systems (LO3, LO4).

What is in a nutrient solution?

Plants take up their mineral nutrients almost exclusively as dissolved ions: nitrogen as nitrate (NO₃⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺), phosphorus as dihydrogen phosphate (H₂PO₄⁻), potassium as K⁺, calcium as Ca²⁺, magnesium as Mg²⁺, sulfur as sulfate (SO₄²⁻), and the micronutrients iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and molybdenum at concentrations a thousand times lower. A nutrient solution is simply water in which these ions are present in the right amounts and proportions, at a pH where they stay dissolved and available. In soil, the clay and organic matter act as a reservoir that releases ions slowly and absorbs mistakes. In hydroponics the solution is the reservoir: what you dissolve is what the roots see (Lesson 2.3 covers the plant side of the story).

Three measurable quantities summarise the state of a solution for everyday control: the concentration of each ion (known from the recipe, or measured by laboratory analysis), the electrical conductivity (EC, a quick proxy for the total ion concentration) and the pH (which controls the chemical form, solubility and availability of several nutrients). The grower adjusts two of them daily — EC by adding stock solution or water, pH by adding acid or base — and checks the ion composition by analysis every week or two. To do this well you need to understand what each number means chemically.

Concentration units and conversions

Four units describe the same solution, and each answers a different question. Mass concentration (mg L⁻¹) answers "how many milligrams of the element are in a litre?" — the unit of fertiliser labels and many water reports. In dilute aqueous solutions one litre weighs almost exactly one kilogram, so 1 mg L⁻¹ ≈ 1 mg kg⁻¹ = 1 ppm (part per million by mass). Molar concentration (mmol L⁻¹) answers "how many particles?" — the natural unit for chemistry and for plant uptake, which is ion by ion. Equivalent concentration (meq L⁻¹, or mmol₍c₎ L⁻¹, "millimoles of charge") answers "how much electric charge?" — the unit needed for ion balance and conductivity, because a Ca²⁺ ion carries twice the charge of a K⁺ ion.

Conversion between mass, molar and equivalent concentration(5.2.1)
\[ c_{\text{mmol}} = \frac{c_{\text{mg}}}{M}, \qquad c_{\text{meq}} = |z|\;c_{\text{mmol}} = \frac{|z|\,c_{\text{mg}}}{M} \]

the molar concentration in millimoles per litre is the mass concentration in milligrams per litre divided by the molar mass in grams per mole; the equivalent (charge) concentration is the molar concentration multiplied by the magnitude of the ion's charge.

Follow the derivation
  1. By definition, amount of substance \(n = m/M\). Dividing mass and amount by the same volume gives \(c_{\text{mol}} = c_{\text{mass}}/M\).
  2. With mass in mg and amount in mmol, the molar mass keeps the same number: 1 g mol⁻¹ = 1 mg mmol⁻¹. So mg L⁻¹ ÷ (mg mmol⁻¹) = mmol L⁻¹ with no extra factors.
  3. Each mole of an ion of charge \(z\) carries \(|z|\) moles of elementary charge; one "equivalent" is one mole of charge. Hence meq = |z| × mmol.
  4. Which molar mass? If a report gives "NO₃⁻ 62 mg L⁻¹" (mass of the nitrate ion), divide by 62.00; if it gives "NO₃-N 14 mg L⁻¹" (mass of nitrogen only), divide by 14.007. Both are 1.0 mmol L⁻¹. Mixing these up is the single most common error in nutrient calculations.
Water-report units: as CaCO₃, °dH and ppm

Hardness and alkalinity are often reported "as CaCO₃": 1 meq L⁻¹ = 50.04 mg L⁻¹ as CaCO₃ (half the molar mass of CaCO₃, because CaCO₃ carries two equivalents). Swedish and German water reports often give hardness in German degrees: 1 °dH = 10 mg CaO per litre = 0.1783 mmol L⁻¹ of Ca²⁺ + Mg²⁺ = 0.357 meq L⁻¹. Alkalinity may be given as mg L⁻¹ HCO₃⁻ (divide by 61.02 to get mmol L⁻¹ = meq L⁻¹). "ppm" on a handheld "TDS meter" is not a concentration at all but EC multiplied by an arbitrary factor (0.5 or 0.7) — avoid it.

Classic recipes: Hoagland, Steiner and a lettuce solution

Three recipes are worth knowing by heart, because they represent three philosophies. Hoagland solution (Hoagland & Arnon, 1950) was designed for plant-physiology experiments: rich in everything so that no nutrient limits growth. Its "solution 1" uses only four macronutrient salts — 1 mmol L⁻¹ KH₂PO₄, 5 mmol L⁻¹ KNO₃, 5 mmol L⁻¹ Ca(NO₃)₂ and 2 mmol L⁻¹ MgSO₄ — giving 210 mg N, 31 mg P, 235 mg K, 200 mg Ca, 49 mg Mg and 64 mg S per litre. Steiner's universal solution (Steiner, 1961, 1984) starts from the idea that the ratios between ions matter as much as their levels. Steiner expressed anions and cations as percentages of their total charge — NO₃⁻ : H₂PO₄⁻ : SO₄²⁻ = 60 : 5 : 35 and K⁺ : Ca²⁺ : Mg²⁺ = 35 : 45 : 20 — at a total of 20 meq L⁻¹ on each side for his standard solution, and showed how to calculate the salts that produce any chosen ratio and concentration. Commercial lettuce solutions are more dilute: the Cornell floating-raft system, for example, runs at EC 1.2 dS m⁻¹ with about 8.9 mmol L⁻¹ N, 1.0 P, 5.5 K, 2.1 Ca, 1.0 Mg and 1.1 S (Brechner & Both, 2013).

Ion balance: the electroneutrality check

A solution cannot carry a net electric charge: every positive charge on a cation is matched by a negative charge on an anion. This is not an approximation but a consequence of electrostatics — a litre of water with even a 10⁻⁹ mol excess of one sign would sit at a potential of thousands of volts. The rule gives the recipe designer a powerful check: if the charges do not balance, you have made an arithmetic error, forgotten a partner ion or used a wrong formula. The same check is used by laboratories to judge the quality of a water analysis.

Electroneutrality and ion-balance error(5.2.3)
\[ \sum_{\text{cations}} z_i\,c_i = \sum_{\text{anions}} |z_j|\,c_j, \qquad \text{IBE} = \frac{\Sigma_{+} - \Sigma_{-}}{\Sigma_{+} + \Sigma_{-}}\times100\ \% \]

the sum over all cations of charge times molar concentration equals the sum over all anions of the magnitude of charge times molar concentration; the ion-balance error of an analysis is the difference between the two sums divided by their total, in per cent.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(z_i,\ c_i\)charge number and molar concentration of ion \(i\)–, mmol L⁻¹
\(\Sigma_+,\ \Sigma_-\)sums of cation and anion equivalentsmeq L⁻¹
IBEion-balance error (acceptance: typically within ±5 % for complete analyses)%
Follow the derivation
  1. The charge carried per litre by ion \(i\) is \(z_i\,c_i\,F\) coulombs (with \(c_i\) in mol L⁻¹ and \(F\) = 96 485 C mol⁻¹).
  2. Net charge per litre: \(F\sum_i z_i c_i\). Electroneutrality requires this to be zero (to far better than one part per million).
  3. Splitting the sum into positive and negative terms gives \(\sum_+ z_i c_i = \sum_- |z_j| c_j\) — i.e. Σcations = Σanions in meq L⁻¹.
  4. H⁺ and OH⁻ are included in principle but are negligible between pH 5 and 8 (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁸ mol L⁻¹). Uncharged species — boric acid, dissolved CO₂, the chelated iron complex — do not appear.
Assumptions and validity

A recipe calculated from salt formulas always balances exactly, because every salt is itself neutral. An analysis may not: unmeasured ions (e.g. organic acids from roots, silicate), analytical error or a wrong alkalinity figure show up as imbalance. In a recipe, when you "adjust" one ion without changing its partner, the recipe no longer corresponds to any real mixture of salts — the imbalance tells you so.

(–) × mmol L⁻¹ = mmol of charge L⁻¹ = meq L⁻¹ on both sides ✓; IBE dimensionless ✓

Electrical conductivity: measuring the total

Pure water barely conducts electricity; dissolved ions do, because they move in an electric field and carry their charge with them. A conductivity meter applies an alternating voltage between two electrodes a fixed distance apart and measures the current; the electrical conductivity (EC) is the conductance scaled by the cell geometry, in siemens per metre. Horticulture uses dS m⁻¹, which is numerically identical to mS cm⁻¹; lettuce solutions are typically 1.0–2.0 dS m⁻¹ and tomato solutions 2–4. How electrochemical sensors work is covered in Lesson 9.2. Here we ask how EC depends on the composition.

EC from composition: Kohlrausch's law and the cation rule(5.2.4)
\[ \kappa^{\circ} = \sum_i c_i\,\lambda_i^{\circ} \quad(\mu\text{S cm}^{-1}), \qquad \text{EC} \approx \frac{\Sigma_{+}}{10}\quad(\text{dS m}^{-1}) \]

at infinite dilution, conductivity is the sum over all ions of concentration times the ion's limiting molar conductivity (Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions). For real nutrient solutions, the EC in decisiemens per metre is approximately the sum of cation equivalents in meq per litre divided by ten.

Follow the derivation of Kohlrausch's law
  1. In a field \(E\), an ion of charge \(z e\) drifts at velocity \(v = u E\), where \(u\) is its mobility (set by its size and hydration shell).
  2. The current density carried by ion \(i\) is charge per volume × velocity: \(j_i = (z_i F c_i)(u_i E)\).
  3. Conductivity is current density per unit field, summed over independent ions: \(\kappa = \sum_i z_i F u_i c_i = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i\), with \(\lambda_i = z_i F u_i\).
  4. Kohlrausch showed experimentally (1870s) that at infinite dilution each ion contributes independently of its partner — the "law of independent migration". The \(\lambda^{\circ}\) values are tabulated (Haynes, 2014).
  5. Units: with \(c\) in mmol L⁻¹ = 10⁻⁶ mol cm⁻³ and \(\lambda\) in S cm² mol⁻¹, \(c\lambda\) is in 10⁻⁶ S cm⁻¹ = µS cm⁻¹ — so the sum of \(c_i\lambda_i\) with c in mmol L⁻¹ gives µS cm⁻¹ directly.
Why the rule "Σcations/10" works — and Kohlrausch overestimates

At real concentrations ions are not independent: each is surrounded by an "atmosphere" of opposite charge that drags on it (the Debye–Hückel–Onsager effect) and some pair up (e.g. CaSO₄⁰, MgSO₄⁰ ion pairs carry no current). Conductivity therefore grows less than proportionally with concentration. For typical nutrient solutions, the infinite-dilution sum overestimates the measured EC by roughly a quarter to a third, and a simple empirical rule — EC in dS m⁻¹ ≈ Σcations in meq L⁻¹ divided by 10 — works within about ±10 % (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009). The course library implements the rule as ecFromCations(). The rule is less accurate for solutions dominated by sulfate or by sodium chloride, and at EC above about 4 dS m⁻¹.

mmol L⁻¹ × S cm² mol⁻¹ = 10⁻⁶ mol cm⁻³ × S cm² mol⁻¹ = µS cm⁻¹ ✓; meq L⁻¹ ÷ 10 → dS m⁻¹ (empirical constant has units dS m⁻¹ per meq L⁻¹)

For the Cornell recipe the Kohlrausch sum is 1.61 dS m⁻¹, the rule gives 1.19, and the target measured value is 1.2: the real solution conducts about 75 % as well as the ideal sum. Use Kohlrausch to understand why different ions contribute differently (sulfate and calcium are "strong" per mole because they carry two charges; phosphate is weak), and the rule to set the EC target of a recipe.

\[ \text{EC}_{25} = \frac{\text{EC}_T}{1 + \alpha\,(T - 25)} \]

Assumptions and validity

Common misconception

"EC tells me how much nitrogen is in my tank." EC measures total charge carriers only. It cannot tell nitrate from sodium chloride, and boric acid, urea and the chelated iron complex contribute almost nothing. In a recirculating system where plants remove nitrate and potassium but leave sodium, the EC can stay constant while the solution slowly becomes a salt solution with little nutrient in it. EC is a control variable; laboratory analysis is the check.

pH, bicarbonate and buffering

pH is minus the logarithm of the hydrogen-ion activity. Most crops grow well in solution culture between pH 5.5 and 6.5; the Cornell lettuce system aims at 5.8 with 5.6–6.0 acceptable (Brechner & Both, 2013). The window is set by chemistry rather than by the roots themselves. Below about pH 5, root membranes are damaged and calcium and magnesium uptake is depressed; above about 6.5, iron, manganese, zinc and copper become less available, phosphate shifts from H₂PO₄⁻ to HPO₄²⁻ and calcium phosphates can precipitate, and some iron chelates begin to lose their iron (Section 5.2.8; Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009).

Two processes push pH around. Water brings bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), a base, which makes most tap water slightly alkaline and resists acidification. Plants shift pH as they take up ions: to keep their own charge balance, roots that take up more anions than cations (the usual case with nitrate nutrition) release OH⁻ or HCO₃⁻, and the solution becomes more alkaline; roots feeding on ammonium release H⁺ and acidify it. This is why nitrate-based solutions drift upward in pH and why growers keep a little ammonium in the recipe (typically 5–10 % of N) to counteract the drift.

The carbonate buffer (Henderson–Hasselbalch form)(5.2.6)
\[ \mathrm{CO_2(aq) + H_2O \rightleftharpoons HCO_3^{-} + H^{+}}, \qquad \text{pH} = \text{p}K_{a1} + \log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{HCO_3^{-}}]}{[\mathrm{CO_2(aq)}]} \]

dissolved carbon dioxide and bicarbonate form an acid–base pair. The pH equals the first acid dissociation constant, expressed as pKa (6.35 at 25 °C), plus the logarithm of the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved carbon dioxide. When the two are equal, pH = 6.35 and the buffer is strongest.

Assumptions and validity: open versus closed systems

The equation holds whether or not CO₂ can escape, but the amounts differ. Acidifying bicarbonate water converts HCO₃⁻ to CO₂. In a closed tank the CO₂ stays and holds the pH down; in an aerated tank (DWC) or a splashing NFT return, CO₂ degasses towards equilibrium with air (about 0.014 mmol L⁻¹ at today's ≈ 420 ppm CO₂), so the ratio [HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂] rises and the pH creeps upward over hours even though no base was added. Well-aerated solutions therefore need a lower residual alkalinity, or more frequent pH correction, than still ones.

Nutrient availability, iron chelates and precipitation

Iron is the micronutrient most affected by pH. Free Fe³⁺ is essentially insoluble at pH above about 4 — it precipitates as iron(III) hydroxide — so hydroponic iron is always supplied as a chelate: an organic molecule (a ligand) that wraps around the Fe³⁺ ion with several bonds and keeps it in solution. Chelates differ in how strongly they hold iron against competition from OH⁻ and from other metal ions such as Ca²⁺. A recent comparison summarised the practical limits: Fe-EDTA holds iron up to about pH 6.5, Fe-DTPA up to about pH 7.5 and Fe-EDDHA up to about pH 9 — and costs roughly two (DTPA) and four (EDDHA) times as much as EDTA (Hershkowitz et al., 2025; see also Lucena, 2003). In a well-managed solution at pH 5.5–6.3, the cheaper EDTA or DTPA chelates are sufficient; EDDHA is insurance for systems whose pH drifts high. Iron chelates are also photodegraded by blue and ultraviolet light, which releases iron that then precipitates — one more reason to use opaque tanks and pipes (Albano & Miller, 2001).

Precipitation is the other chemical enemy. When the product of the activities of two ions exceeds the solubility product of a solid, that solid can form: calcium with sulfate gives gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O); calcium with hydrogen phosphate gives brushite (CaHPO₄·2H₂O) and, more slowly, other calcium phosphates; iron with phosphate gives iron phosphates. The tool that predicts this is the saturation index.

\[ \begin{gathered} \text{SI} = \log_{10}\frac{\text{IAP}}{K_{sp}} = \log_{10}\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{Ca}}[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]\;\gamma_{\mathrm{SO_4}}[\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}]}{K_{sp}} \\[6pt] \log_{10}\gamma = -0.51\,z^2\left(\frac{\sqrt{I}}{1+\sqrt{I}} - 0.3\,I\right) \end{gathered} \]

Why A and B stock tanks exist

Weighing hundreds of grams of salt into every tank of working solution is impractical, so growers prepare concentrated stock solutions — typically 100 or 200 times the working concentration — and dose them into the irrigation water with injectors or dosing pumps. The concentration factor \(F\) links the two: the stock contains \(F\) times the working concentration, and is diluted 1 : \(F\) (1 L of each stock per \(F\) litres of working solution). At \(F\) = 100, a Hoagland-like solution would need 0.5 mol L⁻¹ Ca²⁺ and 0.2 mol L⁻¹ SO₄²⁻ in the same tank. The ideal ion product is 0.1 (mol L⁻¹)², about 4000 times the gypsum \(K_{sp}\): even allowing activity coefficients of 0.1–0.2 at that ionic strength, SI ≈ +2 or more — the tank would fill with gypsum. Calcium and phosphate behave similarly. Hence the universal rule:

  • Tank A: calcium nitrate, iron chelate, part of the potassium nitrate (and ammonium nitrate).
  • Tank B: phosphates, sulfates (magnesium sulfate, potassium sulfate), micronutrient sulfates, borate, molybdate, the rest of the potassium nitrate — and often the acid.
  • Never mix concentrated A and B directly; dilute each into the water separately (the working solution is undersaturated, SI ≈ −1 for gypsum in Hoagland 1).

A complete recipe: from water analysis to grams per stock tank

Now we combine everything. The procedure below is the one used, with more ions and software, by greenhouse fertiliser consultants in the Netherlands and Scandinavia (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009). It works in mmol L⁻¹ of ions throughout and converts to grams only at the end.

  1. Choose the target composition for the crop (mmol L⁻¹): e.g. for lettuce K 5.5, Ca 2.5, Mg 1.0, NH₄ 0.5, H₂PO₄ 1.0, SO₄ 1.5, plus micronutrients; NO₃ follows from the balance.
  2. Subtract the water. Ions already present in the water (Ca, Mg, SO₄, and the unwanted Na, Cl) are counted; only the difference is added.
  3. Neutralise the bicarbonate with acid (Eq. 5.2.7), keeping ≈ 0.5 mmol L⁻¹. Use phosphoric acid for as much of the phosphorus as possible, nitric acid for the rest.
  4. Allocate cations to salts: Ca from calcium nitrate (brings NO₃ and a little NH₄); Mg from magnesium sulfate (brings SO₄); remaining SO₄ from potassium sulfate (brings K); remaining K from potassium nitrate (brings NO₃); remaining NH₄ from ammonium nitrate.
  5. Check the ion balance and EC (Σ+/10).
  6. Convert to grams with Eq. 5.2.2 for the stock volume and concentration factor; split the salts between tanks A and B; split KNO₃ between the tanks to balance their EC and solubility.

Recirculation: what accumulates

In a closed system the crop removes water and nutrients from the loop, and the grower tops up with water and stock solution to restore the EC. Ions the crop takes up only slowly — sodium and chloride from tap water, and to a lesser extent sulfate — are replaced by fresh water faster than the crop removes them, so they accumulate. Because the grower controls EC, the accumulation of "ballast" ions gradually displaces nutrients: at a constant EC of 1.4 dS m⁻¹, every 1 mmol L⁻¹ of NaCl in the tank is roughly 0.1 dS m⁻¹ that is no longer nutrient. A mass balance tells you how fast this happens and how much to discharge (bleed) to keep it under control.

Accumulation of a non-absorbed ion in a closed loop(5.2.9)
\[ \begin{gathered} V\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t} = Q_{\text{in}}\,c_w - Q_b\,c \\[6pt] c(t) = c_{ss} + (c_0 - c_{ss})\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad c_{ss} = \frac{c_w}{b}, \qquad \tau = \frac{V(1-b)}{b\,E} \end{gathered} \]

the rate of change of the amount of sodium in the loop equals what enters with the top-up water minus what leaves with the bleed. The concentration approaches a steady state equal to the concentration in the top-up water divided by the bleed fraction, exponentially, with a time constant equal to the system volume divided by the bleed flow.

Managing EC and pH in a Kratky system

The Kratky method is a closed system with no top-up and no bleed: everything that enters at planting stays until harvest, except what the plants take up. Its EC therefore evolves according to how the crop's uptake of nutrients compares with its uptake of water. Define the uptake concentration \(c_u\): the concentration of nutrients in the water that the plants remove (nutrients taken up ÷ water taken up). If plants took up nutrients and water in exactly the proportion of the solution (\(c_u = c_0\)), the concentration would never change.

Concentration drift in a non-circulating tank(5.2.10)
\[ c(f) = \frac{c_0 - c_u\,f}{1 - f}, \qquad f = \frac{W}{V_0} \]

the concentration after a fraction f of the initial solution has been taken up equals the initial concentration minus the uptake concentration times f, all divided by the fraction of solution remaining. For a salt the plant does not take up at all (cu = 0), the concentration simply rises as one over the fraction remaining.

Use rain water or RO water if your tap water's EC is high, use deeper tanks (smaller \(f\) at harvest), and empty the tank after each crop.

pH in a Kratky tank usually drifts slowly upward with nitrate nutrition, but because the volume per plant is large, the drift is gentle. Kratky reports that crops are typically completed without pH adjustment when a commercial hydroponic fertiliser and good-quality water are used. If pH becomes too acidic, he suggests suspending dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate) in a mesh bag in the tank; if too basic, adding ammonium sulfate, whose uptake releases H⁺ — for example a 12 g L⁻¹ ammonium sulfate stock dosed at the same rate as the fertiliser stocks, checking pH again after four days (Kratky, 2009). Strong acid can also be used, carefully — but remember that adding volume is forbidden in Kratky, so use concentrated acid in small amounts, and never raise the solution level.

Daily routine for your project system

(1) Read the solution level (water used). (2) Measure temperature, EC (note whether it is compensated) and pH, always at the same time of day and at the same depth. (3) In DWC/NFT: top up with water to restore volume first, then add equal volumes of A and B until EC is back at the set point, then correct pH. In Kratky: do not add volume; only record, and correct pH if it leaves 5.5–6.5 for more than a day. (4) Calibrate pH meters weekly with pH 4 and 7 buffers and check the EC meter with a standard (e.g. 1.413 mS cm⁻¹ KCl). (5) Enter everything in the grow log.

Key terms

Millimole per litre (mmol L⁻¹)
Molar concentration: amount of an ion or molecule per litre of solution.
Milliequivalent (meq L⁻¹)
Millimoles of charge per litre, |z| × mmol L⁻¹; used for ion balance and EC.
ppm
Parts per million by mass; in dilute aqueous solution numerically equal to mg L⁻¹.
Ion balance
Equality of the sums of cation and anion equivalents required by electroneutrality.
Electrical conductivity (EC)
Ability of a solution to conduct current, proportional to the concentration and mobility of ions; in dS m⁻¹ = mS cm⁻¹.
Kohlrausch's law
At infinite dilution, conductivity is the sum of concentration × limiting ionic conductivity over all ions.
Temperature compensation
Conversion of EC measured at temperature T to the value at 25 °C, about 1.9–2 % per °C.
Alkalinity
Acid-neutralising capacity of water, mainly bicarbonate in natural waters; expressed in meq L⁻¹ or mg L⁻¹ as CaCO₃.
Buffer
An acid–base pair that resists pH change; the carbonate buffer is strongest near pH 6.35.
Chelate
An organic ligand that binds a metal ion (e.g. Fe³⁺) at several points, keeping it soluble; EDTA, DTPA and EDDHA differ in pH stability.
Saturation index (SI)
log₁₀ of the ion activity product over the solubility product; SI > 0 means precipitation is possible.
Concentration factor
Ratio of stock to working concentration (e.g. 100×), equal to the dilution ratio at injection.
A/B stock tanks
Separate concentrated stocks that keep calcium apart from sulfate and phosphate to prevent precipitation.
Bleed (discharge)
Solution deliberately removed from a closed system to limit the accumulation of non-absorbed ions.
Uptake concentration
Nutrient uptake divided by water uptake of a crop; determines whether solution EC rises or falls.
