Remote sensing and vegetation indices

Every few days a Sentinel-2 satellite passes about 786 km above Västmanland and photographs every field in the county in thirteen colours, most of them invisible to us. The pictures are free. A farmer near Västerås can open them in a web browser, see that the north-east corner of a wheat field is paler than the rest, and send a variable-rate nitrogen file to the spreader before lunch. How can a camera in space tell a hungry crop from a healthy one? The answer lies in the physics of how leaves absorb, scatter and reflect light. This lesson follows a photon from the Sun to the leaf and back to the sensor, turns reflectances into vegetation indices, and asks the engineer's questions: how precise, how often, how fine — and how far can we trust a map made from space?

Seeing crops from a distance

Remote sensing is the measurement of an object's properties without touching it — by recording electromagnetic radiation that the object reflects or emits. A farmer who walks into a field and notices that the leaves are yellow is doing a crude kind of remote sensing with two sensors (her eyes) that record three broad colour bands. Modern instruments do the same thing with dozens or hundreds of narrow bands, many of them outside the visible range, from platforms that range from a hand-held sensor 50 cm above the crop to satellites 700–800 km up. In Lesson 11.1 you saw how precision agriculture needs maps of within-field variability; remote sensing is the cheapest way to make such maps repeatedly during the season (Mulla, 2013; Weiss et al., 2020).

Two families of sensors exist. Passive sensors record radiation from a natural source: reflected sunlight in the visible, near-infrared (NIR) and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) regions (400–2500 nm), or thermal radiation emitted by the surface itself (8–14 µm). Active sensors bring their own illumination: a radar (such as the synthetic aperture radar on Sentinel-1) emits microwave pulses and records the echo; a LiDAR emits laser pulses; and a tractor-mounted crop sensor may carry its own flash lamp or LEDs. Active sensors work at night and — in the case of radar — through clouds.

What a satellite actually records is radiance: power per unit area, per unit solid angle and per unit wavelength arriving at the sensor (W m⁻² sr⁻¹ µm⁻¹). Radiance depends on how bright the Sun is, how high it stands and how hazy the air is, so it changes from day to day even if the crop does not. What we want is a property of the surface itself: the reflectance, the fraction of the incoming light that the surface reflects at each wavelength. Reflectance is dimensionless and lies between 0 and 1, which makes images from different days, sensors and latitudes comparable.

From at-sensor radiance to reflectance(11.2.1)
\[ \rho_\lambda = \frac{\pi\,L_\lambda\,d^{2}}{E_{0,\lambda}\,\cos\theta_s} \]

the reflectance in a band equals pi times the measured radiance in that band times the squared Earth–Sun distance in astronomical units, divided by the solar irradiance at the top of the atmosphere in that band times the cosine of the solar zenith angle.

  1. Irradiance on the ground. Sunlight arriving at an angle θₛ from the vertical spreads over a larger area: a horizontal square metre intercepts \(E = (E_0/d^2)\cos\theta_s\), where \(E_0/d^2\) corrects the mean solar irradiance for the actual Earth–Sun distance (inverse-square law).

Assumptions and validity

The equation ignores the atmosphere, so it gives top-of-atmosphere (TOA) reflectance — Sentinel-2 "Level-1C" products. Haze adds path radiance (making dark targets such as a red-absorbing crop look too bright) and absorbs light (making bright targets look too dark); correcting for this gives bottom-of-atmosphere or surface reflectance ("Level-2A"). Real canopies are not Lambertian: they look brighter when viewed with the Sun behind the observer (the "hot spot") and darker when looking into the Sun, so viewing geometry matters for off-nadir images and for drones with wide-angle lenses.

How leaves and canopies interact with light

When a photon reaches a leaf it can be reflected (at the waxy surface or after scattering inside), transmitted (it leaves through the lower side) or absorbed (by a pigment, by water or by dry matter). Energy conservation requires that, at every wavelength, the three fractions add up to one: \(\rho_\lambda + \tau_\lambda + \alpha_\lambda = 1\). Which of the three dominates depends on the wavelength, and each region of the spectrum tells us about a different property of the plant (Gates et al., 1965; Knipling, 1970).

  • Visible (400–700 nm): pigments. Chlorophylls a and b absorb strongly in the blue (≈ 430–450 nm) and red (≈ 640–680 nm); carotenoids add absorption in the blue. Absorption is weaker in the green, so a healthy leaf reflects about 10–15 % of green light and looks green — but it still absorbs most of it. Because pigment absorption is so strong in the red, a canopy's red reflectance is only a few per cent.
  • Red edge (≈ 690–750 nm): the steepest slope in nature. Chlorophyll absorption stops abruptly at about 700 nm, so reflectance climbs from ≈ 5 % to ≈ 45 % within a few tens of nanometres. The wavelength of the inflection point, the red-edge position, moves to longer wavelengths when chlorophyll content increases, because the absorption band widens (Horler et al., 1983). This makes the red edge a sensitive indicator of chlorophyll — and therefore of nitrogen status.
  • Near-infrared plateau (≈ 750–1300 nm): structure. No leaf pigment absorbs here. Instead, light is scattered at the countless interfaces between wet cell walls (refractive index ≈ 1.4) and the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll (≈ 1.0). A leaf reflects roughly half and transmits most of the rest; absorption is only a few per cent. In a canopy, light transmitted through one leaf is reflected by the leaves below, so canopy NIR reflectance increases with the number of leaf layers — that is, with leaf area index (LAI).
  • Shortwave infrared (1300–2500 nm): water and dry matter. Liquid water absorbs strongly at ≈ 1450 and 1940 nm and increasingly beyond 2000 nm, weakly at 970 and 1200 nm. SWIR reflectance therefore rises as a leaf dries. Cellulose, lignin and protein add weaker features near 1730, 2100 and 2300 nm.
Common misconception

"Plants are green because they reflect most of the green light." A healthy leaf reflects only about a tenth of the green light that falls on it; it absorbs most of the rest (which is why green photons still drive photosynthesis, as you saw in Lesson 4.1). What a leaf reflects most is near-infrared light, about half of it. If our eyes could see the NIR, a healthy wheat field would look brilliant white, and a drought-stressed one grey.

Stress changes the spectrum in characteristic ways. Nitrogen deficiency lowers chlorophyll content: red and green reflectance rise, and the red edge shifts to shorter wavelengths. Drought first closes stomata (visible in thermal images before any change in reflectance), then lowers leaf water content (SWIR reflectance rises) and finally reduces leaf area (NIR falls). Senescence and disease destroy chlorophyll and cell structure: the red trough fills in and the NIR plateau collapses. Spectral signatures and mixed pixels

The shape of a reflectance spectrum is a spectral signature: a fingerprint that lets us separate vegetation from soil, water, snow or asphalt. Table 1 lists typical band reflectances for the surfaces a farmer sees from space. Three rules of thumb follow directly from the physics of the previous section:

  • Vegetation: dark in blue and red, a small green peak, a steep red edge, bright NIR, moderate SWIR that depends on water content.
  • Bare soil: a smooth spectrum rising slowly with wavelength; moisture, organic matter and iron oxides darken it but hardly change its shape. On a plot of NIR against red reflectance, bare soils of one field fall on a straight line — the soil line.
  • Water: some reflectance in blue and green, almost none beyond 750 nm, because water absorbs NIR and SWIR within millimetres to centimetres. Open water therefore appears black in NIR images.

A satellite pixel, however, rarely contains a single surface. A 10 m Sentinel-2 pixel on a field edge may contain wheat, a tramline, a ditch and part of the road; early in the season, every pixel of a cereal field is a mixture of plants and soil. To a good approximation, the reflectance of a mixed pixel is the area-weighted average of the reflectances of its components:

Linear spectral mixing(11.2.2)
\[ \rho_{\text{pixel},\lambda} = \sum_{j=1}^{m} f_j\,\rho_{j,\lambda},\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{m} f_j = 1,\qquad f_j\ge 0 \]

the reflectance of a pixel in each band is the sum, over all surface types in the pixel, of the fraction of the pixel covered by that surface times its reflectance; the fractions add up to one.

Follow the reasoning
  1. The radiant power leaving the pixel towards the sensor is the sum of the power leaving each part of it: \(M_{\text{pixel}}A = \sum_j M_j A_j\), where \(A\) is area and \(M\) exitance.
  2. Each part receives the same irradiance \(E\), so \(M_j = \rho_j E\).
  3. Divide by \(EA\): \(\rho_{\text{pixel}} = \sum_j (A_j/A)\rho_j = \sum_j f_j\rho_j\). ✓
  4. Unmixing reverses the process: with \(m\) known endmember spectra and at least \(m-1\) bands, the fractions \(f_j\) can be estimated by least squares. This is how fractional vegetation cover can be mapped from a mixed pixel.
Assumptions and validity

The linear model assumes that each photon interacts with only one surface type before reaching the sensor. In real canopies light is scattered between leaves and soil (non-linear mixing), which matters most in the NIR, where leaves transmit a lot. The model also needs good endmember spectra — and the "soil" endmember changes with moisture. Still, linear mixing is the right first model for field edges, tramlines and early-season crops.

Reveal the reasoning

Vegetation indices: formulas and behaviour

A single band says little on its own: a field looks darker in the red both when the crop is denser and when a cloud shadow passes. A vegetation index combines two or more bands so that the vegetation signal is enhanced and unwanted effects — illumination, soil background, atmosphere — are suppressed. More than a hundred indices have been published (Xue & Su, 2017); a handful do most of the work in agriculture.

NDVI — the workhorse

The Normalised Difference Vegetation Index was introduced by Rouse et al. (1974) for the first Landsat satellite and popularised by Tucker (1979). It uses the contrast between red absorption by chlorophyll and NIR scattering by leaf structure:

Normalised Difference Vegetation Index(11.2.3)
\[ \text{NDVI} = \frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}}} = \frac{\text{SR}-1}{\text{SR}+1},\qquad \text{SR}=\frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}}{\rho_{\text{red}}} \]

NDVI is the near-infrared reflectance minus the red reflectance, divided by their sum. It can also be written in terms of the simple ratio of NIR to red reflectance: the simple ratio minus one, over the simple ratio plus one.

\(\rho_{\text{NIR}}\)near-infrared reflectance–B8 (842 nm, 10 m) or B8A (865 nm, 20 m)
\(\rho_{\text{red}}\)red reflectance–B4 (665 nm, 10 m)

Follow the derivation: why normalise?
  1. Start with the difference \(\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}\): large for vegetation, small for soil, negative for water. But it is also proportional to illumination: under a thin cloud that dims both bands by a factor \(k\), the difference becomes \(k(\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}})\).
  2. Divide by the sum. \(\dfrac{k\rho_{\text{NIR}}-k\rho_{\text{red}}}{k\rho_{\text{NIR}}+k\rho_{\text{red}}} = \dfrac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}}}\): any multiplicative factor common to both bands cancels. Topographic shading, sun angle and uniform haze are reduced (though not removed, because they are not perfectly multiplicative).
  3. Bounds. With non-negative reflectances the ratio lies between −1 (\(\rho_{\text{NIR}}=0\)) and +1 (\(\rho_{\text{red}}=0\)).
  4. Relation to SR. Divide numerator and denominator by \(\rho_{\text{red}}\): \(\text{NDVI}=(\text{SR}-1)/(\text{SR}+1)\). NDVI is a compressed version of the simple ratio: SR = 9 gives NDVI = 0.80, SR = 19 gives 0.90 — doubling the ratio moves NDVI only 0.1. This compression is the mathematical root of saturation (Section 11.2.5).
Assumptions and validity

NDVI assumes that red and NIR reflectance differences are caused by green vegetation. Bright soils, wet soils, crop residues, senescent leaves and additive atmospheric effects (path radiance, which is not multiplicative) all change NDVI without a change in the green canopy. NDVI values from different sensors are not identical because band positions and widths differ; always compare like with like and use surface reflectance.

SAVI and MSAVI — correcting for soil

Early in the season, when crop cover is sparse, NDVI depends strongly on the soil under the plants: the same crop gives a higher NDVI over dark wet soil than over bright dry soil. Huete (1988) showed that lines of equal vegetation amount in the red–NIR plane (vegetation "isolines") do not pass through the origin, as NDVI implicitly assumes, but converge at a point in the negative quadrant. Shifting the origin gives the Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index:

Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index(11.2.4)
\[ \text{SAVI} = (1+L)\,\frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}}+L} \]

SAVI is one plus L, times the NIR-minus-red difference, divided by the NIR-plus-red sum plus the soil-adjustment factor L.

\(L\)soil-adjustment factor–0.5 (general); 1 (very sparse); 0.25 (dense)

Follow the derivation
  1. NDVI measures the angle of the line from the origin to the point \((\rho_{\text{red}},\rho_{\text{NIR}})\): all points on a straight line through the origin have the same NDVI.
  2. Huete observed that isolines of a given canopy over different soils meet not at \((0,0)\) but near \((-a,-a)\).
  3. Measure the angle from that point instead: replace \(\rho\) by \(\rho+a\) in both bands. \(\dfrac{(\rho_{\text{NIR}}+a)-(\rho_{\text{red}}+a)}{(\rho_{\text{NIR}}+a)+(\rho_{\text{red}}+a)} = \dfrac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}}+2a}\). Call \(L=2a\).
  4. For an ideal target with \(\rho_{\text{NIR}}=1\), \(\rho_{\text{red}}=0\) this gives \(1/(1+L)\); multiplying by \((1+L)\) restores the maximum of 1. ✓
  5. MSAVI2 (Qi et al., 1994) replaces the constant \(L\) by one that adjusts itself to the vegetation amount: \(\text{MSAVI2} = \tfrac12\left[2\rho_{\text{NIR}}+1-\sqrt{(2\rho_{\text{NIR}}+1)^2-8(\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}})}\right]\). Assumptions and validity

\(L=0.5\) was found to minimise soil effects over a wide range of cover. With \(L=0\) SAVI equals NDVI. SAVI reduces, but does not eliminate, soil effects, and it is slightly less sensitive than NDVI to changes in dense canopies.

EVI — less atmosphere, less saturation

The Enhanced Vegetation Index was developed for NASA's MODIS sensors to stay sensitive in dense forests and crops and to be less affected by aerosols (Huete et al., 2002). It uses the blue band, which is scattered much more strongly by aerosols than red, to correct the red band, and a background term like SAVI's:

Enhanced Vegetation Index(11.2.5)
\[ \text{EVI} = G\,\frac{\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}}}{\rho_{\text{NIR}}+C_1\,\rho_{\text{red}}-C_2\,\rho_{\text{blue}}+L},\qquad G=2.5,\;C_1=6,\;C_2=7.5,\;L=1 \]

EVI is a gain factor times the NIR-minus-red difference, divided by the NIR reflectance plus six times the red, minus seven and a half times the blue, plus one.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(G\)gain factor–2.5
\(C_1, C_2\)aerosol-resistance coefficients for red and blue–6 and 7.5
\(L\)canopy-background adjustment–1
\(\rho_{\text{blue}}\)blue reflectance (Sentinel-2 B2, 490 nm)–0.02–0.1
Where the terms come from
  1. Aerosol resistance. Aerosol scattering adds more light in the blue than in the red. The combination \(C_1\rho_{\text{red}}-C_2\rho_{\text{blue}}\) cancels much of that additive contribution in the denominator, following the "atmospherically resistant" idea of combining blue and red.
  2. Background adjustment. The constant \(L=1\) in the denominator plays the role of SAVI's soil factor, reducing soil-brightness effects.
  3. Gain. \(G=2.5\) scales the index to a similar range as NDVI for typical vegetation.
  4. Result. Because the red term is multiplied by 6 in the denominator and NIR is not divided by a sum that approaches 2·NIR, EVI keeps rising in dense canopies where NDVI has flattened.
Assumptions and validity

The coefficients were tuned for MODIS bands; they are widely used with Sentinel-2 and Landsat surface reflectance but are not exact for them. EVI needs an accurate, atmospherically corrected blue band; over bright targets (clouds, snow) the denominator can approach zero and values explode. For sensors without a blue band (many drone cameras) the two-band EVI2 \(=2.5(\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}})/(\rho_{\text{NIR}}+2.4\rho_{\text{red}}+1)\) behaves similarly (Jiang et al., 2008).

Red-edge, green and water indices

Most other indices used in agriculture have the same normalised-difference form, with different pairs of bands chosen for different targets. Sentinel-2 made the red-edge bands (B5–B7) available free of charge at 20 m, which is why red-edge indices are now common in nitrogen advice.

The normalised-difference family: NDRE, GNDVI and NDWI(11.2.6)
\[ \text{ND}(i,j)=\frac{\rho_i-\rho_j}{\rho_i+\rho_j}:\quad \text{NDRE}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{RE}),\;\; \text{GNDVI}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{green}),\;\; \text{NDWI}=\text{ND}(\text{NIR},\text{SWIR}) \]

a normalised difference of two bands i and j is their difference divided by their sum. NDRE uses the near-infrared and a red-edge band, GNDVI the near-infrared and green, and Gao's NDWI the near-infrared and shortwave infrared.

Why these band pairs work
  1. NDRE. At 705 nm chlorophyll absorption is weaker than in the red, so light penetrates deeper and the band does not saturate at the top of a dense canopy. Reflectance at 705 nm keeps falling as chlorophyll rises, while NIR carries the structural information: the pair tracks canopy chlorophyll (≈ nitrogen) content even at high LAI.
  2. GNDVI. Green reflectance is controlled by chlorophyll concentration over a wide range, again without the strong saturation of the red band (Gitelson et al., 1996).
  3. NDWI (Gao). Both 865 and 1610 nm are scattered by the same leaf structure, but only 1610 nm is absorbed by water. Their normalised difference isolates water content from structure.
  4. Two NDWIs! McFeeters' NDWI uses green and NIR and is designed to map lakes and flooded fields; Gao's uses NIR and SWIR and measures vegetation water. Always check which one a paper or software means.
  5. Red-edge position. Frampton et al. (2013) estimated the inflection point of the red edge from four Sentinel-2 bands by linear interpolation: \(\text{S2REP} = 705 + 35\,\dfrac{(\rho_{B4}+\rho_{B7})/2-\rho_{B5}}{\rho_{B6}-\rho_{B5}}\) nm. It moves from ≈ 715 nm in pale crops to ≈ 725 nm in dark green ones (see the readouts of Figure 3).
Assumptions and validity

All normalised differences inherit NDVI's cancellation of multiplicative effects and its sensitivity to additive ones. Red-edge bands on Sentinel-2 are 20 m, so NDRE maps are coarser than NDVI maps from the 10 m bands. Relationships between any index and nitrogen are crop-, variety- and stage-specific and need local calibration.

Key idea

A vegetation index is a model, not a measurement of anything biological. It is a number that correlates with LAI, biomass, chlorophyll or water content under certain conditions. The link to the agronomic quantity you care about — kilograms of nitrogen in the crop, tonnes of yield — must always be established by calibration against field measurements (Section 11.2.9).

Why NDVI saturates — and what to do about it

Imagine a wheat crop growing from emergence to flowering. At first each new leaf covers bare soil, and NDVI shoots up. By LAI ≈ 2–3 almost no soil is visible from above, and red reflectance has nearly reached its floor of about 0.03: the top leaves absorb almost all red light, and additional leaves below them cannot absorb what never reaches them. NIR reflectance, in contrast, keeps rising to LAI ≈ 5–6, because each additional leaf layer reflects some of the NIR transmitted by the layers above (Gitelson, 2004). NDVI, however, barely notices the NIR increase. When \(\rho_{\text{red}}\ll\rho_{\text{NIR}}\), NDVI \(\approx 1-2\rho_{\text{red}}/\rho_{\text{NIR}}\), and its sensitivity to NIR,

\[ \frac{\partial\,\text{NDVI}}{\partial\rho_{\text{NIR}}}=\frac{2\rho_{\text{red}}}{(\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}})^2}, \]

becomes tiny: with \(\rho_{\text{red}}=0.03\), increasing NIR from 0.40 to 0.50 (a 25 % change) moves NDVI only from 0.860 to 0.887. This is NDVI saturation. It matters practically, because the most valuable decisions — late nitrogen doses, yield forecasts, lodging risk — are made in dense canopies, exactly where NDVI has stopped changing (Carlson & Ripley, 1997; Baret & Guyot, 1991).

Saturation also turns small measurement errors into large errors in the estimated LAI. If an index changes by \(dV\) per unit LAI, an error \(\sigma_V\) in the index gives an LAI error of about \(\sigma_V/|dV/d\text{LAI}|\). Figure 4 makes this concrete.

What can be done? (1) Use NDVI when it is sensitive — early in the season, which is conveniently when the first nitrogen top-dressing is decided. (2) Use indices that saturate later: red-edge indices (NDRE, S2REP), EVI, or the Wide Dynamic Range Vegetation Index WDRVI \(=(\alpha\rho_{\text{NIR}}-\rho_{\text{red}})/(\alpha\rho_{\text{NIR}}+\rho_{\text{red}})\) with α = 0.1–0.2, whose sensitivity at LAI 2–6 is at least three times that of NDVI (Gitelson, 2004). (3) Combine optical data with radar, whose signal responds to canopy structure and biomass (Section 11.2.7). (4) Invert a physical canopy model rather than a single index, using all bands at once.

Common misconception

"An NDVI of 0.90 is better than 0.85, so the crop in that zone is 6 % better." Near saturation, a difference of 0.05 in NDVI can correspond to a large difference in biomass — or to no difference at all but a slightly darker soil, a different view angle or residual haze. Always ask whether the index is still in its sensitive range and whether the difference is larger than the noise.

Platforms and the four resolutions

Choosing a data source is choosing a compromise between four kinds of resolution:

  • Spatial resolution — the size of the ground area represented by one pixel, the ground sampling distance (GSD). Fine enough to resolve the variation you want to manage.
  • Spectral resolution — the number, position and width of the bands. Red-edge and SWIR bands add information about chlorophyll and water that red and NIR alone cannot give.
  • Temporal resolution — how often the same place is observed (revisit time), and in practice how often a cloud-free observation is obtained.
  • Radiometric resolution — how finely the signal is quantised (bits) and how large the signal is compared with the sensor noise. Sentinel-2 records 12-bit data (4096 levels); Landsat 9 records 14 bits (Masek et al., 2020).

No platform is best on all four. Satellites cover whole regions consistently but coarsely; drones are fine but cover small areas at high cost per hectare; proximal sensors on the tractor measure exactly where the fertiliser is applied, at the moment it is applied, but only along the tramlines.

From radiance to reliable maps: atmosphere, clouds and radar

Atmospheric correction

The atmosphere affects an image twice (Figure 1). Gases absorb in known bands — water vapour near 945 and 1375 nm, oxygen at 760 nm — and molecules and aerosols scatter light, mostly at short wavelengths. Scattering adds path radiance, which brightens dark targets. For vegetation the red band is dark, so haze raises red reflectance proportionally much more than NIR, and NDVI drops. A simple numerical check shows how large the effect can be: a crop with surface reflectance 0.03 (red) and 0.45 (NIR) has NDVI 0.875; add a haze contribution of 0.02 in the red and 0.01 in the NIR and the apparent NDVI becomes \((0.46-0.05)/(0.46+0.05)=0.80\) — a "loss" of 0.07 that is entirely atmospheric.

Atmospheric correction converts top-of-atmosphere reflectance (Eq. 11.2.1; Sentinel-2 Level-1C) into surface reflectance (Level-2A). ESA's Sen2Cor processor estimates aerosol load from dark, dense vegetation and water vapour from the 945 nm band, detects thin cirrus with the 1375 nm band, runs a radiative-transfer model, and also produces a scene classification that flags clouds, cloud shadows, snow, water and vegetation (Main-Knorn et al., 2017). The oldest and simplest method, dark-object subtraction, assumes that the darkest pixels in a scene (deep clear water, dense shadow) should have near-zero reflectance, and subtracts their apparent value — an estimate of path radiance — from every pixel (Chavez, 1988). For precision agriculture the lesson is simple: use Level-2A surface reflectance, and treat index changes of a few hundredths between dates with suspicion unless they are consistent across the field.

Clouds in a Nordic growing season

Optical sensors cannot see through clouds, and clouds are common exactly when agricultural decisions are made. Using ten years of twice-daily MODIS cloud flags, Whitcraft et al. (2015) showed that the early and middle parts of the growing season are cloudy over many important agricultural regions, to the point that in some areas and periods less than half of all 8-day periods yield even a 70 % clear view; they concluded that radar should be considered wherever optical monitoring is unreliable. In Sweden the window for the second nitrogen dose to winter wheat is only two or three weeks long, so the question "will I get at least one usable image in time?" is a probability question:

Radar: Sentinel-1 sees through clouds

A synthetic aperture radar (SAR) sends microwave pulses obliquely to the ground and records the echo. Sentinel-1 operates in the C band at 5.405 GHz, i.e. a wavelength \(\lambda=c/f\approx 5.5\) cm, which passes through clouds and needs no sunlight (Torres et al., 2012). The echo strength is expressed as the backscatter coefficient \(\sigma^0\), usually in decibels, \(\sigma^0_{\text{dB}}=10\log_{10}\sigma^0\) (so −18 dB means \(\sigma^0 = 0.016\)). It depends on surface roughness, soil moisture (through the dielectric constant of water) and canopy structure. Sentinel-1 transmits vertically polarised waves and receives both vertical (VV) and horizontal (VH) echoes. Multiple scattering inside a canopy depolarises the wave, so VH rises as biomass accumulates; the ratio VH/VV tracks crop growth in a way that complements optical indices (Veloso et al., 2017). Radar images are noisy ("speckle") and harder to interpret, but their reliability under Scandinavian skies makes them a valuable partner of optical data. After Sentinel-1B failed at the end of 2021, Sentinel-1C (launched December 2024) and Sentinel-1D (November 2025) restored the two-satellite constellation.

Thermal remote sensing and crop water stress

Transpiration cools leaves: evaporating one kilogram of water absorbs about 2.45 MJ of latent heat. When soil water runs short, stomata close, transpiration falls and the canopy warms up — often before any change in colour or leaf area is visible. Thermal cameras (8–14 µm) measure the long-wave radiation emitted by the canopy; with an emissivity of about 0.98 for leaves, this can be converted into canopy temperature using the Stefan–Boltzmann law (see Lesson 2.2 for the leaf energy balance). Drones with thermal cameras resolve individual plants and rows (Maes & Steppe, 2019); Landsat's thermal bands (100 m) resolve fields.

Canopy temperature alone is ambiguous: a crop is warmer on a hot, dry, calm afternoon whether or not it is stressed. Idso et al. (1981) and Jackson et al. (1981) normalised it by comparing the canopy–air temperature difference with two limits: the lower baseline of a well-watered crop transpiring at the potential rate (which becomes colder relative to the air as the vapour pressure deficit increases), and the upper limit of a crop that does not transpire at all.

Crop water stress index (reference-surface form)(11.2.9)
\[ \text{CWSI} = \frac{T_c - T_{\text{wet}}}{T_{\text{dry}} - T_{\text{wet}}}\;\approx\; 1-\frac{E}{E_p} \]

the crop water stress index is the canopy temperature minus the temperature of a fully transpiring reference, divided by the temperature range between a non-transpiring and a fully transpiring reference. It approximately equals one minus the ratio of actual to potential evapotranspiration.

  1. The leaf energy balance (Lesson 2.2) gives canopy temperature as a function of net radiation, wind, vapour pressure deficit and stomatal conductance. With all weather variables fixed, canopy temperature rises monotonically as conductance — and thus transpiration — falls.

  3. Scaling the measured temperature linearly between them gives an index from 0 to 1. Jackson et al. (1981) showed from the Penman–Monteith equation that this index equals \(1-E/E_p\).

At high latitudes, lower radiation and VPD make the temperature signal smaller than in the arid climates where the method was developed, so the reference-surface approach is preferred. CWSI detects stress; it does not say how much water to apply — that requires the water balance of Lesson 11.3.

From indices to LAI, biomass and yield

Farmers and advisers need agronomic quantities — leaf area, nitrogen uptake, biomass, expected yield — not index values. There are three routes from reflectance to these quantities (Weiss et al., 2020): empirical regressions fitted to field samples (simple, but local); physical models of leaf and canopy reflectance that are inverted to find the LAI and chlorophyll that best reproduce the observed spectrum (general, but demanding); and machine learning trained on large datasets or on simulated spectra (powerful, but only as good as the training data). A transparent semi-physical chain, useful for understanding and for quick estimates, links NDVI to cover, cover to LAI, LAI to absorbed radiation and absorbed radiation to growth:

Frontier (2024–2026)

Three trends are changing agricultural remote sensing: (1) fusion of Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical time series with machine learning, so that cloudy periods no longer interrupt monitoring; (2) data assimilation, in which satellite estimates of LAI continuously correct the state of a crop-growth model driven by weather data, turning snapshots into forecasts (compare the digital twin of Lesson 10.5); and (3) very frequent, very fine imagery from commercial constellations and drones that approaches the scale of individual plants. The bottleneck is increasingly not data but calibrated agronomic interpretation (Weiss et al., 2020).

A pixel whose area contains more than one surface type; its reflectance is approximately the area-weighted mean of the components.
NDVI
Normalised Difference Vegetation Index, (NIR − red)/(NIR + red); increases with green biomass but saturates in dense canopies.
SAVI
Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Index, (1 + L)(NIR − red)/(NIR + red + L), which reduces the influence of soil brightness in sparse canopies.
EVI
Enhanced Vegetation Index, which uses the blue band to resist aerosol effects and remains sensitive in dense canopies.
NDRE
Normalised Difference Red-Edge index, (NIR − red edge)/(NIR + red edge); sensitive to canopy chlorophyll and nitrogen status.
NDWI
Normalised Difference Water Index; in Gao's form (NIR − SWIR)/(NIR + SWIR) it indicates vegetation water content, in McFeeters' form (green − NIR)/(green + NIR) it maps open water.
Ground sampling distance
The distance on the ground between the centres of adjacent pixels, GSD = Hp/f for a frame camera.
Revisit time
The interval between successive observations of the same place by a satellite or constellation; the useful interval is longer because of clouds.
Atmospheric correction
Removing the effects of atmospheric absorption and scattering to convert top-of-atmosphere reflectance into surface reflectance.
Synthetic aperture radar (SAR)
An active microwave imaging system, such as Sentinel-1, that works through clouds and at night and responds to surface roughness, moisture and canopy structure.
Crop water stress index (CWSI)
A thermal index that scales canopy temperature between a fully transpiring and a non-transpiring reference; approximately 1 − E/Eₚ.

  • I can explain, with the physics of pigments, cell structure and water, why a healthy crop is dark in the red, bright in the NIR and darker again in the SWIR.
  • I can calculate NDVI, SAVI, EVI, NDRE, GNDVI and NDWI from band reflectances and say which index I would choose for a sparse, a dense and a water-stressed crop.
  • I can show with numbers why NDVI saturates and what that means for the uncertainty of an LAI estimate.
  • I can plan a drone survey (GSD, footprint, line spacing, flight time) and compare it with free satellite data.
  • I can estimate the chance of getting a cloud-free image in a decision window and explain when radar or proximal sensors are the better choice.
  • I can take a vegetation index through LAI and absorbed radiation to a biomass estimate and state the main assumptions.
