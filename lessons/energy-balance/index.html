Energy balances of greenhouses and plant factories

On a January night in Västerås a hectare of glass loses heat to a sky that radiates like a surface at −20 °C, and the grower pays for every watt of it. On the same night, a few kilometres away, a sealed plant factory full of LEDs has the opposite problem: it must pump heat out, even though it is freezing outside. Both buildings obey the same law — energy is conserved — and in this lesson you will learn to write that law down for a greenhouse and for a plant factory, put numbers into every term, and find out where each kilowatt-hour really goes.

What you will be able to do

  • Identify the four ways heat moves in a cultivation system — conduction, convection, thermal radiation and latent heat — and calculate each with its governing equation (LO2, LO3).
  • Draw a control volume around a greenhouse and write its steady-state energy balance with solar gain, heating, cover losses, ventilation and transpiration, keeping track of units (LO3).
  • Estimate the annual heating demand of a greenhouse in a Swedish climate with the degree-hour method and quantify the effect of screens, glazing and set-points (LO3, LO7).
  • Calculate the coefficient of performance of a heat pump, explain its Carnot limit, and compare heat pumps, boilers, combined heat and power and waste heat as heat sources (LO3, LO7).
  • Trace every kilowatt-hour of electricity through a plant factory, size its cooling load and the HVAC electricity, and compute kWh per kilogram of lettuce (LO2, LO3).
  • Interpret Sankey diagrams of greenhouse and plant-factory energy flows and distinguish total energy from purchased energy when comparing systems (LO7).

Every kilogram of lettuce is an energy balance

The first law of thermodynamics is the most useful single sentence an agricultural engineer can learn: energy is neither created nor destroyed, only converted and moved. Applied to a greenhouse, it says that every joule of sunlight and every joule of heating fuel that enters must either leave again — through the glass, through the vents, as water vapour — or be stored in the air, the soil, the structure and, to a tiny extent, in the chemical bonds of the crop. Applied to a plant factory, it says something even more striking: because the building is sealed and insulated, practically every kilowatt-hour of electricity that you buy for the lamps must later be removed again as heat by the air-conditioning system, which itself needs electricity.

This is why energy dominates the economics and the climate footprint of controlled-environment agriculture (CEA). In the most widely cited modelling comparison, producing 1 kg of lettuce dry weight required about 247 kWh of purchased electricity in a plant factory, against about 70 kWh of purchased energy in a Dutch greenhouse, 111 kWh in the United Arab Emirates and 182–211 kWh in a greenhouse in northern Sweden (Kiruna, 67.8° N), depending on the supplementary-lighting scenario (Graamans et al., 2018). At the same time, the plant factory used its total energy — including free sunlight in the greenhouses — more efficiently (1411 against at best 1699 MJ per kg dry weight). Understanding why both statements are true is one of the goals of this lesson.

We proceed in four steps. First, the physics of heat transfer: the four mechanisms and their equations. Second, the control volume and the steady-state balance of a greenhouse, including the powerful degree-hour method for annual heating demand in the Västerås climate. Third, the technologies that supply heat — boilers, heat pumps, combined heat and power, waste heat — and the idea of the greenhouse as an energy producer. Fourth, the plant factory, where the balance is turned inside out. Sankey diagrams, in which the width of each arrow is proportional to the energy flow, tie everything together.

Big idea

An energy balance is bookkeeping: inputs − outputs = change in storage. Choose a boundary, list every flow that crosses it with a sign, express each flow with a physical equation and consistent units (watts per square metre of floor is the convention in greenhouse engineering), and the unknown — heating demand, ventilation rate, cooling load — follows from the arithmetic.

Four ways heat moves

Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. It always flows from warm to cold, and it does so by three physical mechanisms — conduction, convection and thermal radiation — plus a fourth "carrier" that is crucial in any system containing plants: latent heat, the energy absorbed when liquid water evaporates and released again when vapour condenses. Figure 1 shows all four acting on a single pane of greenhouse glass.

Conduction

Conduction is heat transfer by molecular collisions inside a solid (or a still fluid). Hot, fast-vibrating atoms pass energy to their slower neighbours. Joseph Fourier found in 1822 that the heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient:

Fourier's law of conduction (plane wall)(8.1.1)
\[ q_{\text{cond}} = -k\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_{\text{cond}} = \frac{k}{L}\,\left(T_1 - T_2\right) \]

the conductive heat flux through a flat layer equals the thermal conductivity of the material divided by its thickness, multiplied by the temperature difference between its two faces. The minus sign in the differential form says that heat flows down the temperature gradient.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(q_{\text{cond}}\)conductive heat flux densityW m⁻²—
\(k\)thermal conductivityW m⁻¹ K⁻¹glass ≈ 1.0; polycarbonate ≈ 0.2; still air 0.025; mineral wool 0.035; concrete 1.4
\(L\)thickness of the layermgreenhouse glass 0.004
\(T_1, T_2\)temperatures of the two faces°C or Ka difference is the same in both scales
Follow the derivation
  1. Fourier's law is empirical: experiments show that doubling the temperature difference or halving the thickness doubles the heat flow.
  2. In steady state no energy accumulates inside the slab, so the flux \(q\) is the same at every depth \(x\). Then \(\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}x = -q/k\) is constant: the temperature profile is a straight line.
  3. Integrate from face 1 (\(x=0\), \(T_1\)) to face 2 (\(x=L\), \(T_2\)): \(T_2 - T_1 = -qL/k\), hence \(q = k(T_1-T_2)/L\).
  4. It is convenient to define a thermal resistance per unit area, \(R = L/k\) (m² K W⁻¹), so that \(q = \Delta T/R\) — the thermal analogue of Ohm's law \(I = V/R\).
Assumptions and validity

One-dimensional heat flow, homogeneous material, constant \(k\), steady state. Glass is thin and conductive, so its resistance (0.004 m ÷ 1.0 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.004 m² K W⁻¹) is tiny compared with the surface resistances discussed below. That is why a single glass pane insulates so poorly — the glass itself hardly matters; the thin air films on each side do almost all the insulating.

\(\dfrac{\text{W m}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}}{\text{m}}\times\text{K} = \text{W m}^{-2}\) ✓

Convection

Convection is heat carried by a moving fluid. Next to any surface there is a thin boundary layer of slow-moving air across which heat must be conducted; outside it, the moving air carries the heat away. The details are complicated (they depend on wind speed, surface roughness and whether the flow is driven by buoyancy or by wind), so engineers lump them into a single convective heat-transfer coefficient:

Newton's law of cooling (convection)(8.1.2)
\[ q_{\text{conv}} = h_c\,\left(T_s - T_a\right) \]

the convective heat flux from a surface equals the convective heat-transfer coefficient times the difference between the surface temperature and the temperature of the surrounding air.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(h_c\)convective heat-transfer coefficientW m⁻² K⁻¹still indoor air 2–5; greenhouse inside 3–6; outside with wind 10–40
\(T_s\)surface temperature°C—
\(T_a\)air temperature far from the surface°C—
Where does \(h_c\) come from?
  1. Heat crosses the boundary layer (thickness \(\delta\), a few millimetres) by conduction: \(q \approx k_{\text{air}}(T_s - T_a)/\delta\). So \(h_c \approx k_{\text{air}}/\delta\).
  2. With \(k_{\text{air}} = 0.025\) W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ and \(\delta = 5\) mm, \(h_c \approx 5\) W m⁻² K⁻¹ — the order of magnitude of natural convection indoors.
  3. Wind thins the boundary layer. For a greenhouse roof a common empirical form is \(h_c \approx a + b\,u\), with \(u\) the wind speed (m s⁻¹); values of roughly 10 W m⁻² K⁻¹ in calm conditions to 30–40 W m⁻² K⁻¹ in a strong wind are typical (Stanghellini et al., 2019).
  4. Heat-transfer textbooks derive \(h_c\) from dimensionless correlations (Nusselt, Reynolds, Grashof numbers; Bergman et al., 2011) — beyond this course, but good to know that these numbers are not arbitrary.
W m⁻² K⁻¹ × K = W m⁻² ✓

Thermal radiation

Every surface emits electromagnetic radiation because of its temperature. At the temperatures of greenhouses, leaves and skies (roughly 250–310 K) the emission is in the thermal infrared, around 10 µm — "long-wave" radiation, invisible to us and to plants' photosynthesis, but a major heat-loss pathway. The energy emitted rises with the fourth power of absolute temperature (Stefan–Boltzmann law). For the net exchange between two large parallel surfaces (a cover and the sky, or a crop and a cover):

Net long-wave radiation exchange (Stefan–Boltzmann)(8.1.3)
\[ q_{\text{rad}} = \varepsilon\,\sigma\left(T_1^{4} - T_2^{4}\right) \approx h_r\,(T_1 - T_2), \qquad h_r = 4\,\varepsilon\,\sigma\,\bar{T}^{3} \]

the net radiant flux from surface 1 to surface 2 equals the emissivity times the Stefan–Boltzmann constant times the difference of the fourth powers of their absolute temperatures. For small temperature differences it can be linearised into a "radiative heat-transfer coefficient" that multiplies an ordinary temperature difference.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(\sigma\)Stefan–Boltzmann constantW m⁻² K⁻⁴5.670 × 10⁻⁸
\(\varepsilon\)(effective) emissivity–glass ≈ 0.84–0.9; leaves 0.95–0.98; low-emissivity coatings 0.1–0.2
\(T_1, T_2\)absolute surface temperaturesK\(T\)(K) = \(T\)(°C) + 273.15
\(h_r\)radiative heat-transfer coefficientW m⁻² K⁻¹≈ 4–6 near 0–20 °C
\(\bar{T}\)mean of the two absolute temperaturesK—
Follow the derivation
  1. A perfect emitter (black body) at absolute temperature \(T\) emits \(\sigma T^4\) per unit area — the integral of Planck's spectrum over all wavelengths. A real surface emits \(\varepsilon\sigma T^4\) with \(0 < \varepsilon \le 1\).
  2. Surface 1 emits \(\varepsilon\sigma T_1^4\) towards surface 2 and absorbs (Kirchhoff: absorptivity = emissivity) what surface 2 emits towards it. For large parallel black-ish surfaces the net exchange is \(\varepsilon\sigma(T_1^4 - T_2^4)\). (With two grey surfaces the exact effective emissivity is \(1/(1/\varepsilon_1 + 1/\varepsilon_2 - 1)\).)
  3. Factorise: \(T_1^4 - T_2^4 = (T_1 - T_2)(T_1+T_2)(T_1^2+T_2^2) \approx (T_1 - T_2)\cdot 4\bar{T}^3\) when \(T_1 \approx T_2\). This gives \(h_r = 4\varepsilon\sigma\bar{T}^3\).
  4. At \(\bar{T} = 264\) K (−9 °C) and \(\varepsilon = 0.9\): \(h_r = 4\times0.9\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times264^3 = 3.8\) W m⁻² K⁻¹ — comparable to convection, so radiation can never be ignored.
Assumptions and validity: the cold sky

The sky is not a surface, but it can be assigned an "effective sky temperature" that reproduces the long-wave radiation it sends down. Under a clear sky this is typically 10–30 K below air temperature, because the dry upper atmosphere is cold and partly transparent in the 8–13 µm "window" (Swinbank, 1963; Monteith & Unsworth, 2013). Clouds are warm, nearly black emitters, which is why clear winter nights are the coldest and the most expensive for a greenhouse. Glass is opaque to long-wave radiation (it absorbs it), whereas thin polyethylene film transmits a good part of it — one reason why film greenhouses cool faster on clear nights.

W m⁻² K⁻⁴ × K⁴ = W m⁻² ✓  ·  \(h_r\): W m⁻² K⁻⁴ × K³ = W m⁻² K⁻¹ ✓

Latent heat

Evaporating one kilogram of water at 20 °C takes 2.45 MJ — enough energy to warm 2000 m³ of air by 1 K. A crop that transpires 3 L of water per square metre on a sunny day therefore converts more than 7 MJ m⁻² of absorbed solar energy into latent heat, energy hidden in water vapour that does not raise the air temperature. It reappears as sensible heat wherever the vapour condenses — on a cold glass roof, on the cooling coil of a plant factory, or outdoors after it has been vented.

Latent heat flux of transpiration(8.1.4)
\[ \lambda E = \lambda(T)\,\frac{E_h}{3600}, \qquad \lambda(T) \approx 2.501\times10^{6} - 2361\,T \]

the latent heat flux (in watts per square metre) equals the latent heat of vaporisation of water multiplied by the evaporation rate, converted from kilograms per square metre per hour to per second. The latent heat decreases slightly with temperature.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(\lambda E\)latent heat flux densityW m⁻²0–500 in a greenhouse
\(\lambda(T)\)latent heat of vaporisation at temperature \(T\) (°C)J kg⁻¹2.501 × 10⁶ at 0 °C; 2.454 × 10⁶ at 20 °C
\(E_h\)transpiration (evaporation) ratekg m⁻² h⁻¹ (= L m⁻² h⁻¹ = mm h⁻¹)0–0.7
Why is λ so large, and why does it fall with temperature?
  1. Evaporation breaks the hydrogen bonds that hold liquid water together. Each mole (18.0 g) needs about 44 kJ at 20 °C — roughly 2.45 MJ per kilogram.
  2. Warmer water is already closer to breaking free, so a little less extra energy is needed: λ decreases by about 2.4 kJ kg⁻¹ per kelvin. The linear fit above (used in physics.js) is accurate to better than 0.1 % between 0 and 40 °C.
  3. 1 mm of water over 1 m² is 1 L = 1 kg. Evaporating it at 20 °C takes 2.45 MJ m⁻²; spread over a 12-hour day that is an average of 57 W m⁻².

Heat flow in series: the U-value

Heat leaving a greenhouse through the glass passes three resistances in series: the inside surface (convection plus long-wave radiation from crop and heating pipes), the glass itself (conduction) and the outside surface (wind convection plus radiation to the sky). Just as series electrical resistances add, thermal resistances add, and engineers summarise the whole chain by one number, the overall heat-transfer coefficient or U-value.

Overall heat-transfer coefficient of a cover(8.1.5)
\[ \frac{1}{U} = \frac{1}{h_i} + \sum_j \frac{L_j}{k_j} + \frac{1}{h_o}, \qquad q_{\text{cover}} = U\,\left(T_i - T_o\right) \]

the reciprocal of the U-value is the sum of the thermal resistances in series: the inside surface resistance, the conduction resistance of each solid layer, and the outside surface resistance. The heat flux through the cover is then the U-value times the inside–outside air temperature difference.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(U\)overall heat-transfer coefficient (per m² of cover)W m⁻² K⁻¹single glass 6–7.5; double glazing 3–4
\(h_i\)inside surface coefficient (convection + radiation)W m⁻² K⁻¹≈ 7–9
\(h_o\)outside surface coefficient (wind + sky radiation)W m⁻² K⁻¹≈ 15–40
\(L_j / k_j\)conduction resistance of layer \(j\)m² K W⁻¹4 mm glass: 0.004
\(T_i, T_o\)inside and outside air temperature°C—
Follow the derivation
  1. In steady state the same flux \(q\) passes each layer (nothing accumulates). Across the inside film: \(T_i - T_{s,i} = q/h_i\). Across the glass: \(T_{s,i} - T_{s,o} = qL/k\). Across the outside film: \(T_{s,o} - T_o = q/h_o\).
  2. Add the three equations; the unknown surface temperatures cancel: \(T_i - T_o = q\,(1/h_i + L/k + 1/h_o)\).
  3. Define \(U\) through \(q = U(T_i - T_o)\); comparison gives the formula above. A double-glazed unit adds the resistance of the enclosed air gap (convection and radiation across the gap, about 0.15–0.2 m² K W⁻¹), which roughly halves \(U\).
Assumptions and validity

Steady state and one-dimensional flow. In a real greenhouse, \(U\) is not a material constant: it rises with wind speed, falls when a screen is closed, changes when condensate covers the glass (water films are strongly emitting) and depends on the sky temperature, because radiation to the sky is part of the outside coefficient. Engineering handbooks therefore quote "effective" U-values measured on whole greenhouses (e.g., ASABE EP406; Stanghellini et al., 2019). Always state the conditions a U-value refers to.

\(1/U\): (W m⁻² K⁻¹)⁻¹ + m ÷ (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) = m² K W⁻¹ ✓  ·  \(q\): W m⁻² K⁻¹ × K = W m⁻² ✓
Worked example 1The U-value of single glass — and the sky

A greenhouse roof is 4 mm float glass (\(k\) = 1.0 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹). Take \(h_i\) = 7.5 W m⁻² K⁻¹ and, for a moderate wind, \(h_o\) = 25 W m⁻² K⁻¹. (a) Calculate the U-value. (b) On a clear night the outer glass surface is at 2 °C and the effective sky temperature is −20 °C. How much of the outside heat loss is radiation? Use \(\varepsilon\) = 0.9.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. List the resistances (m² K W⁻¹): inside film \(1/7.5 = 0.1333\); glass \(0.004/1.0 = 0.0040\); outside film \(1/25 = 0.0400\).
  2. Add them: \(R_{\text{tot}} = 0.1333 + 0.0040 + 0.0400 = 0.1773\) m² K W⁻¹, so \(U = 1/0.1773 = 5.64\) W m⁻² K⁻¹.
  3. Interpret: the glass contributes only 2 % of the resistance; 75 % sits in the inside air film. Thicker glass would barely help; an extra air layer (double glazing or a screen) helps enormously.
  4. Radiation to the sky (Eq. 8.1.3): \(q = 0.9\times5.670\times10^{-8}\times(275.15^4 - 253.15^4)\). The fourth powers are \(5.732\times10^{9}\) and \(4.107\times10^{9}\) K⁴; the difference is \(1.625\times10^{9}\) K⁴, so \(q = 82.9\) W m⁻².
  5. Sanity check: if the outside air were −5 °C, convection would remove \(25\times(2-(-5))\approx 175\) W m⁻² with \(h_o\) = 25 — but note that the 25 W m⁻² K⁻¹ we assumed already lumps together wind and sky radiation. Radiation is thus a large share (tens of per cent) of the outside loss on a clear night, which is why U-values measured on clear, windy nights are the highest.
U ≈ 5.6 W m⁻² K⁻¹; radiation to a −20 °C sky ≈ 83 W m⁻²

The greenhouse as a control volume

A control volume is an imaginary closed boundary that you draw around the system you want to analyse. For a greenhouse we draw it just outside the cover and just below the soil surface, and we express every flux per square metre of floor area (W m⁻²) so that greenhouses of different sizes can be compared. Figure 2 shows the terms.

Writing inputs on the left and outputs on the right gives the steady-state greenhouse energy balance. "Steady state" means we assume that the temperatures do not change over the period considered, so the storage term is zero — a good approximation over an hour at night or when we average over a day.

Steady-state energy balance of a greenhouse(8.1.6)
\[ \underbrace{\tau\,I}_{\text{solar gain}} + \underbrace{Q_{\text{heat}}}_{\text{heating}} = \underbrace{U\,\frac{A_c}{A_f}\,(T_i - T_o)}_{\text{cover}} + \underbrace{\rho\,c_p\,g\,(T_i - T_o)}_{\text{ventilation (sensible)}} + \underbrace{\lambda E}_{\text{transpiration}} + \underbrace{G}_{\text{soil}} \]

the transmitted solar radiation plus the heat supplied by the heating system must equal the heat lost through the cover, the sensible heat carried away by ventilation and leakage, the latent heat used to evaporate water from the crop, and the heat conducted into the soil — all per square metre of floor.

\(I\)global solar irradiance outside (horizontal)W m⁻²0–900 in Sweden
\(\tau\)transmissivity of the greenhouse for solar radiation–0.6–0.8
\(Q_{\text{heat}}\)heat delivered by the heating systemW m⁻²0–250
\(A_c / A_f\)cover area per floor area–1.2–1.4 for multi-span Venlo; larger for small houses
\(\rho\)density of airkg m⁻³1.2
\(c_p\)specific heat of air at constant pressureJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹1005
\(g\)ventilation rate per floor aream³ m⁻² s⁻¹see Eq. 8.1.7
\(\lambda E\)latent heat flux of the cropW m⁻²often ≈ 0.4–0.7 × \(\tau I\) by day
\(G\)soil heat fluxW m⁻²small; often neglected
Follow the derivation
  1. Start from the first law for an open system: \(\mathrm{d}E_{\text{stored}}/\mathrm{d}t = \sum \text{inflows} - \sum\text{outflows}\).
  2. Inflows: solar radiation that passes the cover, \(\tau I\) (the rest, \((1-\tau)I\), is reflected or absorbed by glass and structure — the absorbed part mostly leaves again from the outside surface); heat from pipes or air heaters, \(Q_{\text{heat}}\).
  3. Outflows: conduction–convection–radiation through the cover, Eq. 8.1.5, multiplied by the ratio of cover area to floor area because we express everything per m² of floor; the enthalpy carried by air leaving through vents and cracks (Eq. 8.1.7); latent heat in transpired vapour; conduction into the soil.
  4. Steady state: the storage term vanishes and inflows equal outflows. The equation can be solved for whichever term is unknown — usually \(Q_{\text{heat}}\) (night, winter) or the ventilation rate \(g\) (sunny day).
Assumptions and validity

Well-mixed air at one temperature \(T_i\); no heat storage (not valid at sunrise and sunset, when the thermal mass of soil, water in pipes and crop matters); latent heat of transpired water leaves the control volume either through the vents or by condensing on the cover (in which case it is released there and partly lost through the cover). Radiation from the crop to the cold sky through the cover is lumped into \(U\). For design calculations these simplifications are standard; dynamic greenhouse climate models solve the full set of storage terms every few minutes (see the greenhouse climate simulator).

Every term is W m⁻²: e.g. kg m⁻³ × J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ × m³ m⁻² s⁻¹ × K = J s⁻¹ m⁻² = W m⁻² ✓

The ventilation term needs one more equation. Ventilation is usually specified as an air exchange rate \(N\): the number of times per hour that the greenhouse air volume is replaced. A closed modern glasshouse still leaks at 0.2–1 h⁻¹ through cracks and gaps; with vents wide open on a summer day, 20–60 h⁻¹ is common.

Sensible heat loss by ventilation and infiltration(8.1.7)
\[ Q_{\text{vent}} = \rho\,c_p\,g\,(T_i - T_o), \qquad g = \frac{N\,V}{3600\,A_f} = \frac{N\,\bar{h}}{3600} \]

the sensible heat carried away by ventilation equals air density times specific heat times the volumetric air-flow rate per square metre of floor times the inside–outside temperature difference. The air-flow rate per floor area equals the air exchange rate times the mean height of the greenhouse, divided by 3600 seconds per hour.

\(N\)air exchange rateh⁻¹0.2–1 (closed, leakage); up to 60 (vents open)
\(V\)air volume of the greenhousem³—
\(\bar{h} = V/A_f\)mean height (volume per floor area)m4–7 for modern glasshouses

Follow the derivation
  1. Each second, a volume \(NV/3600\) of inside air (temperature \(T_i\)) is replaced by the same volume of outside air (\(T_o\)).
  2. The mass exchanged per second is \(\rho NV/3600\); warming each kilogram of incoming air from \(T_o\) to \(T_i\) takes \(c_p(T_i - T_o)\) joules.
  3. Multiply and divide by the floor area to get a flux per m² of floor: \(\rho c_p (NV/3600A_f)(T_i - T_o)\).
  4. The latent part — vapour carried out — is treated separately (it is the transpiration term in Eq. 8.1.6, or more exactly the difference in enthalpy of inside and outside air; see Lesson 8.2).

Worked example 2Heating a hectare on a January night

A 1.0 ha Venlo glasshouse near Västerås has a cover area of 12 500 m² (\(A_c/A_f\) = 1.25) and a mean height of 5.5 m. On a clear January night the outside temperature is −10 °C and the set-point is 18 °C. The effective U-value of the single glass is 6.0 W m⁻² K⁻¹ and air leakage is 0.5 h⁻¹. Transpiration and soil heat flux are negligible at night. (a) What heating power is needed? (b) What if an energy screen is closed, lowering the effective U-value to 3.6 W m⁻² K⁻¹?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Temperature difference: \(\Delta T = 18 - (-10) = 28\) K. No sun: \(\tau I = 0\).
  2. Cover loss: \(U A_c \Delta T = 6.0 \times 12\,500 \times 28 = 2.10\times10^{6}\) W = 2.10 MW.
  3. Leakage: \(V = 10\,000 \times 5.5 = 55\,000\) m³; flow \(= 0.5\times55\,000/3600 = 7.64\) m³ s⁻¹. Loss \(= 1.2\times1005\times7.64\times28 = 2.58\times10^{5}\) W = 0.26 MW.
  4. Total: \(Q_{\text{heat}} = 2.10 + 0.26 = 2.36\) MW, i.e. 236 W per m² of floor. The cover dominates (89 %).
  5. With the screen: cover loss \(= 3.6\times12\,500\times28 = 1.26\) MW; total 1.52 MW (152 W m⁻²) — a 36 % saving while the screen is closed.
  6. Sanity check: 2.36 MW for one hectare is the output of a sizeable boiler; burning natural gas (≈ 8.8 kWh per m³) at 95 % efficiency, that is about 280 m³ of gas per hour. Greenhouse heating systems in cold climates are indeed designed for 200–300 W m⁻² of floor.
≈ 2.4 MW (236 W m⁻²) without screen; ≈ 1.5 MW (152 W m⁻²) with the screen closed

By day the balance flips. Sunlight pours in; part of it drives transpiration (latent heat), the rest heats crop and air. If the sensible remainder exceeds what the cover can lose, the grower must open the vents. Solving Eq. 8.1.6 for \(g\) gives the ventilation needed to hold the set-point:

Worked example 3How far must the vents open on a sunny April day?

The same greenhouse (\(\tau\) = 0.70, \(U\) = 6.0 W m⁻² K⁻¹, \(A_c/A_f\) = 1.25, \(\bar h\) = 5.5 m) receives \(I\) = 600 W m⁻² at noon in April. Outside it is 10 °C; the set-point for ventilation is 24 °C. Assume half of the transmitted radiation is used for transpiration. What air exchange rate is needed, and how much water does the crop transpire per hour?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Solar gain: \(\tau I = 0.70\times600 = 420\) W m⁻².
  2. Split: latent \(\lambda E = 0.5\times420 = 210\) W m⁻²; sensible heat available = 210 W m⁻².
  3. Cover loss: \(6.0\times1.25\times(24-10) = 105\) W m⁻². No heating: \(Q_{\text{heat}} = 0\).
  4. Ventilation must remove the rest: \(210 - 105 = 105\) W m⁻² \(= \rho c_p g\,\Delta T\) → \(g = 105/(1.2\times1005\times14) = 6.22\times10^{-3}\) m³ m⁻² s⁻¹.
  5. Air exchange rate: \(N = 3600\,g/\bar h = 3600\times0.00622/5.5 = 4.1\) h⁻¹.
  6. Transpiration: \(E = \lambda E/\lambda = 210/(2.444\times10^6)\) kg m⁻² s⁻¹ \(= 8.6\times10^{-5}\) kg m⁻² s⁻¹ = 0.31 L m⁻² h⁻¹.
  7. Sanity check: 0.31 L m⁻² h⁻¹ over the middle of a spring day is realistic for a full tomato canopy. Notice that transpiration removed as much heat as the cover and the vents together: the crop is the greenhouse's most important air conditioner.
N ≈ 4 air changes per hour; transpiration ≈ 0.3 L m⁻² h⁻¹

Covers, screens and the light–insulation dilemma

Because the cover dominates heat loss, the cover is where energy is saved — but every layer that insulates also absorbs and reflects light, and in northern Europe light is the factor that limits winter production most. A widely used horticultural rule of thumb is that 1 % less light costs roughly 1 % of yield for light-limited crops. The trade-off between insulation (low \(U\)) and transmission (high \(\tau\)) is the central design problem of greenhouse covers.

Energy screens are thin fabrics (often polyester strips, transparent or with aluminium strips) drawn horizontally at gutter height at night and on cold, dark days. Common misconception

"The U-value is a property of the glass." It is not: as Worked example 1 showed, the glass itself contributes about 2 % of the thermal resistance of a single pane. The U-value is a property of the whole cover in its environment — wind, sky, condensation and screens change it by a factor of two. Comparing covers is only meaningful under the same reference conditions.

Annual heating demand in a Swedish climate

Designing a heating system requires the peak load (Worked example 2); estimating fuel cost and carbon footprint requires the annual heat demand. The simplest reliable method sums the temperature deficit over all hours of the year. If the greenhouse is kept at \(T_{\text{set}}\) and the outside temperature is \(T_o(t)\), the heating degree-hours are \(\text{DH} = \sum_t \max\{0,\,T_{\text{set}} - T_o(t)\}\,\Delta t\).

Annual heating demand by the degree-hour method(8.1.8)
\[ Q_{\text{year}} = \frac{K\cdot \text{DH}}{1000} - \eta_s\,\tau\,H_{\text{heating season}}, \qquad K = U\,\frac{A_c}{A_f} + \frac{\rho\,c_p\,N\,\bar h}{3600} \]

the annual heat demand in kilowatt-hours per square metre of floor equals the total heat-loss coefficient times the heating degree-hours, divided by 1000 to convert watt-hours to kilowatt-hours, minus the part of the transmitted solar radiation that usefully offsets heating. The heat-loss coefficient combines cover losses and ventilation or leakage.

Follow the derivation
  1. At night, Eq. 8.1.6 with \(\tau I = \lambda E = G = 0\) gives \(Q_{\text{heat}} = K(T_i - T_o)\), with \(K\) collecting the cover and leakage terms.
  2. Integrate over time: energy = power × time. Summing \(K\max(0, T_{\text{set}}-T_o)\) over all hours gives \(K\cdot\text{DH}\) in W h m⁻²; divide by 1000 for kWh m⁻².
  3. By day, sunlight supplies part of the heat. Much of it arrives when no heating is needed (it is vented), and part is used for transpiration, so only a fraction \(\eta_s\) of the transmitted radiation reduces the heating bill. Month by month: \(Q_m = \max\{0,\, K\,\text{DH}_m/1000 - \eta_s\tau H_m\}\).
  4. With monthly mean temperatures instead of hourly data, \(\text{DH}_m \approx 24\,n_m\max(0, T_{\text{set}}-\bar T_m)\). This underestimates the demand in spring and autumn, when warm afternoons hide cold nights; hourly data are better when available.
Assumptions and validity

Constant \(K\) (in reality \(U\) varies with wind and screens); a single set-point day and night (growers often use lower night temperatures); thermal storage ignored; solar utilisation represented by one factor. The method is excellent for comparing options (screen or no screen, 16 °C or 20 °C) and typically within 10–20 % of detailed simulation for the absolute value.

Where the heat comes from: boilers, heat pumps, CHP and waste heat

Once the demand is known, the question becomes how to supply it — at what cost and with what carbon footprint. Four families of heat sources dominate.

Boilers burn natural gas, oil, wood chips or pellets and transfer 90–105 % of the fuel's lower heating value to water (the figure can exceed 100 % for condensing boilers because the lower heating value excludes the latent heat of the water vapour in the flue gas, which a condensing boiler partly recovers). Natural gas has the practical advantage that clean flue gas can be used for CO₂ enrichment (Lesson 8.3), but it is fossil: 56.1 kg CO₂ per GJ burned (IPCC, 2006).

Heat pumps do not create heat; they move it from a cold source (outdoor air, a lake, the ground, an aquifer, a cooling coil) to a warmer sink, using mechanical work. Figure 5 shows the cycle.

Coefficient of performance and the Carnot limit(8.1.9)
\[ \text{COP}_{h} = \frac{Q_h}{W} = \frac{Q_c + W}{W}, \qquad \text{COP}_{h,\text{Carnot}} = \frac{T_h}{T_h - T_c}, \qquad \text{COP}_{c} = \text{COP}_{h} - 1 \]

the heating coefficient of performance is the useful heat delivered divided by the electrical work consumed. It can never exceed the Carnot value, the hot-side absolute temperature divided by the temperature lift. For cooling, the useful effect is the heat removed from the cold side, which is one unit less than the heating COP.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(Q_h\)heat delivered to the warm sideJ or W—
\(Q_c\)heat taken from the cold sideJ or W—
\(W\)electrical (mechanical) workJ or W—
\(T_h, T_c\)absolute temperatures of sink and sourceKe.g. 318 K and 278 K
\(\text{COP}_h\)heating COP of a real machine–≈ 2.5–5 (≈ 40–60 % of Carnot)
Follow the derivation
  1. First law for the machine (a cycle stores no energy): \(Q_h = Q_c + W\).
  2. Second law: the entropy the refrigerant takes from the cold side, \(Q_c/T_c\), cannot exceed the entropy it gives to the warm side, \(Q_h/T_h\). For an ideal reversible (Carnot) machine they are equal: \(Q_c/T_c = Q_h/T_h\).
  3. Substitute \(Q_c = Q_h T_c/T_h\) into the first law: \(W = Q_h(1 - T_c/T_h)\), so \(\text{COP}_h = Q_h/W = T_h/(T_h - T_c)\).
  4. The key insight: the COP depends on the temperature lift \(T_h - T_c\). Delivering heat at 45 °C from a 5 °C lake: \(318.15/40 = 7.95\). From −5 °C outdoor air to 55 °C: \(328.15/60 = 5.47\). Low-temperature heating systems and warm sources (waste heat!) make heat pumps efficient.
  5. Real machines lose to friction, finite heat-exchanger temperature differences and compressor inefficiency; their COP is typically 40–60 % of the Carnot value.

Combined heat and power (CHP) units — in greenhouses usually gas engines driving generators — convert roughly 40 % of the fuel energy into electricity and 45–50 % into usable heat (from the engine cooling water and the exhaust), with total efficiencies around 85–90 %. The flue gas, after catalytic cleaning of nitrogen oxides and ethylene, supplies CO₂ for enrichment. In the Netherlands this triple use — electricity, heat and CO₂ — made CHP the standard heating technology of large greenhouse firms from the 1990s onward. Growers run the engines when electricity prices are high, sell surplus power to the grid, and store the heat in large insulated water tanks for use at night. In that sense the greenhouse sector became an energy producer for the electricity market; the obvious drawback is that the fuel is still natural gas.

A second, more radical sense of "greenhouse as energy producer" comes from the closed greenhouse. On a sunny summer day a greenhouse receives far more solar energy than it needs; normally the surplus is vented. A closed greenhouse keeps its vents shut, removes the surplus heat with heat exchangers and stores it — typically as warm water in an aquifer tens of metres underground — and returns it in winter with a heat pump (Figure 6). Dutch demonstration projects in the early 2000s showed that such a greenhouse can harvest more heat in summer than it needs in winter, while keeping CO₂ high and humidity controlled (Opdam et al., 2005; review in Vadiee & Martin, 2012). The Dutch innovation programme Kas als Energiebron ("greenhouse as a source of energy") grew out of this idea. The catch is investment cost and the electricity for pumps, fans and heat pumps; semi-closed designs that combine limited ventilation with heat recovery are more common today.

Waste heat is the Nordic speciality. Industrial processes, sewage treatment plants and — increasingly — data centres reject large amounts of low-temperature heat (typically 25–40 °C from air-cooled servers). In Finland, simulations of the Espoo district-heating network showed that data-centre waste heat of 20–60 MW could be used for more than 95 % of the operating hours and lower system costs (Wahlroos et al., 2017), and Nordic data-centre operators are planning or building heat recovery at scale (Wahlroos et al., 2018). Greenhouses and plant factories are attractive customers because they need low-temperature heat, they are often located outside cities, and — in the case of greenhouses — their demand is highest exactly when waste heat is abundant (winter nights). The low temperature of the source is the main obstacle; a heat pump that lifts 30 °C waste heat to 45 °C has a very high COP (Eq. 8.1.9), so the combination works well. Iceland has taken the geothermal equivalent furthest: greenhouses there have been heated with hot spring water for about a century.

Worked example 5Gas boiler or heat pump?

A 1 ha greenhouse needs 400 kWh m⁻² of heat per year (4.0 GWh). Option A: a condensing gas boiler, 95 % efficient. Option B: a water-source heat pump using lake water at 5 °C and delivering 45 °C, with a real COP of 50 % of Carnot. Compare the annual fuel or electricity use and the CO₂ emissions. Use 56.1 kg CO₂ per GJ of gas (IPCC, 2006). For the electricity, compare a low-carbon grid at 40 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹ and a coal-heavy grid at 820 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹ (life-cycle values of the kind reported by Schlömer et al., 2014).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Boiler fuel: \(4.0\times10^{6}/0.95 = 4.21\times10^{6}\) kWh of gas = \(4.21\times10^{6}\times3.6\) MJ = 15 160 GJ.
  2. Boiler CO₂: \(15\,160\ \text{GJ}\times56.1\ \text{kg GJ}^{-1} = 850\,000\) kg = 850 t CO₂ per year.
  3. Heat-pump COP: Carnot \(= 318.15/(45-5) = 7.95\); real \(= 0.5\times7.95 = 3.98\).
  4. Electricity: \(4.0\times10^{6}/3.98 = 1.01\times10^{6}\) kWh per year (1.0 GWh). The other 3.0 GWh come free from the lake.
  5. Heat-pump CO₂: low-carbon grid \(1.01\times10^{6}\times0.040 = 40\) t; coal-heavy grid \(1.01\times10^{6}\times0.820 = 824\) t.
  6. Interpret: on a low-carbon grid such as Sweden's, the heat pump cuts emissions by about 95 %. On a coal grid it is no better than burning gas directly — the "where does the electricity come from?" question decides the answer.

Research frontier

As electricity systems decarbonise, greenhouse energy research (2024–2026) is moving from "save gas" to "use electricity when it is cheap and clean": large heat pumps coupled to thermal storage, electric boilers that absorb surplus wind power, and co-location with data centres, biogas plants and industrial parks. The analytical tools are exactly those of this lesson — energy balances with time-varying prices and emission factors — solved hour by hour.

The plant factory: every kilowatt-hour becomes heat

Draw a control volume around a well-insulated, sealed plant factory with artificial lighting (PFAL). No sunlight enters; the walls conduct little heat (U-values of 0.1–0.3 W m⁻² K⁻¹ for insulated sandwich panels); air leakage is tiny — Kozai (2013) gives 0.01–0.02 air exchanges per hour for a well-sealed facility. The only significant energy input is electricity. Where does it go?

  • Lamps. A modern LED fixture with a photon efficacy of 3 µmol J⁻¹ turns roughly 60 % of its electricity into PAR photons and 40 % into heat at the fixture (see Lesson 4.3).
  • Photons. Most are absorbed by leaves; some hit walls, trays and floor. Whatever is absorbed becomes heat — sensible (warming the leaf) or latent (evaporating water in transpiration) — except for the small fraction stored as chemical energy in sugars.
  • Pumps, fans, controls — all end as heat.
  • Transpired water carries latent heat into the air; it becomes sensible again when the vapour condenses on the cooling coil of the air-conditioner, where it is collected as clean water (Kozai, 2013; see Lesson 8.2).

How big is the chemical-energy fraction? Lettuce dry matter has a heat of combustion of roughly 17–18 kJ g⁻¹. With a light-use efficiency of 0.55 g of dry weight per mole of photons — the average reported for lettuce in vertical farms (Jin et al., 2023) — and a DLI of 14.4 mol m⁻² d⁻¹, the crop fixes about 7.9 g DW m⁻² d⁻¹, i.e. about 0.14 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹. The electricity input is about 6.6 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹ (Worked example 6). So only about 2 % of the electrical energy is stored in the harvest; Kozai (2013) estimated a fraction below 1 % for older fluorescent-lit facilities. Everything else must be removed by the HVAC system.

Energy balance and HVAC electricity of a plant factory(8.1.10)
\[ \underbrace{P_{\text{light}} + P_{\text{aux}}}_{\text{electricity in}} = \underbrace{Q_{\text{sens}} + Q_{\text{lat}}}_{\text{removed by HVAC}} + \underbrace{P_{\text{chem}}}_{\approx 1\text{-}3\,\%} + \underbrace{U_w A_w (T_i - T_o)}_{\text{envelope}}, \qquad P_{\text{HVAC}} = \frac{Q_{\text{sens}} + Q_{\text{lat}}}{\text{COP}_c} \]

the electricity supplied to lamps and auxiliary equipment ends up as sensible and latent heat that the air-conditioning system must remove, apart from the small part stored as chemical energy in the crop and the part conducted through the walls. The electricity needed by the air-conditioning equals the heat it removes divided by its cooling coefficient of performance.

  2. Heat leaves either through the envelope (conduction: in winter this helps, in summer it adds load) or through the HVAC coil. In a well-insulated building the envelope term is small, so nearly all heat goes through the coil.
  3. The HVAC is a heat pump used for its cold side (Eq. 8.1.9): removing \(Q\) costs \(Q/\text{COP}_c\) of electricity, and it rejects \(Q + P_{\text{HVAC}}\) to the outside.
  4. Total electricity per day: \(E_{\text{tot}} \approx E_{\text{light}}(1 + f_{\text{aux}})(1 + 1/\text{COP}_c)\), where \(f_{\text{aux}}\) is the auxiliary fraction — the formula in the calculator below.
  5. In a cold climate the plant factory could use cold outside air directly ("free cooling", air-side economiser), but opening to outside air loses CO₂ and humidity; this is a design choice with trade-offs.

Greenhouse versus plant factory: total and purchased energy

We can now return to the paradox of the introduction. In the model comparison by Graamans et al. (2018), lettuce in a plant factory needed 247 kWh of purchased electricity per kg of dry weight, but the greenhouses needed only 70 (Netherlands), 111 (United Arab Emirates) and 182 or 211 kWh (Kiruna, Sweden, for two supplementary-lighting scenarios). Yet the plant factory was the most efficient in total energy use — 1411 MJ kg⁻¹ DW against 1699 MJ kg⁻¹ DW for the best greenhouse. The difference is sunlight: it is free, abundant and carbon-free, but a greenhouse uses it less efficiently because the light arrives in a fluctuating, uncontrollable way, and because the greenhouse loses heat and CO₂ through its cover and vents. The plant factory uses a costly, purchased light source very efficiently in a perfectly controlled climate.

Three lessons follow for anyone assessing a CEA project.

  1. Say which energy you mean. Total energy (including sunlight), purchased energy, primary energy (including power-plant losses) and electricity are different quantities. A claim that "system A is more energy-efficient" is meaningless without the boundary.
  2. Climate decides. A greenhouse in Kiruna needs three times the purchased energy of one in the Netherlands for the same lettuce; the plant factory is almost independent of outside climate. In the Arctic winter or in a desert summer, the plant factory's disadvantage shrinks.
  3. Carbon depends on the source. A kilowatt-hour of Swedish electricity (hydro, nuclear and wind dominated; among the lowest greenhouse-gas intensities in the EU according to the European Environment Agency) carries a small fraction of the emissions of a kilowatt-hour of natural gas heat, let alone coal power. You will compute carbon footprints in Lesson 8.4 and full life-cycle comparisons in Lesson 15.1.

Key terms

Control volume
An imaginary boundary around the system being analysed; the energy balance counts every flow that crosses it.
Steady state
A condition in which temperatures and stored energy do not change with time, so that inputs equal outputs.
Conduction
Heat transfer through a material by molecular collisions; described by Fourier's law \(q = k\Delta T/L\).
Convection
Heat carried by a moving fluid from or to a surface; lumped into a heat-transfer coefficient \(h_c\).
Stefan–Boltzmann law
A surface emits \(\varepsilon\sigma T^4\) of thermal radiation; net exchange between surfaces depends on the difference of fourth powers of absolute temperature.
Latent heat flux
Energy transported as water vapour (λE); absorbed on evaporation, released on condensation.
U-value
Overall heat-transfer coefficient of a building element (W m⁻² K⁻¹): heat flux per kelvin of inside–outside temperature difference.
Air exchange rate
Number of times per hour the air volume of a building is replaced by outside air (h⁻¹).
Heating degree-hours
The sum over time of the positive difference between set-point and outside temperature (K h); multiplied by a heat-loss coefficient it gives heat demand.
Energy screen
A horizontal fabric closed at night in greenhouses to reduce heat loss through the roof.
Coefficient of performance (COP)
Useful heat delivered (or removed) by a heat pump divided by the electrical work used; limited by the Carnot value \(T_h/(T_h - T_c)\).
Combined heat and power (CHP)
Simultaneous generation of electricity and useful heat from one fuel, typically a gas engine with 85–90 % total efficiency.
Closed greenhouse
A greenhouse that keeps vents closed, harvests surplus summer heat with heat exchangers and stores it (e.g., in an aquifer) for winter use.
Purchased energy
Energy bought as fuel or electricity, as opposed to total energy, which also counts free solar radiation.

Agricultural Systems, 160, 31–43. doi:10.1016/j.agsy.2017.11.003The reference comparison of energy, water, CO₂ and land use in greenhouses and plant factories in three climates, including Kiruna. Read the methods (how the energy balances were modelled) and the results on purchased versus total energy.
  • Required · open accessKozai, T. (2013). Resource use efficiency of closed plant production system with artificial light: Concept, estimation and application to plant factory. Proceedings of the Japan Academy, Series B, 89(10), 447–461. doi:10.2183/pjab.89.447A clear derivation of the plant-factory energy, water and CO₂ balances by the pioneer of the field, with measured COPs and electricity shares.
  • RecommendedVadiee, A., & Martin, V. (2012). Energy management in horticultural applications through the closed greenhouse concept, state of the art. Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews, 16(7), 5087–5100. doi:10.1016/j.rser.2012.04.022A review from KTH Stockholm of closed and semi-closed greenhouses and seasonal thermal storage.
  • Recommended · open accessWahlroos, M., Pärssinen, M., Rinne, S., Syri, S., & Manner, J. (2018). Future views on waste heat utilization – Case of data centers in Northern Europe. Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews, 82, 1749–1764. doi:10.1016/j.rser.2017.10.058Why Nordic data-centre waste heat is attractive, and the barriers (low temperature, business models) that also apply to greenhouses.
  • TextbookStanghellini, C., van 't Ooster, B., & Heuvelink, E. (2019). Greenhouse horticulture: Technology for optimal crop production. Wageningen Academic Publishers. doi:10.3920/978-90-8686-879-7The chapters on the greenhouse energy balance, covers and screens go deeper into everything in this lesson.
