LED technology and lighting design

A modern horticultural LED fixture turns more than half of the electricity it draws into photons that plants can use — roughly twice as many photons per joule as the high-pressure sodium lamps that lit the world's greenhouses for half a century. Yet the photons are only valuable if they land on leaves, evenly, for a price the grower can afford. This lesson follows the energy from the wall socket through the semiconductor, the heat sink and the optics to the canopy, and then turns the physics into a lighting design: how many fixtures, how high, how far apart, and at what cost.

  • Trace the efficiency chain from wall socket to canopy — driver, internal quantum efficiency, extraction, droop, optics — and compute photon efficacy, heat output and electricity per mole of photons (LO3).
  • Relate junction temperature to efficiency and lifetime, and project Q90/L70 lifetimes with the TM-21 exponential model (LO2, LO3).
  • Apply the inverse-square, cosine and Lambertian laws to point, line and area sources, and design a fixture layout that meets a PPFD target with good uniformity (LO3).
  • Compare LED and high-pressure sodium lighting on capital cost, electricity cost, heat and controllability, including dimming to follow sunlight and electricity prices (LO3).

From the sodium glow to solid-state lighting

For most of the twentieth century, a greenhouse at night in the Netherlands or southern Sweden glowed orange. The light came from high-pressure sodium (HPS) lamps: a ceramic arc tube filled with sodium vapour at high pressure, whose broadened sodium lines between 560 and 620 nm are converted to photosynthetic photons with an efficacy of about 1.7 µmol J⁻¹ in the best double-ended fixtures (Nelson & Bugbee, 2014). HPS lamps are cheap, robust and bright, but they cannot be dimmed smoothly, need minutes to warm up, and radiate a large share of their power as infrared heat directly onto the crop.

Light-emitting diodes (LEDs) replace the hot plasma with a cold solid. The first visible (red) LED was demonstrated by Holonyak and Bevacqua (1962); NASA-funded researchers were growing lettuce under red LEDs by 1991 (Bula et al., 1991); but white and blue LEDs had to wait for Shuji Nakamura's high-brightness InGaN blue LED in 1994 (Nakamura et al., 1994), work that earned Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Nakamura the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics. The efficacy race since then is summarised below.

1962

First visible-spectrum LED (red GaAsP) — Holonyak & Bevacqua.

1991

"Light-emitting diodes as a radiation source for plants": red LEDs grow lettuce for space research (Bula et al.).

1994

Candela-class InGaN blue LED — the key to blue and white solid-state lighting (Nakamura et al.).

2014

Best LED and best double-ended HPS fixtures both measured at 1.66–1.70 µmol J⁻¹; LEDs cost 5–10 × more per photon (Nelson & Bugbee).

2020

Best fixtures 2.5–2.8 µmol J⁻¹ (white + red) and ≈ 3.0 µmol J⁻¹ (blue + red); projected maxima 3.4 and 4.1 µmol J⁻¹ (Kusuma et al.).

2023–2025

DesignLights Consortium minimum photosynthetic photon efficacy for listed fixtures: 2.30 µmol J⁻¹ (Hort V3.0, 2023), raised to 2.5 µmol J⁻¹ (Hort V4.0, from April 2025).

Photon efficacy has thus roughly doubled in a decade. Manufacturers' datasheets for the most efficient top-lighting fixtures on the market in 2024–2026 claim values of 3.5 µmol J⁻¹ and above; treat such claims with healthy scepticism and look for independent test reports (for example the DLC qualified-products list, which requires testing to ANSI/IES LM-79). This lesson explains why the numbers are what they are, how close they are to physical limits, and how to turn them into a design (for a broad review of LED physics and applications, see Pattison et al., 2018).

Big idea

The electricity cost of a lighting system depends on two numbers only: the photon efficacy of the fixture (µmol J⁻¹) and the capture efficiency of the layout (the fraction of emitted photons that land on leaves). Semiconductor physics sets the first; geometry sets the second. Everything else — spectrum, lifetime, controls — modifies these two.

How an LED makes photons

An LED is a p–n junction: a crystal of semiconductor doped on one side with atoms that donate electrons (n-type) and on the other with atoms that accept them, leaving mobile "holes" (p-type). In a semiconductor, electrons can only have energies in two bands separated by a forbidden gap, the band gap \(E_g\): the lower valence band (almost full) and the upper conduction band (almost empty). Under forward bias, electrons from the n-side and holes from the p-side are pushed into the junction region, where they meet and recombine: the electron drops across the gap and gives up its energy — ideally as one photon of energy close to \(E_g\).

Emission wavelength from the band gap(4.3.1)
\[ \lambda_{\text{peak}} \approx \frac{h\,c}{E_g} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda_{\text{nm}} \approx \frac{1240}{E_{g,\text{eV}}} \]

the peak wavelength of an LED is approximately Planck's constant times the speed of light divided by the band-gap energy; with the gap in electronvolts, 1240 divided by the gap gives the wavelength in nanometres.

Follow the derivation
  1. Energy conservation for one recombination event: the electron falls from the bottom of the conduction band to the top of the valence band, releasing \(E_g\) (plus a little thermal kinetic energy, of order \(k_BT \approx 0.026\) eV at room temperature).
  2. If that energy leaves as one photon, \(h\nu = hc/\lambda = E_g\) (Eq. 4.1.2), so \(\lambda = hc/E_g\).
  3. With \(h = 4.1357\times10^{-15}\) eV s and \(c = 2.9979\times10^{17}\) nm s⁻¹, \(hc = 1239.84\) eV nm.
  4. The emission spectrum is not a line but a band of width \(\approx 1.8\,k_BT\) in energy plus alloy broadening: about 20 nm FWHM for blue InGaN and red AlGaInP LEDs — the "Gaussian" peaks used in physics.js (ledSpectrum).
Direct and indirect band gaps

Photon emission requires conservation of both energy and crystal momentum. In direct-gap semiconductors (GaN, InGaN, GaAs, AlGaInP in its red–amber range) the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same momentum, so an electron can drop straight down and emit a photon efficiently. In indirect-gap materials such as silicon a phonon (lattice vibration) must participate, radiative recombination is slow, and the energy is lost as heat instead. That is why there are no silicon LEDs, yet silicon makes excellent photodiodes for quantum sensors (Lesson 4.1).

Two features of Table 1 deserve attention. First, the green gap: indium-rich InGaN is strained and defect-prone, so green LEDs are far less efficient than blue or red — one reason why most fixtures do not use green LEDs even where green light would be useful (Lesson 4.4). Second, the white LED is a blue LED coated with a phosphor that absorbs part of the blue and re-emits it at longer wavelengths. The energy difference between the absorbed and emitted photon — the Stokes shift — is lost as heat in the phosphor. But notice what this means in photon terms: if the phosphor had a quantum efficiency of 100 %, each blue photon would become exactly one longer-wavelength photon and the photon efficacy would be unchanged; white LEDs would "be as efficient as blue LEDs if phosphor conversion was 100 % efficient" (Kusuma et al., 2020). In practice conversion efficiencies are 80–95 %, lower for warm white (more phosphor) than for cool white.

the number of micromoles of photons produced per joule of electricity equals the fraction of electrical power that leaves as radiation times the number of micromoles per joule of radiation at the (energy-weighted mean) wavelength.

The efficiency chain: from wall socket to canopy

Where does the rest of the electricity go? Follow one joule from the wall socket. The driver (power supply) converts mains AC to the constant DC current the LEDs need, losing 5–15 % as heat (Kusuma et al., 2020). In the LED chip, four efficiencies multiply: not every injected electron reaches the active region (injection efficiency); not every electron–hole pair recombines by emitting a photon — some recombine non-radiatively at crystal defects or by Auger processes (internal quantum efficiency, IQE); not every photon escapes the high-refractive-index crystal (extraction efficiency); and the photon carries a little less energy than the electron's electrical energy \(eV_f\) (voltage efficiency). Finally the fixture's lens or protective cover absorbs or back-reflects a few per cent.

The efficiency chain of an LED fixture(4.3.3)
\[ \eta_{\text{fix}} = \eta_{\text{drv}}\cdot\underbrace{\eta_{\text{inj}}\,\eta_{\text{IQE}}\,\eta_{\text{extr}}}_{\text{EQE}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{h\nu}{e\,V_f}}_{\eta_V}\cdot f_T\,f_I\cdot\eta_{\text{opt}}, \qquad \eta_{\text{ph,fix}} = 0.008359\,\eta_{\text{fix}}\,\bar\lambda_{\text{nm}} \]

the fraction of electrical power that leaves the fixture as light is the product of the driver efficiency, the external quantum efficiency of the chip (injection × internal quantum efficiency × extraction), the voltage efficiency, correction factors for junction temperature and drive current (the "droops"), and the transmission of the optics. Multiplying by photons per joule gives the fixture's photon efficacy.

Follow the derivation
  1. Let the fixture draw power \(P_{\text{el}}\). The driver delivers \(\eta_{\text{drv}}P_{\text{el}}\) to the LEDs as a current \(I\) at forward voltage \(V_f\): \(\eta_{\text{drv}}P_{\text{el}} = IV_f\).
  2. Electrons pass the junction at a rate \(I/e\) per second. A fraction EQE of them produces a photon that escapes the chip: photon rate \(=\text{EQE}\,I/e\).
  3. Optical power from the chip: \(\text{EQE}\,(I/e)\,h\nu\). Dividing by the electrical power \(IV_f\) gives the chip's wall-plug efficiency \(\text{WPE} = \text{EQE}\cdot h\nu/(eV_f)\).
  4. Multiply by driver and optics efficiencies and by the droop factors to get \(\eta_{\text{fix}}\); multiply by photons per joule (Eq. 4.3.2) to get the fixture photon efficacy.
  5. Kusuma et al. (2020) multiply the four fixture-level factors (drive current, thermal, driver, optics) and find that near-optimal design retains \(1.04\times0.95\times0.95\times0.99 = 93\) % of the LED's rated efficacy, whereas typical design retains only \(0.90\times0.90\times0.90\times0.92 = 67\) %.
Why do LEDs "droop"?

Efficiency droop is the fall of EQE at high current density. At high carrier densities, three-particle Auger recombination — an electron and hole recombine and give their energy to a third carrier instead of a photon — grows faster than radiative recombination; Iveland et al. (2013) detected the resulting hot Auger electrons directly. Thermal droop is the fall of output at high junction temperature, as non-radiative recombination and carrier leakage increase; red AlGaInP emitters are markedly more temperature-sensitive than blue InGaN ones (Cho et al., 2013; Schubert, 2006). Both droops push designers towards more LEDs driven at lower current and kept cool — which costs capital but saves electricity.

Common misconception

"LEDs produce no heat." Every watt of electricity that enters a grow room ends up as heat in that room — the first law of thermodynamics allows nothing else (apart from the tiny fraction stored as chemical energy in the crop). What LEDs change is where and how the heat appears. An HPS lamp emits much of its power as infrared radiation straight onto the canopy; an LED fixture dumps its heat mainly by convection from a heat sink above the crop, and its useful light carries a larger share of the energy. Nelson and Bugbee (2015) computed leaf energy balances and found that, with well-watered plants, leaves under all sources stay within about 2 °C of air temperature; only under severe water stress and low wind did the difference between sources become large (leaves up to 8 °C above air under LEDs versus 12 °C under HPS).

Heat, junction temperature and lifetime

The temperature that matters for an LED is not the air temperature but the temperature of the p–n junction, \(T_j\), where the heat is generated. Heat flows from the junction through the LED package, the solder joint and circuit board, the thermal interface and the heat sink into the air. Each step has a thermal resistance \(R\) (K W⁻¹), exactly analogous to an electrical resistance: the temperature drop across it is the heat flow times the resistance (Ohm's law for heat).

Junction temperature (thermal Ohm's law)(4.3.4)
\[ T_j = T_a + P_{\text{th,fix}}\left(R_{s\text{-}a} + R_{b\text{-}s}\right) + P_{\text{th,LED}}\,R_{j\text{-}b}, \qquad P_{\text{th}} = P_{\text{el}}\,(1-\eta_{\text{rad}}) \]

the junction temperature equals the air temperature plus the temperature rises across each thermal resistance in series, each rise being the heat flowing through that resistance times the resistance. The heat is the part of the electrical power that does not leave as light.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(T_j,\ T_a\)junction and ambient air temperature°C (differences in K)
\(P_{\text{th,fix}}\)heat flowing through the common heat sink (all LEDs, excluding the separately cooled driver)W
\(P_{\text{th,LED}}\)heat generated in one LED packageW
\(R_{j\text{-}b},\ R_{b\text{-}s},\ R_{s\text{-}a}\)thermal resistances junction→board, board→sink, sink→airK W⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Fourier's law for steady conduction through a slab of thickness \(L\), area \(A\) and conductivity \(k\): \(P = kA\,\Delta T/L\), i.e., \(\Delta T = P\,R\) with \(R = L/(kA)\). Convection from fins obeys the same form with \(R = 1/(h_cA)\).
  2. In series the temperature drops add, exactly like voltage drops in a series circuit.
  3. Resistances shared by many LEDs (heat sink) see the total heat; resistances belonging to one LED see only its own heat — hence two terms.
  4. Because \(P_{\text{th}} = P_{\text{el}}(1-\eta_{\text{rad}})\), a more efficient LED also runs cooler, which makes it more efficient still — a virtuous circle.
Assumptions and validity

Steady state, one-dimensional heat flow, resistances independent of temperature. Real heat sinks lose part of their heat by radiation and their convective resistance depends on air speed — which is why fixtures in still air run hotter than on a test bench. Manufacturers report the in-situ temperature (ISTMT test) so that LM-80 lifetime data can be applied at the correct temperature.

°C + W × K W⁻¹ = °C ✓

Temperature matters twice: hotter LEDs are less efficient now and fade faster over time. LEDs rarely fail abruptly; their output slowly declines. The industry therefore specifies lifetimes as the hours until the output has fallen to a stated fraction: L70 (70 % of initial luminous flux) for general lighting, and — for horticulture, where photons rather than lumens count — Q90, the hours until the photosynthetic photon flux falls to 90 %. The DesignLights Consortium requires Q90 ≥ 36 000 h, established from LED test data according to ANSI/IES LM-80 and extrapolated with ANSI/IES TM-21 (DesignLights Consortium, 2023).

TM-21 lifetime projection(4.3.5)
\[ \frac{\Phi(t)}{\Phi_0} = B\,e^{-\alpha t} \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q_p = \frac{\ln\!\left(B/p\right)}{\alpha}, \qquad Q_p \le 6\,t_{\text{test}} \]

the normalised photon (or luminous) output decays exponentially with operating time; the time to fall to a fraction \(p\) (0.9 for Q90, 0.7 for L70) is the natural logarithm of \(B/p\) divided by the decay rate. Projections may not exceed six times the duration of the test.

Follow the derivation
  1. LM-80 tests run samples of LEDs at fixed case temperatures and currents for thousands of hours, recording output every 1000 h.
  2. TM-21 fits \(\ln(\Phi/\Phi_0) = \ln B - \alpha t\) — a straight line in log space — to the later part of the data (the first 1000 h often show a "burn-in" rise).
  3. Set \(B e^{-\alpha Q_p} = p\): \(-\alpha Q_p = \ln(p/B)\), so \(Q_p = \ln(B/p)/\alpha\).
  4. Because extrapolating an exponential far beyond the data is risky, TM-21 limits reported projections to six times the test duration (with a sample of at least 20 units); longer values are reported as "\(> 6\,t_{\text{test}}\)".
  5. Interpolate between the tested case temperatures (Arrhenius behaviour of \(\alpha\)) to find the rate at the fixture's in-situ temperature.

Geometry of light: point, line and area sources

A fixture emits a photon flux Φ (the PPF, µmol s⁻¹). The crop receives a photon flux density E (the PPFD, µmol m⁻² s⁻¹). Three geometric laws connect them, and you have met them in the grow-room lighting laboratory as Eqs. L1–L2: the inverse-square law (flux spreads over a sphere of area \(4\pi d^2\)), the cosine law of incidence (a tilted surface intercepts less), and the fixture's own intensity distribution. For a diffusing LED board, the intensity follows Lambert's cosine law, \(I(\theta) = I_0\cos\theta\); lenses make the beam narrower, which we describe with \(\cos^n\theta\).

PPFD from a point-like fixture (lab Eqs. L1–L2)(4.3.6)
\[ E(r) = \frac{I_0\cos^{\,n+1}\theta}{d^{2}} = \frac{(n+1)\,\Phi}{2\pi}\,\frac{h^{\,n+1}}{\left(h^2+r^2\right)^{(n+3)/2}}, \qquad E(0) = \frac{(n+1)\,\Phi}{2\pi h^2}\ \ \left(=\frac{\Phi}{\pi h^2}\ \text{for Lambert}\right) \]

the PPFD at horizontal distance \(r\) from the point below a fixture mounted at height \(h\) equals the fixture's intensity in that direction times the cosine of the incidence angle, divided by the square of the distance. Directly below a Lambertian fixture it is the fixture's photon flux divided by π times the height squared.

\(\Phi\)fixture photosynthetic photon flux (PPF)µmol s⁻¹
\(I_0 = (n+1)\Phi/2\pi\)on-axis intensity (normalisation derived in lab Eq. L1)µmol s⁻¹ sr⁻¹
\(n\)beam exponent: 1 = Lambertian, larger = narrower–

Follow the derivation (Lambertian normalisation)
  1. Total flux = intensity integrated over the lower hemisphere: \(\Phi = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^{\pi/2} I_0\cos^n\theta\,\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi = 2\pi I_0/(n+1)\). Hence \(I_0 = (n+1)\Phi/(2\pi)\); for \(n = 1\), \(I_0 = \Phi/\pi\) — not \(\Phi/2\pi\) as one might guess from "half a sphere", because a Lambertian source sends more photons downwards than sideways.
  2. A canopy patch \(\mathrm{d}A\) at distance \(d\), tilted by \(\theta\) to the ray, subtends \(\mathrm{d}\Omega = \mathrm{d}A\cos\theta/d^2\), so \(E = I(\theta)\cos\theta/d^2 = I_0\cos^{n+1}\theta/d^2\).
  3. Substitute \(\cos\theta = h/d\) and \(d^2 = h^2+r^2\) to obtain the second form.
  4. Consequences: directly below, \(E\propto 1/h^2\) — halving the height quadruples the PPFD. At \(r = h\) (45°), a Lambertian fixture gives \(E = E(0)\cos^4 45^{\circ} = 0.25\,E(0)\).
Assumptions and validity

Far-field approximation: valid when the distance is at least about five times the largest dimension of the luminous area. Closer than that — typical in vertical farms, where 1.2 m bars hang 0.2–0.4 m above the crop — the fixture must be treated as a line or area source (Eq. 4.3.7) or split into several point sources, as the lighting laboratory does.

\(\dfrac{\mu\text{mol s}^{-1}}{\text{sr}}\cdot\dfrac{\text{m}^{n+1}}{\text{m}^{n+3}} = \mu\text{mol m}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) ✓

The inverse-square law is the law of a point. Long bars and large panels behave differently when you are close to them, and the difference is the key to the uniform, low-hanging lighting of vertical farms.

Line and area sources(4.3.7)
\[ E_{\text{line}} = \frac{\Phi'}{2h}\quad\text{(infinite Lambertian line)}, \qquad E_{\text{plane}} = M\quad\text{(infinite Lambertian plane)} \]

directly below a very long bar of Lambertian emitters, the PPFD is the bar's photon flux per metre of length divided by twice the height — it falls only as 1/h, not 1/h². Below a very large emitting plane it equals the plane's photon exitance and does not depend on height at all.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\Phi'\)photon flux per unit length of a linear sourceµmol s⁻¹ m⁻¹
\(M\)photon exitance: flux emitted per unit area of a planar sourceµmol m⁻² s⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. Line. Cut the bar into elements \(\mathrm{d}x\), each a Lambertian point with flux \(\Phi'\mathrm{d}x\) and on-axis intensity \(\Phi'\mathrm{d}x/\pi\). At the point below the bar, an element at distance \(x\) along the bar contributes \(\mathrm{d}E = (\Phi'/\pi)\cos^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}x/d^2 = (\Phi'/\pi)\,h^2\,\mathrm{d}x/(h^2+x^2)^2\).
  2. Use \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x/(h^2+x^2)^2 = \pi/(2h^3)\): \(E_{\text{line}} = (\Phi'/\pi)\,h^2\,\pi/(2h^3) = \Phi'/(2h)\).
  3. Plane. Between two infinite parallel planes every photon leaving one must strike the other, so the flux density received equals the flux density emitted: \(E = M\), for any separation. (Formally, integrate the line result across the plane.)
  4. Interpretation. Near a source, its extent "hides" the inverse-square law: a bar looks infinite when \(h\) is small compared with its length, a ceiling of fixtures looks infinite when \(h\) is small compared with the room. Far away, every source looks like a point and \(1/h^2\) returns.
Assumptions and validity

"Infinite" means the source extends well beyond the point of interest (several times \(h\)). Near the ends of a bar, or the edges of a bench, the PPFD falls towards half of the central value — the edge effect that reflective walls are designed to cure.

µmol s⁻¹ m⁻¹ ÷ m = µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ ✓

Designing a layout for a target PPFD

A lighting design must deliver the target PPFD (from the DLI and photoperiod, Lesson 4.2) with acceptable uniformity — every plant should get nearly the same light, otherwise growth is uneven and harvest is spread out — while wasting as few photons as possible on aisles and walls. Uniformity has been a design criterion since the HPS era, when Ciolkosz et al. (2001) showed how the choice of luminaire and its placement above a greenhouse bench determine how evenly the crop is lit. Three numbers describe a design:

Number of fixtures, uniformity and spacing ratio(4.3.8)
\[ N = \frac{\bar E\,A}{\Phi_{\text{fix}}\;\eta_{\text{cap}}\;MF}, \qquad U = \frac{E_{\min}}{\bar E}, \qquad \text{SHR} = \frac{s}{h} \]

the number of fixtures equals the target mean PPFD times the growing area, divided by the photon flux of one fixture, the fraction of photons captured by the canopy and a maintenance factor for ageing. Uniformity is the minimum PPFD divided by the mean. The spacing-to-height ratio is the distance between fixtures divided by their height above the canopy.

Follow the derivation
  1. Photons needed at the canopy per second: \(\bar E A\) (µmol s⁻¹).
  2. Of the \(N\Phi_{\text{fix}}\) photons emitted, only a fraction \(\eta_{\text{cap}}\) reaches the canopy, and at the end of the maintenance period the fixtures emit only \(MF\) of their initial flux: \(N\Phi_{\text{fix}}\eta_{\text{cap}}MF = \bar E A\).
  3. Solve for \(N\) and round up. This is the "lumen method" of architectural lighting, translated into photons.
  4. Why SHR controls uniformity. For an infinite square grid of Lambertian fixtures, summing Eq. 4.3.6 over neighbours gives (computed for this lesson) \(U = 1.00\) at SHR 0.5, 0.98 at SHR 1.0, 0.87 at 1.5 and 0.71 at 2.0. Narrow beams (\(n=5\)) need tighter spacing: \(U = 0.81\) already at SHR 1.0 and 0.43 at 1.5.
Assumptions and validity

\(\eta_{\text{cap}}\) depends on the layout itself (height, beam, walls), so the design is iterative: guess \(\eta_{\text{cap}}\), compute \(N\), simulate the PPFD map (Figure 5 or the 3D laboratory), update \(\eta_{\text{cap}}\). Uniformity figures above are for an infinite array; at the edges of a real bench they are lower unless walls reflect or the outer rows are placed closer.

Worked example 3Lighting one tier of a vertical farm

A vertical-farm tier is 10 m long and 1.2 m wide. Lettuce needs 14.4 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ over 16 h. The available LED bars (1.2 m long) emit 180 µmol s⁻¹ each at 60 W. Mounted 0.3 m above the canopy with white side curtains, the capture efficiency is estimated at 0.85, and the design must still meet the target at Q90 (MF = 0.9). How many bars, at what spacing, and what electrical power density results?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Target PPFD. \(14.4\times10^6/(16\times3600) = 250\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  2. Number of bars (Eq. 4.3.8). \(N = 250\times12/(180\times0.85\times0.9) = 3000/137.7 = 21.8\) → 22 bars.
  3. Spacing. Bars across the tier, spaced \(10/22 = 0.45\) m. SHR = 0.45/0.30 = 1.5: along the tier the uniformity will be good because each point sees several bars; across the 1.2 m width each bar is a line source (Eq. 4.3.7), and the edges will be dimmer unless the curtains reflect well.
  4. Power density. \(22\times60 = 1320\) W for 12 m² = 110 W m⁻². Photon efficacy 3.0 µmol J⁻¹.
  5. Sanity check. New fixtures deliver \(22\times180\times0.85/12 = 281\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — 12 % above target, so they should be dimmed to 89 % when new (saving electricity) and turned up as they age.
  6. Daily electricity. 1.32 kW × 16 h = 21.1 kWh per tier per day, 1.76 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ at full power — the number that dominates the economics of a plant factory (Graamans et al., 2018).
22 bars at ≈ 0.45 m spacing, 110 W m⁻² (≈ 1.8 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹)

LED versus HPS: capital, electricity and heat

A lighting investment is paid twice: once when the fixtures are bought (capital expenditure, capex) and every hour they run (operating expenditure, opex, mostly electricity). The fairest single figure for comparing technologies is the cost per mole of photons delivered to the crop, with the capital spread over the fixture's life by a capital recovery factor (CRF) — the annual payment that repays a loan at interest rate \(r\) over \(n\) years.

Cost per mole of photons at the canopy(4.3.9)
\[ c_{\text{mol}} = \underbrace{\frac{0.2778\,p_{\text{el}}}{\eta_{\text{ph}}\,\eta_{\text{cap}}}}_{\text{electricity}} + \underbrace{\frac{\text{CRF}\cdot c_{\text{cap}}}{\eta_{\text{cap}}\,t_{\text{op}}\cdot0.0036}}_{\text{capital}}, \qquad \text{CRF} = \frac{r\,(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n-1} \]

the cost of each mole of photons that reaches the crop is the electricity cost per mole (Eq. L4 times the price) plus the annualised capital cost of one micromole-per-second of fixture output, divided by the moles that output delivers to the crop in a year.

Follow the derivation
  1. Electricity per mole at the canopy: \(0.2778/(\eta_{\text{ph}}\eta_{\text{cap}})\) kWh mol⁻¹ (lab Eq. L4); multiply by the price.
  2. One µmol s⁻¹ of fixture output costs \(c_{\text{cap}}\) up front. The CRF converts this into an equal annual payment over \(n\) years at rate \(r\) (the standard annuity formula: the present value of \(n\) payments of CRF equals 1).
  3. In one year that µmol s⁻¹ delivers \(\eta_{\text{cap}}\times t_{\text{op}}\times3600\times10^{-6} = 0.0036\,\eta_{\text{cap}}t_{\text{op}}\) mol to the crop.
  4. Divide the annual payment by the annual moles and add the electricity term.
  5. Note the structure: electricity cost falls with efficacy, capital cost falls with operating hours. A lamp used 1000 h per year (a greenhouse in Almería) is dominated by capital; one used 6000 h per year (a vertical farm) by electricity.
Assumptions and validity

Ignores maintenance (HPS re-lamping), disposal, the value or cost of the heat, installation and cabling, and changes in price over time. Kusuma et al. (2020) reported fixture prices in 2020 of roughly 0.5–1.5 µmol s⁻¹ per US dollar for LEDs and 5–9 µmol s⁻¹ per dollar for HPS; use current quotations for real decisions.

\(\dfrac{\text{kWh}\ \mu\text{mol J}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{EUR}\,\text{kWh}^{-1}}{\mu\text{mol J}^{-1}} = \text{EUR mol}^{-1}\); \(\dfrac{\text{yr}^{-1}\cdot\text{EUR}\,(\mu\text{mol s}^{-1})^{-1}}{\text{h yr}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol s }\mu\text{mol}^{-1}\text{h}^{-1}} = \text{EUR mol}^{-1}\) ✓
Worked example 4LED or HPS for a Swedish tomato greenhouse?

A grower needs 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at the canopy (ηcap = 0.9) for 4000 h per year. Option A: LED, 3.0 µmol J⁻¹, €1.00 per µmol s⁻¹. Option B: HPS, 1.7 µmol J⁻¹, €0.15 per µmol s⁻¹. Both are financed over 10 years at 6 %. Compare the annual cost per square metre at electricity prices of €0.10 and €0.20 kWh⁻¹, and find the break-even price.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Fixture output per m². \(200/0.9 = 222\) µmol s⁻¹ m⁻².
  2. Electrical power. LED \(222/3.0 = 74\) W m⁻²; HPS \(222/1.7 = 131\) W m⁻². Annual energy: 296 and 523 kWh m⁻².
  3. Capital. CRF(6 %, 10 yr) = \(0.06\times1.06^{10}/(1.06^{10}-1) = 0.1359\). LED \(222\times1.00\times0.1359 = 30.2\) € yr⁻¹; HPS \(222\times0.15\times0.1359 = 4.5\) € yr⁻¹.
  4. At €0.10 kWh⁻¹. LED 29.6 + 30.2 = €59.8; HPS 52.3 + 4.5 = €56.8 m⁻² yr⁻¹ — HPS slightly cheaper.
  5. At €0.20 kWh⁻¹. LED 59.3 + 30.2 = €89.5; HPS 104.6 + 4.5 = €109.1 — LED 18 % cheaper.
  6. Break-even. Extra LED capital \(30.2-4.5 = 25.7\) € yr⁻¹ equals the electricity saving \((523-296)\,p\) when \(p = 25.7/227 = 0.113\) € kWh⁻¹.
  7. What the numbers leave out. In a Swedish winter the 57 W m⁻² of extra HPS power is not wasted: it heats the greenhouse, which LEDs then have to buy from the heating system. Katzin et al. (2021) simulated this for climates from subtropical China to arctic Sweden and found total energy savings from switching to LEDs of typically 10–25 %, not the 40 % suggested by the efficacy ratio, because heating demand increased.
≈ €60 (LED) vs €57 (HPS) m⁻² yr⁻¹ at €0.10; €90 vs €109 at €0.20; break-even ≈ €0.11 kWh⁻¹

Dimming and dynamic lighting

The decisive practical advantage of LEDs is not only their efficacy but their controllability. They reach full output in microseconds, can be dimmed continuously by current or by pulse-width modulation, and — in multi-channel fixtures — can change spectrum (Lesson 4.4). Three control strategies use this:

  • Sunlight-following (greenhouses). Dim the lamps so that lamp plus sun just reaches a threshold PPFD. Van Iersel and Gianino (2017) reduced supplementary-lighting electricity by 20–92 % in this way, and because the light response is concave (Lesson 4.2, Eq. 4.2.10), supplementary photons are most valuable when sunlight is weakest.
  • Maintenance dimming. Run new fixtures below full power and increase the current as they age (Worked example 3), saving the over-design energy.
  • Price-following. With hourly spot prices, schedule the photoperiod in the cheapest hours. In Sweden, prices in the SE3 bidding zone that includes Västerås often have morning and evening peaks on winter days. Avgoustaki and Xydis (2021) showed for vertical farms that shifting lighting demand to low-price hours can substantially reduce energy cost — provided the crop tolerates the new schedule, and the cooling system can cope.
Frontier — lighting as a flexible load

Greenhouses and vertical farms are among the largest electricity users in horticulture. With LEDs that can be dimmed instantly, a farm can act as a flexible load for the grid — reducing demand during price peaks or selling balancing services — while a crop model ensures the daily light integral is still reached. Research in 2024–2026 increasingly combines crop models, weather forecasts and price forecasts in model-predictive controllers (Lesson 10.4). The open question is how much the crop's growth and quality suffer when its light schedule is dictated by the market.

Key terms

p–n junction
Interface between n-type (electron-rich) and p-type (hole-rich) semiconductor; under forward bias carriers recombine there and, in an LED, emit light.
Band gap
Energy separation between valence and conduction bands; sets the photon energy and hence the colour of an LED, \(\lambda \approx 1240/E_g\).
Direct band gap
Band structure in which electrons can recombine with holes without a change in crystal momentum, allowing efficient photon emission.
Internal quantum efficiency (IQE)
Fraction of electron–hole recombinations in the active region that produce a photon.
Extraction efficiency
Fraction of photons generated inside the chip that escape into the surroundings.
Wall-plug (radiant) efficiency
Optical power out divided by electrical power in.
Photosynthetic photon efficacy (PPE)
PAR photons emitted per joule of electricity (µmol J⁻¹).
Efficiency droop
Decline of LED efficiency at high current density, mainly due to Auger recombination.
Thermal droop
Decline of LED efficiency with increasing junction temperature.
Stokes shift
Energy difference between an absorbed and a re-emitted photon in a phosphor; lost as heat.
Junction temperature
Temperature of the light-emitting region of an LED; governs efficiency and lifetime.
Q90 / L70
Operating hours until photon flux (Q) or luminous flux (L) falls to 90 % or 70 % of the initial value.
TM-21
IES method that extrapolates LM-80 test data with an exponential decay, limited to six times the test duration.
Lambertian emitter
A source whose intensity falls with the cosine of the angle from its axis; on-axis intensity Φ/π.
Spacing-to-height ratio (SHR)
Fixture spacing divided by mounting height above the canopy; controls uniformity.
