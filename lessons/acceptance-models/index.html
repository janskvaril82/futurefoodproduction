Trust, risk perception and acceptance of food technologies

Genetically modified crops were declared safe by scientific bodies again and again, yet they were rejected by large parts of the European public. Cultivated meat, insect flour, gene-edited vegetables and vertical-farm produce now face the same test. Why do experts and citizens judge the risks of new food technologies so differently? Whom do people trust, and why does information sometimes backfire? In this lesson you will learn the models that social scientists use to answer these questions — and the measurement tools, above all a reliable questionnaire, that you need to test them in your own project.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain lay risk perception with the psychometric paradigm (dread and unknown risk) and the affect heuristic, and place food technologies on a risk map (LO6).
  • Describe how trust in institutions and scientists acts on perceived risks and benefits, and compute direct and indirect effects in a simple path model (LO6).
  • Apply the theory of planned behaviour as a regression model of intention, compare it with the technology acceptance model, and interpret weights and explained variance (LO4, LO6).
  • Evaluate framing, naming, information, labelling, social norms and nudges as interventions, using published effect sizes and the attitude–behaviour gap (LO6).
  • Construct a Likert attitude scale, derive and compute Cronbach's α, predict reliability with the Spearman–Brown formula, and distinguish reliability from validity (LO4).
  • Argue why public engagement and deliberation, rather than one-way information, are needed for contested food technologies (LO6).

Will it be accepted? Lessons from a controversy

In the late 1990s European supermarkets removed genetically modified ingredients from their own-brand products, and for two decades very few GM foods were sold in the EU. The technical risk assessments had not changed; public acceptance had. The episode taught a generation of scientists that acceptance is not a communication problem to be solved after the technology is ready. It is shaped by how people perceive risks and benefits, whom they trust, what they find natural or disgusting (Lesson 13.1), and how the technology is framed and governed. A meta-analysis of public perceptions of agri-food applications of genetic modification confirmed that concerns about GM foods were widespread and varied systematically between regions and over time (Frewer et al., 2013).

Today the same questions are asked about cultivated meat, precision-fermented dairy proteins, insects, new genomic techniques and even about “high-tech” vertical farming. Siegrist and Hartmann (2020) summarise the psychological drivers of acceptance of novel food technologies as trust, perceived risks and benefits, perceived naturalness, disgust and food (technology) neophobia. This lesson turns these drivers into quantitative models you can estimate from questionnaire data.

Common misconception — the knowledge deficit

“People oppose new food technologies because they do not understand them; more science education will fix it.” The relationship between scientific knowledge and attitudes is real but modest and context-dependent (Sturgis & Allum, 2004). In a US representative sample, the most extreme opponents of GM foods had the least objective knowledge of science and genetics but the highest self-assessed understanding of GM foods (Fernbach et al., 2019). Knowledge matters — but trust, values, affect and identity matter at least as much, and people who are confident that they already understand are the least receptive to information.

Risk perception and the psychometric paradigm

Engineers define risk as probability × consequence. Lay people are not bad at arithmetic; they simply include more dimensions. In the late 1970s, Fischhoff, Slovic and colleagues asked people to rate technologies and activities on nine characteristics of risk — for example whether the risk is voluntary, whether its effects are immediate or delayed, whether it is known to science, whether it is controllable, new or old, chronic or catastrophic, common or dreaded, and how fatal its consequences are (Fischhoff et al., 1978). This psychometric paradigm revealed that perceived risk and the acceptable level of risk depend systematically on these qualities.

When the ratings of many hazards were factor-analysed, two factors explained most of the structure (Slovic, 1987):

  • Factor 1 — Dread risk: perceived lack of control, dread, catastrophic potential, fatal consequences, inequitable distribution of risks and benefits, high risk to future generations, risks that are increasing and not easily reduced, involuntary exposure.
  • Factor 2 — Unknown risk: hazards that are not observable, unknown to those exposed, have delayed effects, are new, and whose risks are unknown to science.

Hazards high on dread risk are those the public most wants regulated. Technologies high on unknown — typically new, invisible, chemical or biological processes — attract suspicion even when their dread score is moderate. Most novel food technologies sit exactly there: you cannot see whether a burger contains cultured cells, you cannot observe long-term effects, and the technology is new by definition.

Dread and unknown scores from characteristic ratings(13.2.1)
\[ D = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{D}} r_j, \qquad U = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}} r_j \]

a respondent's dread score is the average of their ratings on the characteristics that belong to the dread factor, and their unknown score is the average of their ratings on the characteristics that belong to the unknown factor.

From factor analysis to a simple score
  1. Factor analysis models each rating as a weighted sum of a few latent factors plus noise: \(r_j = \lambda_{j1}F_1 + \lambda_{j2}F_2 + \varepsilon_j\).
  2. Slovic's analyses found that the characteristics split into two groups with high loadings on one factor and low on the other.
  3. Exact factor scores weight each rating by a regression coefficient. When loadings within a group are similar, a unit-weighted score — the plain mean — correlates very highly with the exact score and is far easier to interpret and to replicate.
  4. Plot \(U\) on the horizontal axis and \(D\) on the vertical axis to reproduce the familiar two-dimensional risk map.
Assumptions and validity

The original factor structure was obtained from mean ratings of many hazards across respondents; applying it to a single person's ratings of a single technology is a teaching simplification. Always orient items so that a high rating means “more risky” (e.g., rate “controllable” reversed as “uncontrollable”). The assignment of characteristics to factors follows Slovic (1987).

Affect, benefits and the inverse risk–benefit relation

In the real world, technologies with large benefits often carry large risks (think of cars or electricity). In people's minds, the opposite holds: the more beneficial a technology is judged to be, the less risky it is judged, and vice versa. Alhakami and Slovic (1994) showed this inverse relationship and linked it to the overall affective evaluation of the technology. Finucane et al. (2000) called the mechanism the affect heuristic: people consult a quick good/bad feeling about a technology and derive both risk and benefit judgements from it. Supporting evidence came from experiments in which information that raised perceived benefits also lowered perceived risks, even though it said nothing about risks — and under time pressure, which forces reliance on feelings, the inverse relation became stronger.

Acceptance as perceived benefit minus perceived risk(13.2.2)
\[ \text{Acc} = \beta_0 + \beta_B\,B - \beta_R\,R + \varepsilon, \qquad \operatorname{corr}(B,R) < 0 \ \text{(affect heuristic)} \]

acceptance of a technology rises with perceived benefit and falls with perceived risk; because a single affective feeling drives both judgements, perceived benefit and perceived risk are negatively correlated across people.

SymbolMeaningUnit
Accacceptance (e.g., mean of acceptance items)scale points
\(B, R\)perceived benefit and perceived riskscale points
\(\beta_B, \beta_R\)positive weights estimated by regression–
\(\varepsilon\)residual (everything the model does not capture)scale points
Why the correlation matters for interpretation
  1. If \(B\) and \(R\) were independent, their regression weights would tell you their separate contributions.
  2. When they are negatively correlated, part of what looks like a “benefit effect” is shared with the “risk effect”: both are partly the same underlying affect.
  3. Consequently, an intervention that improves affect (a pleasant tasting, an attractive name) can move both B and R — a double effect on acceptance.
  4. It also works in reverse: a disgust reaction lowers perceived benefits even of healthy, sustainable foods.
All terms are in the same scale points; weights are dimensionless ✓

Trust in institutions and scientists

Most people cannot evaluate the safety of cultivated meat or gene editing themselves. They delegate the judgement to people and institutions they trust — regulators such as EFSA and the Swedish Food Agency, scientists, farmers, companies, NGOs. Trust therefore acts as a heuristic for risk and benefit. Siegrist (2000) tested a causal model on a random quota sample of 1 001 Swiss citizens: trust in institutions using gene technology increased perceived benefits and decreased perceived risks, and these two perceptions determined acceptance. Trust thus had an indirect influence on acceptance. The model fitted well for both women and men; women reported more trust, perceived less benefit and were less accepting.

Indirect (mediated) effect of trust on acceptance(13.2.3)
\[ \text{IE}_{\text{trust}} = a_B\,b_B + a_R\,b_R \]

the indirect effect of trust on acceptance is the path from trust to benefit times the path from benefit to acceptance, plus the path from trust to risk times the path from risk to acceptance.

SymbolMeaningSignIllustrative value
\(a_B\)standardised effect of trust on perceived benefit+0.50
\(b_B\)effect of perceived benefit on acceptance+0.60
\(a_R\)effect of trust on perceived risk−−0.40
\(b_R\)effect of perceived risk on acceptance−−0.30
Follow the derivation
  1. Write the model as linear equations in standardised variables: \(B = a_B T + e_1\), \(R = a_R T + e_2\), \(\text{Acc} = b_B B + b_R R + e_3\).
  2. Substitute the first two into the third: \(\text{Acc} = (a_B b_B + a_R b_R)\,T + (b_B e_1 + b_R e_2 + e_3)\).
  3. The coefficient of \(T\) is the total effect transmitted through the mediators. Two negatives make a positive: trust lowers risk, and lower risk raises acceptance, so \(a_R b_R > 0\).
  4. With the illustrative values: \(0.50\times0.60 + (-0.40)\times(-0.30) = 0.30 + 0.12 = 0.42\): a one-SD increase in trust raises acceptance by 0.42 SD.
Assumptions and validity

The values are illustrative, not Siegrist's estimates. Survey data are cross-sectional, so the direction of causality is an assumption: people may also come to trust institutions because they like a technology. Siegrist (2021) concluded that the importance of trust varies by hazard and respondent group, and called for experiments that test whether trust is a cause or a consequence of attitudes.

Products of standardised coefficients are dimensionless (SD per SD) ✓

Who is trusted, and why? Frewer et al. (1996) investigated the psychological constructs underlying trust in sources of information about food-related risks, and later work distinguishes trust based on perceived competence from trust based on shared values and honesty. The distinction matters: a company may be seen as competent but self-interested, an NGO as honest but not expert. Trust is also asymmetric — it is built slowly and lost quickly — which is why a single food scandal can undo years of confidence in a new technology. Siegrist's (2021) critical review adds that trust in the relevant institutions is most influential when people know little about a hazard, which is precisely the situation for new food technologies.

Swedish context

In Sweden, the Swedish Food Agency (Livsmedelsverket) enforces food law and participates in EU risk assessment, while EFSA performs the scientific assessment of EU novel-food applications. When your tasting study informs participants that an insect ingredient is “authorised by the EU after an EFSA safety assessment”, you are invoking institutional trust — a legitimate but powerful framing that should be reported.

The theory of planned behaviour and technology acceptance

Risk perception explains attitudes; to predict behaviour — will someone actually eat the cricket bread? — social psychologists most often use the theory of planned behaviour (TPB; Ajzen, 1991). Behaviour follows from the intention to perform it, and intention follows from three considerations:

  • Attitude toward the behaviour (A) — do I think eating insect-based food is good or bad? (the expectancy–value sum of Eq. 13.1.1);
  • Subjective norm (SN) — do people important to me think I should do it?
  • Perceived behavioural control (PBC) — is it easy for me to do (available, affordable, I know how to cook it)?
Theory of planned behaviour — the regression model of intention(13.2.4)
\[ I = w_0 + w_A\,A + w_{SN}\,SN + w_{PBC}\,PBC + \varepsilon, \qquad B = f(I,\ PBC) \]

intention equals an intercept plus a weighted sum of attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control, plus an error; behaviour is a function of intention and, where control is not complete, of perceived behavioural control as well.

\(I\)intention (e.g., “I intend to eat insect-based food in the next month”)1–7mean of 2–4 items
\(A\)attitude toward the behaviour1–7semantic differentials (bad–good, unpleasant–pleasant)

\(PBC\)perceived behavioural control1–7“It is easy for me to…”, “It is up to me whether…”
\(w_0, w_A, w_{SN}, w_{PBC}\)intercept and regression weights–estimated by ordinary least squares
\(B\)observed or self-reported behaviourvariese.g., tasted the product: yes/no
Where does the regression model come from?
  1. Ajzen's theory states that intention is a function of A, SN and PBC, and that their relative importance varies across behaviours and populations.
  2. The simplest function is linear. Measure all four constructs on the same people and fit \(I\) on \(A, SN, PBC\) by least squares: the weights minimise \(\sum_i (I_i - \hat I_i)^2\).
  3. If all variables are standardised (z-scores), the weights are standardised regression coefficients (β), comparable across predictors: “one SD more attitude → βA SD more intention”.
  4. The proportion of variance in \(I\) explained by the three predictors is \(R^2 = 1 - SS_{\text{res}}/SS_{\text{tot}}\).
  5. For behaviour, a binary outcome (tasted or not) is best modelled with logistic regression on \(I\) and \(PBC\) (the odds model of Eq. 13.1.5).
Assumptions and validity

Linear, additive effects; constructs measured reliably (see Eq. 13.2.7); predictors not too strongly correlated (multicollinearity inflates standard errors of the weights). Items must be compatible: same action, target, context and time frame (e.g., “eating bread with cricket flour in the next month”). A meta-analysis of 185 studies found that the TPB explained on average 39 % of the variance in intention and 27 % in behaviour, that PBC added explained variance beyond attitudes and norms, and that subjective norm was generally a weak predictor of intention (Armitage & Conner, 2001).

All constructs on the same 1–7 scale: weights are dimensionless and \(I\) is in scale points ✓

The TPB has been applied directly to insect foods. Menozzi et al. (2017) studied 231 Italian young adults and a chocolate-chip cookie containing 10 % cricket flour. They measured intention to eat it in the next month and then observed whether participants actually tasted it. The model explained 78 % of the variance in intention and 19 % in behaviour; attitude and PBC were significant predictors of intention, and intention and PBC predicted behaviour. Beliefs about health and environmental benefits drove attitudes, while disgust at seeing insects, incompatibility with local food culture and the lack of products in supermarkets were the main barriers.

Explained variance and correlation(13.2.5)
\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i (y_i-\bar y)^2}, \qquad R^2 = r^2 \ \text{(one predictor)} \]

the coefficient of determination is one minus the ratio of the residual sum of squares to the total sum of squares; with a single predictor it equals the square of the correlation coefficient.

Why variance explained sounds smaller than it is

A correlation of \(r = 0.53\) — the average intention–behaviour correlation reported in Sheeran's (2002) review of meta-analyses — corresponds to \(R^2 = 0.28\): intentions “explain only 28 %” of behaviour. Yet \(r = 0.53\) is a large effect by the standards of behavioural science. The unexplained 72 % includes measurement error, changing circumstances and, importantly, people who intend but do not act.

The technology acceptance model

Information-systems researchers developed a parallel model: the technology acceptance model (TAM; Davis, 1989), in which the intention to use a technology depends on its perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. For foods, usefulness maps onto perceived benefits (health, climate, price) and ease of use onto convenience, familiarity and knowing how to prepare the product — PBC in TPB terms. TAM is attractive for technology-embedded food products (e.g., a home hydroponic appliance or a meal-planning app) but misses the affective and moral dimensions — disgust, naturalness, identity — that dominate reactions to foods themselves.

Framing, naming, information and nudges

Names frame the product

What should meat grown from animal cells be called? “Lab-grown” evokes test tubes; “clean” evokes purity; “cultivated” evokes farming. Bryant and Barnett (2019) randomly assigned 185 participants to one of four names. Participants in the “clean meat” and “animal-free meat” conditions had significantly more positive attitudes than those in the “lab-grown meat” condition, and “clean meat” produced significantly more positive behavioural intentions than “lab-grown meat”; mediation analysis showed that the valence of the associations evoked by each name explained much of the difference. Since then the industry has largely adopted “cultivated meat”. Naming is also a legal battleground: in October 2024 the Court of Justice of the EU ruled (case C-438/23) that, in the absence of a legal name adopted at EU level, a Member State cannot prohibit the use of customary or descriptive terms such as “steak” for products containing plant proteins. The EU legislator then acted: Regulation (EU) 2026/1739, published in July 2026, reserves terms such as “steak”, “bacon” and “chicken” for meat from August 2029 and excludes them — and the term “meat” itself — for cell-cultured foods.

Information can backfire

Explaining a technology is not neutral. In two Swiss experiments, participants perceived cultured meat as unnatural, and informing them about how it is produced and about its benefits had the paradoxical effect of increasing acceptance of traditional meat; describing cultured meat in a non-technical way that emphasised the final product rather than the production method improved perceptions (Siegrist et al., 2018). The lesson for communicators: lead with the product and what it offers, not with the bioreactor.

Labels

Mandatory labels carry information that consumers read as risk signals. The EU authorisations of dried yellow mealworm (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/882) and partially defatted house-cricket powder (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/5) require a statement that the ingredient may cause allergic reactions in consumers with known allergies to crustaceans and dust mites (and, for cricket powder, molluscs). These statements are essential for safety — and you must include the same information in your informed-consent material (Lesson 13.3) — but they also make “insect” salient, which may raise disgust.

Social norms and nudges

A nudge changes the choice architecture — the order, default, visibility or social framing of options — without forbidding options or changing prices (Thaler & Sunstein, 2008). Two field studies show what this can achieve for sustainable diets:

  • Dynamic norms. Messages that more and more people are reducing their meat consumption (a dynamic norm) increased interest in eating less meat even where the static norm was to eat meat, and doubled meatless orders at a café (Sparkman & Walton, 2017).
  • Availability. In three cafeterias at an English university (94 644 meals), doubling the proportion of vegetarian options from 25 % to 50 % increased vegetarian meal sales by 14.9 and 14.5 percentage points in the observational study and by 7.8 percentage points in the experimental study — proportional increases of 61.8 %, 78.8 % and 40.8 % — with no detectable rebound at other mealtimes, and the largest effects among diners who previously chose vegetarian meals least (Garnett et al., 2019).

Effect sizes in such studies are reported in two ways that are easy to confuse.

Percentage points versus relative change(13.2.6)
\[ \Delta_{\text{pp}} = p_1 - p_0, \qquad \Delta_{\text{rel}} = \frac{p_1 - p_0}{p_0} = \frac{\Delta_{\text{pp}}}{p_0} \]

the absolute change, in percentage points, is the new share minus the old share; the relative change is that difference divided by the old share.

A campaign that raises insect-bread purchases from 1 % to 2 % of bread sales is a 100 % relative increase but only 1 pp. Relative changes sound impressive when baselines are small; absolute changes tell you the impact on the food system. Report both, and always state the baseline.

The attitude–behaviour gap and lessons from GMOs

Consumers in surveys are more positive about sustainable food than their shopping baskets suggest. Vermeir and Verbeke (2006) studied this attitude–behavioural intention gap for sustainable dairy products among young Belgian consumers and found that positive attitudes were often not matched by intentions, partly because of low perceived availability and low perceived consumer effectiveness. Between intention and behaviour there is a second gap: people who intend but do not act (“inclined abstainers”) are the main reason why intentions explain only about a quarter of behavioural variance (Sheeran, 2002). For novel foods the gap has concrete causes — the product is not in the shop, costs more, or the first taste disappoints.

What did the GMO controversy teach? First, opposition can be absolute rather than calculative. In a US survey representative on gender, age and income, 64 % opposed genetic modification, and 71 % of the opponents — 45 % of the whole sample — were “absolutely” opposed, agreeing that GM should be prohibited whatever the risks and benefits; absolutists were more disgust sensitive, and disgust predicted support for legal restrictions even after controlling for risk–benefit assessments (Scott et al., 2016). Arguments about risks and benefits will not reach such opponents. Second, confidence is not knowledge (Fernbach et al., 2019). Third, framing the technology around its producers' benefits (herbicide tolerance for farmers) rather than consumers' benefits gave consumers risk without personal benefit — a combination the affect heuristic punishes. Fourth, governance matters: labelling, traceability and transparent regulation are prerequisites for trust. All four lessons apply to cultivated meat, where consumer research to date is dominated by intentions towards a product few people have tasted (Bryant & Barnett, 2020).

History

Political reactions can pre-empt markets. Italy passed a law banning the production and marketing of cultivated meat in 2023, and several US states (among them Florida and Alabama) have enacted bans — while Singapore (2020), the United States (2023) and Israel (2024) approved cultivated products. Whether such bans reflect or shape public opinion is itself a research question in the psychology of acceptance.

Measuring attitudes: Likert scales, reliability and validity

Every model in this lesson rests on questionnaire measurements. In your project you will likely measure attitudes toward the novel food, trust, perhaps the FNS and FTNS (Lesson 13.1). The dominant format is the Likert scale (Likert, 1932): several statements, each answered on an ordered agreement scale (typically 5 or 7 points), summed or averaged into a scale score. The key insight is that a single item is noisy, but the average of several items measuring the same construct is much more reliable. How reliable? Classical test theory answers the question.

Cronbach's α — internal-consistency reliability(13.2.7)
\[ \alpha = \frac{k}{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} s_i^2}{s_X^2}\right), \qquad X = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i \]

alpha equals the number of items divided by the number of items minus one, times one minus the ratio of the sum of the item variances to the variance of the total score.

\(k\)number of items–4–13
\(x_i\)score on item \(i\) (after reverse-scoring)scale points1–7
\(s_i^2\)sample variance of item \(i\) across respondentspoints²1–3
\(X\)total (summed) scorepoints
\(s_X^2\)sample variance of the total scorepoints²
\(\alpha\)reliability coefficient–0.70–0.95 desirable
Derivation from classical test theory
  1. Model. Each observed item score is a true score plus random error: \(x_i = t_i + e_i\), with errors uncorrelated with each other and with true scores.
  2. Reliability of the total \(X = \sum x_i\) is defined as the share of its variance that is true-score variance: \(\rho_{XX'} = \sigma_T^2/\sigma_X^2\), where \(T = \sum t_i\).
  3. Variance of a sum. \(\sigma_X^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{ij}\): the item variances plus all \(k(k-1)\) covariances.
  4. Covariances carry only true score. Because errors are uncorrelated, \(\sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{cov}(t_i, t_j)\) for \(i \neq j\). If the items are (essentially) tau-equivalent — they measure the same true score, \(t_i = t + c_i\) — then every covariance equals \(\sigma_t^2\).
  5. True-score variance of the total. \(T = kt + \sum c_i\), so \(\sigma_T^2 = k^2\sigma_t^2\). The sum of all covariances is \(k(k-1)\sigma_t^2\). Hence \(\sigma_T^2 = \dfrac{k}{k-1}\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{ij}\).
  6. Substitute. \(\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_X^2 - \sum_i\sigma_i^2\), so \(\rho_{XX'} = \dfrac{k}{k-1}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_X^2 - \sum\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_X^2} = \dfrac{k}{k-1}\left(1-\dfrac{\sum\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_X^2}\right)\) — Cronbach's α (Cronbach, 1951).
  7. Estimate by replacing population variances with sample variances. If items are not tau-equivalent, α is a lower bound to reliability.
Assumptions and validity

α assumes unidimensionality and uncorrelated errors. It increases with the number of items even if they are only weakly related, so a high α does not prove that a scale measures one thing. Values above about 0.95 may indicate redundant items (Tavakol & Dennick, 2011). Negative α almost always means that reverse-worded items were not recoded. With small samples (n ≈ 20–30, as in a student panel) α itself is imprecise — report it, but do not over-interpret the second decimal.

\(s_i^2\) and \(s_X^2\) are both in points², so their ratio and α are dimensionless ✓

\[ \alpha_{\text{std}} = \frac{k\,\bar r}{1 + (k-1)\,\bar r}, \qquad \alpha^{*} = \frac{m\,\alpha}{1 + (m-1)\,\alpha} \;\Leftrightarrow\; m = \frac{\alpha^{*}(1-\alpha)}{\alpha(1-\alpha^{*})} \]

standardised alpha depends only on the number of items and their average inter-item correlation; lengthening a test by a factor m with parallel items raises reliability according to the Spearman–Brown formula, which can be solved for the lengthening factor needed to reach a target reliability.

Follow the derivation
  1. For standardised items every \(s_i^2 = 1\) and every covariance equals a correlation. Then \(\sum s_i^2 = k\) and \(s_X^2 = k + k(k-1)\bar r\).
  2. Insert into Eq. 13.2.7: \(\alpha = \dfrac{k}{k-1}\cdot\dfrac{k(k-1)\bar r}{k + k(k-1)\bar r} = \dfrac{k\bar r}{1 + (k-1)\bar r}\).
  3. A test of \(k\) items with reliability \(\alpha\) implies \(\bar r = \alpha/(k - (k-1)\alpha)\). Lengthening to \(mk\) items with the same \(\bar r\) gives \(\alpha^* = m\alpha/(1+(m-1)\alpha)\) — the Spearman–Brown formula.
  4. Solve for \(m\): \(\alpha^*(1 + (m-1)\alpha) = m\alpha\) → \(m = \dfrac{\alpha^*(1-\alpha)}{\alpha(1-\alpha^*)}\).
Assumptions and validity

New items must be as good as the old ones (parallel). In practice longer questionnaires cause fatigue and careless answering, which lowers data quality — for a tasting session keep the whole questionnaire under about 10 minutes.

Validity: are you measuring the right thing?

Reliability is necessary but not sufficient. A bathroom scale that always reads 3 kg too heavy is perfectly reliable and invalid. For attitude scales, collect evidence of content validity (do the items cover the construct? ask experts and pilot respondents), construct validity (does the scale correlate with related constructs — convergent — and not with unrelated ones — discriminant? FNS should correlate with willingness to try unfamiliar foods but hardly with, say, height) and criterion or predictive validity (does the score predict behaviour, such as tasting the cricket bread?). Translating a scale into Swedish requires back-translation and a new check of reliability and factor structure, as the cross-national FNS studies show (Ritchey et al., 2003).

Practical rules for your questionnaire

Use existing validated scales where possible (FNS, FTNS, FCQ) and cite them. Keep the response format identical within a scale. Include a few reverse-worded items to detect acquiescence (agreeing with everything), but recode them before analysis. Pilot the questionnaire on 3–5 people. Report n, mean, SD and α for every multi-item scale.

Public engagement and deliberation

If acceptance depends on trust, values and identity, then one-way communication (“educating the public”) is not enough. Research and innovation policy has therefore moved towards responsible innovation: “taking care of the future through collective stewardship of science and innovation in the present”. Stilgoe, Owen and Macnaghten (2013) proposed four dimensions: anticipation (systematically thinking through possible futures and impacts), reflexivity (innovators examining their own assumptions and values), inclusion (opening up the process to publics and stakeholders early — “upstream”) and responsiveness (the capacity to change direction in response to what is learned).

Practical forms include citizens' juries and panels, deliberative workshops, consensus conferences and participatory foresight. Their value is not that they produce acceptance; it is that they surface the conditions under which a technology would be acceptable — who owns it, who benefits, how it is labelled and regulated, what happens to farmers. Gupta, Fischer and Frewer (2012), reviewing socio-psychological determinants of technology acceptance, highlight that perceived risks, benefits, trust, knowledge and individual differences all matter, and that their weight differs between technologies — an argument for technology-specific engagement rather than generic campaigns. For cultivated meat in the Nordic countries, beliefs about whether society needs such an industry were among the strongest predictors of intentions (Engel et al., 2024): a question about purpose and values, not about safety data.

Key idea

Acceptance is not a property of consumers to be “overcome”; it is a relationship between a technology, the institutions that govern it and the people asked to eat it. Measuring it well (this lesson and Lesson 13.4) and engaging people early are two sides of the same coin.

Key terms

Psychometric paradigm
The approach of rating hazards on qualitative characteristics and factor-analysing the ratings to explain lay risk perception.
Dread risk
Risk factor capturing lack of control, catastrophic and fatal potential, involuntariness and inequity.
Unknown risk
Risk factor capturing unobservable, delayed, new and scientifically uncertain hazards.
Affect heuristic
Judging risks and benefits from an overall good/bad feeling, producing an inverse relation between perceived risk and benefit.
Social trust
Willingness to rely on institutions or experts for managing risks one cannot assess oneself; acts on perceived risks and benefits.
Theory of planned behaviour
Model in which attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control determine intention, which with PBC determines behaviour.
Technology acceptance model
Model in which perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use determine the intention to use a technology.
Framing
Presenting the same information in a way that changes its evaluation, e.g., naming cultivated meat.
Nudge
A change in choice architecture that alters behaviour predictably without forbidding options or changing economic incentives.
Attitude–behaviour gap
The discrepancy between positive attitudes or intentions and actual behaviour.
Likert scale
A multi-item attitude measure in which agreement ratings with several statements are summed or averaged.
Cronbach's α
Internal-consistency reliability: \(\frac{k}{k-1}(1-\sum s_i^2/s_X^2)\); a lower bound to reliability.
Validity
The degree to which a scale measures the intended construct (content, construct and criterion validity).
Responsible innovation
Governance approach built on anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion and responsiveness.
