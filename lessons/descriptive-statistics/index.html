Describing and visualising data

Harvest day: eighteen compartments of lettuce, 72 plants, a spreadsheet full of numbers. Before any hypothesis test, you have to see the data and summarise them honestly — where they are centred, how much they vary, whether one rotten plant is dragging the mean down. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the equations of descriptive statistics, explains the single most common error in scientific figures (confusing the standard deviation with the standard error), and shows how to draw graphs that let readers judge your results for themselves.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify variables by measurement scale and organise project data in a tidy table (LO4, LO5).
  • Calculate and choose between measures of location (mean, median, mode, trimmed mean) and spread (range, IQR, variance, SD, CV, MAD), and derive Bessel's \(n-1\) correction (LO4).
  • Explain the difference between the standard deviation and the standard error, convert between them, and state which one a figure should show (LO4).
  • Construct and interpret quantiles, box plots, histograms and kernel density estimates, and use z-scores with the normal distribution (LO4, LO5).
  • Detect outliers with the 1.5 × IQR rule and decide, with justification, how to handle them (LO4).
  • Round and report results with appropriate precision and design honest graphs with correctly labelled error bars (LO4).

Data, variables and scales of measurement

Statistics begins with the question: what kind of number is this? A lettuce's fresh mass of 148.3 g, its tipburn score of 2, its cultivar "Rex" and the temperature of 21.4 °C in its compartment are all recorded in the same spreadsheet, but they support very different operations. You can say that a 150 g plant is twice as heavy as a 75 g plant; you cannot say that a tipburn score of 2 is twice as bad as 1, and 21 °C is not "twice as warm" as 10.5 °C. The psychologist S. S. Stevens (1946) organised these differences into four scales of measurement, which still guide the choice of summary statistics and tests.

Variables are also discrete (countable values: number of leaves, number of panellists who chose sample A) or continuous (any value within a range, limited only by the instrument: mass, length, time). Many instruments turn continuous quantities into discrete readings — a balance with 0.1 g resolution — which matters when you decide how many digits to report (Section 3.2.8).

A practical note on Likert and hedonic scales

The 9-point hedonic scale used in sensory studies ("dislike extremely" … "like extremely") is strictly ordinal: nobody guarantees that the step from 5 to 6 is as large as the step from 8 to 9. In practice, sensory scientists usually analyse hedonic means with ANOVA, because the results are robust and interpretable — but you should know that this is an approximation and check it against rank-based methods when distributions are very skewed (Lesson 13.4).

Tidy data

Before you compute anything, arrange the data so that the computer can understand them. In a tidy data set, each variable is a column, each observation is a row, and each type of observational unit forms its own table (Wickham, 2014). The data sheet of Lesson 3.1 is tidy: one row per measurement of one plant on one date, with columns for identifiers (jar, block, treatment), the variable name, the value and the unit. Tidy data can be filtered, grouped and summarised with a few lines of code or a pivot table; data with merged header cells, colour-coded treatments and units written inside the numbers ("148 g") cannot.

Measures of location: where are the data centred?

Let us work with a real-looking example throughout this lesson. Ten lettuce plants from one deep-water-culture tank were harvested 35 days after transplanting. Their shoot fresh masses (g) were:

142.3 · 156.8 · 138.4 · 149.9 · 161.2 · 145.7 · 152.3 · 97.6 · 147.1 · 158.4

One plant, 97.6 g, had brown, slimy roots at harvest — a sign of Pythium root rot (Lesson 5.3). Keep an eye on it: it will teach us the difference between robust and non-robust statistics.

Arithmetic mean(3.2.1)
\[ \bar x = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \qquad\text{with the property}\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar x\right) = 0 \]

the sample mean is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations; the deviations of the observations from their mean always add up to zero — the mean is the "balance point" of the data.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\bar x\)sample mean ("x-bar"), an estimate of the population mean \(\mu\)same as \(x\), e.g., g
\(x_i\)the \(i\)-th observationg
\(n\)number of observations–
Follow the derivation: why the mean is the least-squares centre
  1. Ask: which single number \(c\) is "closest" to all observations, if closeness is measured by the sum of squared deviations \(S(c) = \sum (x_i-c)^2\)?
  2. Differentiate with respect to \(c\): \(S'(c) = -2\sum(x_i - c)\). Setting \(S'(c)=0\) gives \(\sum x_i = nc\), i.e., \(c = \bar x\). The second derivative \(2n > 0\) confirms a minimum.
  3. The zero-sum property follows directly: \(\sum(x_i-\bar x) = \sum x_i - n\bar x = 0\).
  4. The median minimises instead the sum of absolute deviations \(\sum|x_i-c|\). Squares punish large deviations heavily, which is why a single extreme value pulls the mean much more than the median.
Assumptions and validity

Meaningful for interval and ratio data. For strongly right-skewed ratio data (e.g., bacterial counts, or plant masses spanning several orders of magnitude), the geometric mean \(\left(\prod x_i\right)^{1/n}\) — the mean on a log scale — is often a better summary.

\(\dfrac{1}{-}\times\text{g} = \text{g}\) ✓

Other measures of location answer slightly different questions:

  • The median is the middle value of the sorted data (the average of the two middle values when \(n\) is even). Half of the observations lie below it, half above.
  • The mode is the most frequent value or category. It is the only measure of location for nominal data ("the most common system type in the class was Kratky").
  • The trimmed mean discards a fixed fraction of the smallest and largest observations (often 10 % at each end) and averages the rest — a compromise between the efficiency of the mean and the robustness of the median.

Measures of spread: how much do the data vary?

Two treatments can have identical means and completely different behaviour. For a grower, a uniform crop of 150 g heads is worth more than a mixture of 100 g and 200 g heads with the same average, because every head must meet a minimum market weight. Variability is therefore not a nuisance to hide; it is a result to report.

The simplest measure, the range (maximum − minimum), depends entirely on the two most extreme observations and grows with sample size. The interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1, Section 3.2.5) describes the spread of the middle half of the data and is robust. The workhorse, however, is the variance and its square root, the standard deviation.

Sample variance and standard deviation (with Bessel's correction)(3.2.2)
\[ s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar x\right)^2, \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2} \]

the sample variance is the sum of the squared deviations from the sample mean divided by the number of observations minus one; the standard deviation is its square root and has the same unit as the data.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(s^2\)sample variance, an unbiased estimate of the population variance \(\sigma^2\)g²
\(s\)sample standard deviation (SD)g
\(n-1\)degrees of freedom of the deviations–
Follow the derivation: why divide by n − 1 and not n?
  1. Let the observations be independent draws from a population with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), so \(E[(x_i-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2\).
  2. We would like to average \((x_i-\mu)^2\), but \(\mu\) is unknown, so we use \(\bar x\) instead. Add and subtract \(\mu\): \(x_i - \bar x = (x_i-\mu) - (\bar x-\mu)\).
  3. Square and sum: \(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2 = \sum(x_i-\mu)^2 - 2(\bar x-\mu)\sum(x_i-\mu) + n(\bar x-\mu)^2\).
  4. Since \(\sum(x_i-\mu) = n(\bar x-\mu)\), the middle term is \(-2n(\bar x-\mu)^2\), and the sum simplifies to \(\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2 = \sum(x_i-\mu)^2 - n(\bar x-\mu)^2\).
  5. Take expectations. \(E\left[\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\right] = n\sigma^2\). The variance of the mean of \(n\) independent observations is \(\sigma^2/n\) (Eq. 3.2.4), so \(E\left[n(\bar x-\mu)^2\right] = n\cdot\sigma^2/n = \sigma^2\).
  6. Therefore \(E\left[\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\right] = n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n-1)\sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n-1\) gives an estimator whose expected value is exactly \(\sigma^2\) (unbiased); dividing by \(n\) underestimates \(\sigma^2\) by the factor \((n-1)/n\).
  7. Intuition: the sample mean is, by construction, the point closest to the data (Eq. 3.2.1), so deviations from \(\bar x\) are systematically a little smaller than deviations from the true \(\mu\). Equivalently, the \(n\) deviations must sum to zero, so only \(n-1\) of them are free: they carry \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.
Assumptions and validity

Unbiasedness requires independent observations with a common variance; no normality is needed. Note that \(s\) itself is still slightly biased low (the square root of an unbiased estimator is not unbiased), but the correction is small and conventionally ignored. When the data are the whole population (e.g., all 18 compartments in your room, described rather than generalised), divide by \(n\).

\(\dfrac{\text{g}^2}{-} = \text{g}^2\) for \(s^2\); \(\sqrt{\text{g}^2} = \text{g}\) for \(s\) ✓

When is the CV useful — and when is it meaningless?

The CV lets you compare variability across variables with different units or magnitudes (fresh mass in g versus leaf area in cm²), across crops of different size, or against published experiments (it is also the quantity needed for power calculations, Lesson 3.1). It is only meaningful for ratio data: a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C has a "CV" of 10 %, but the same temperatures in kelvin (293 ± 2 K) give 0.7 % — the value depends on the arbitrary zero. Never compute a CV for °C, pH or hedonic scores.

\(\text{g}/\text{g}\) — dimensionless (quoted in %) ✓

A robust alternative to the SD is the median absolute deviation, \(\text{MAD} = \operatorname{median}\left(|x_i - \operatorname{median}(x)|\right)\). For the tank data, MAD = 7.25 g, while the SD is 18.1 g; without the diseased plant, the SD drops to 7.6 g. For normally distributed data, \(1.4826\times\text{MAD}\) estimates \(\sigma\), which makes the MAD a handy check: if \(s\) is much larger than \(1.48\,\text{MAD}\), one or a few extreme values dominate.

Standard deviation versus standard error

This is the most important distinction in the lesson, and the most frequently botched one in published figures (Altman & Bland, 2005; Cumming et al., 2007). The two quantities answer different questions:

  • The standard deviation (SD) describes the data: how much individual plants differ from one another. It is a property of the population you sampled and does not shrink as you measure more plants — it just gets estimated more reliably.
  • The standard error of the mean (SE or SEM) describes the estimate: how much the sample mean would vary if you repeated the whole experiment. It shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\).
Standard error of the mean(3.2.4)
\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad s = \text{SE}\,\sqrt{n} \]

the variance of a sample mean is the population variance divided by the sample size; so the standard error of the mean is estimated by the sample standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size, and conversely an SD can be recovered from a reported SE by multiplying by the square root of n.

SymbolMeaningUnit
SEstandard error of the mean: the SD of the sampling distribution of \(\bar x\)g
\(s\)sample SD of the observationsg
\(n\)number of independent observations (experimental units!)–
Follow the derivation
  1. \(\bar x = \frac1n\sum x_i\). For a constant \(a\), \(\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum x_i\right)\).
  2. For independent observations, variances add: \(\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum x_i\right) = \sum\operatorname{Var}(x_i) = n\sigma^2\).
  3. Hence \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = n\sigma^2/n^2 = \sigma^2/n\), and its square root is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). Replacing the unknown \(\sigma\) by \(s\) gives the SE.
  4. The independence assumption in step 2 is exactly what pseudo-replication violates: if plants share a tank, their deviations are correlated and \(\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum x_i\right) > n\sigma^2\) (Lesson 3.1, Eq. 3.1.3).
Assumptions and validity

Independent observations, finite variance. No normality assumption is needed for the formula itself; normality (or a large \(n\), by the central limit theorem) is needed to turn the SE into a confidence interval (Lesson 3.3).

\(\text{g}/\sqrt{-} = \text{g}\) ✓
Common misconception

"Error bars show the variation of our plants, and SE bars are smaller, so they look better." SE bars do not show the variation of the plants — they show the uncertainty of the mean. Choosing SE because it looks tidier misleads readers into thinking the plants were more uniform than they were. Weissgerber et al. (2015) found that in physiology papers most bar graphs showed mean ± SE, often without saying so. Rule: to describe variability, show the SD (or better, all data points); to show how precisely a mean is known, show a 95 % confidence interval. And always write in the caption which one you used, and \(n\).

A paper reports that lettuce under a new LED fixture yielded "182 ± 6.4 g (mean ± SE, n = 8)". You want to use this in your power calculation. What is the SD, the CV, and the 95 % confidence interval of the mean?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. SD from SE (Eq. 3.2.4): \(s = 6.4\times\sqrt8 = 6.4\times2.828 = 18.1\) g.
  2. CV: \(18.1/182 = 0.099\), i.e., about 10 %. This, not \(6.4/182 = 3.5\) %, is the CV to use in Eq. 3.1.7.
  3. 95 % CI: with \(n-1 = 7\) degrees of freedom the t quantile is 2.365 (Lesson 3.3), so the half-width is \(2.365\times6.4 = 15.1\) g and the interval is 167–197 g.
  4. Sanity check. Individual plants would typically lie within about ±2 SD, i.e., roughly 146–218 g — a much wider band than "182 ± 6.4" suggests at first glance.

Seeing the whole distribution: quantiles, box plots, histograms and densities

Two numbers (a mean and an SD) cannot describe the shape of a distribution: is it symmetric or skewed, does it have one peak or two, are there stragglers? Quantiles and graphical summaries answer these questions.

Sample quantile (Hyndman–Fan type 7) and Tukey's fences(3.2.5)
\[ Q(p) = x_{(k)} + (h-k)\left(x_{(k+1)}-x_{(k)}\right),\quad h = (n-1)p + 1,\ k = \lfloor h\rfloor; \qquad \text{fences} = \left[Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR},\ Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}\right] \]

to find the p-quantile, sort the data, compute the fractional position h, take the order statistic at the integer part of h and interpolate linearly towards the next one; observations outside the fences, one and a half interquartile ranges beyond the quartiles, are flagged as potential outliers.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x_{(k)}\)\(k\)-th smallest observation (order statistic)g
\(p\)probability level: 0.25 for Q1, 0.5 for the median, 0.75 for Q3–
\(h\), \(k\)fractional position and its integer part–
IQRinterquartile range \(Q_3 - Q_1\)g
Why are there several definitions of a quantile?

With a finite sample, "the value below which 25 % of the data lie" is ambiguous: between two sorted observations, where exactly? Hyndman and Fan (1996) catalogued nine definitions used by statistical packages. Type 7, used here, is the default in R, in Excel's PERCENTILE.INC and QUARTILE.INC, and in this course's stats.js. Other packages can give slightly different quartiles for small samples; state your software when it matters.

Where does 1.5 × IQR come from?

John Tukey proposed the rule in his book Exploratory Data Analysis (1977) as a practical screening device. For normally distributed data, the fences lie at about ±2.7σ from the mean, so only about 0.7 % of observations from a normal population fall outside them. It is a flag for "look at this plant", not a rule for deletion.

Quantiles and fences have the unit of the data (g) ✓

A histogram divides the measurement axis into bins and counts the observations in each. Its appearance depends strongly on the bin width: too narrow and it is noisy, too wide and it hides structure such as two peaks. The Freedman–Diaconis rule, bin width \(= 2\,\text{IQR}\,n^{-1/3}\), is a good starting point (Freedman & Diaconis, 1981). A kernel density estimate (KDE) replaces each observation by a small smooth bump and adds the bumps, giving a smooth curve instead of steps.

Gaussian kernel density estimate with Silverman's bandwidth(3.2.6)
\[ \hat f(x) = \frac{1}{n\,h}\sum_{i=1}^{n} K\!\left(\frac{x-x_i}{h}\right),\quad K(u) = \frac{e^{-u^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}; \qquad h = 0.9\,\min\!\left(s,\ \frac{\text{IQR}}{1.34}\right)n^{-1/5} \]

the estimated density at any value x is the average, over all observations, of a bell-shaped kernel centred on each observation and scaled by the bandwidth h; Silverman's rule of thumb chooses h from the smaller of the SD and the IQR divided by 1.34, shrinking slowly as the sample grows.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\hat f(x)\)estimated probability density at \(x\)g⁻¹
\(K(u)\)kernel: the standard normal density–
\(h\)bandwidth (the SD of each bump)g
Follow the reasoning
  1. Each term \(\frac1h K\left(\frac{x-x_i}{h}\right)\) is a normal density with mean \(x_i\) and SD \(h\); it integrates to 1.
  2. Averaging \(n\) such densities (the factor \(1/n\)) gives a function that also integrates to 1 — a valid probability density.
  3. The bandwidth plays the role of the bin width: small \(h\) gives a wiggly curve that follows every observation; large \(h\) oversmooths.
  4. Silverman (1986) derived \(h\) by minimising the mean integrated squared error for a normal reference distribution; using \(\min(s, \text{IQR}/1.34)\) makes the rule less sensitive to outliers and bimodality (for a normal distribution IQR ≈ 1.34σ).
\(\dfrac{1}{\text{g}}\times(-) = \text{g}^{-1}\): multiplied by a mass interval, it gives a probability ✓

The normal distribution and z-scores

Many biological measurements — the masses of plants grown under uniform conditions, measurement errors of a balance, the averages of many small independent influences — are approximately normally distributed: symmetric and bell-shaped. The normal distribution is completely described by two parameters, its mean \(\mu\) and SD \(\sigma\).

Normal probability density and the z-score(3.2.7)
\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right], \qquad z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \]

the density of a normal distribution falls off symmetrically and exponentially with the squared distance from the mean, measured in units of the standard deviation; the z-score expresses any observation as the number of standard deviations it lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(f(x)\)probability densityg⁻¹
\(\mu\), \(\sigma\)population mean and SDg
\(z\)standardised score; \(z\sim N(0,1)\) if \(x\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\)–
Properties worth memorising
  1. About 68 % of values lie within \(\mu\pm1\sigma\), 95 % within \(\mu\pm1.96\sigma\) (≈ 2σ), and 99.7 % within \(\mu\pm3\sigma\) — the "68–95–99.7 rule".
  2. The cumulative probability \(\Phi(z) = P(Z\le z)\) has no closed form; it is tabulated or computed numerically (as in the calculator below and in stats.js).
  3. Sums and means of independent normal variables are normal; by the central limit theorem, means of any reasonable distribution become approximately normal as \(n\) grows (Lesson 3.3).
  4. The factor \(1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\) makes the total area equal to 1, using \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2/2}\,du = \sqrt{2\pi}\).
Assumptions and validity

Real data are never exactly normal: fresh masses cannot be negative, and skewness appears when a few plants are stunted. Check the shape with a histogram or QQ plot (Lesson 3.3) before using normal probabilities for individual observations.

\(z = \text{g}/\text{g}\) is dimensionless; \(f\) has unit g⁻¹ because \(1/\sigma\) does ✓
Worked example 5How unusual is this plant?

In a large, uniform batch of butterhead lettuce, fresh mass at harvest is approximately normal with \(\mu = 148\) g and \(\sigma = 12\) g. (a) What fraction of heads exceed 165 g? (b) A retailer requires at least 130 g per head. What fraction fails? (c) What is the z-score of the diseased 97.6 g plant, and what does it tell you?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(z = (165-148)/12 = 1.42\). \(\Phi(1.42) = 0.922\), so \(1-0.922 = 0.078\): about 8 % of heads exceed 165 g.
  2. (b) \(z = (130-148)/12 = -1.50\). \(\Phi(-1.50) = 0.067\): about 7 % of heads are below the market weight.
  3. (c) \(z = (97.6-148)/12 = -4.2\). Under a normal model, a value this low occurs in fewer than 2 in 100 000 plants. The plant is not simply "at the low end" of the same distribution; it belongs to a different process (disease). This is useful evidence when you justify excluding it — together with the documented root symptoms.
  4. Sanity check. The 68–95–99.7 rule says ~2.5 % of values lie beyond +2σ = 172 g; 165 g is less extreme, so a result of 8 % above 165 g is plausible.
(a) ≈ 8 %; (b) ≈ 7 %; (c) z ≈ −4.2: not an ordinary member of the distribution

Outliers: flag, investigate, decide — never delete blindly

An outlier is an observation that lies far from the bulk of the data. The 1.5 × IQR rule and z-scores flag candidates; they do not tell you what to do. Outliers arise for three very different reasons, each calling for a different response:

  1. Errors — a typing mistake (164.5 recorded as 16.45), a balance not tared, a sensor spike. If you can prove the error from the notebook or the raw file, correct it (never silently) or remove the value.
  2. A different process — a plant with root rot, a jar that leaked and dried out, a seedling transplanted a week late. The observation is real but does not answer your research question. Exclude it only if the exclusion criterion was defined in advance (your preregistration, Lesson 3.1) or can be justified by an independent observation (root symptoms), and report every exclusion.
  3. Genuine extreme values — the natural tail of the distribution. Deleting them makes your data look cleaner than nature is, underestimates the SD and biases tests. Keep them; if they are influential, use robust statistics or show results with and without them (a sensitivity analysis).
Sensor data are different

Automatic outlier removal is appropriate for high-frequency sensor streams — a temperature logger recording every minute occasionally produces a physically impossible spike. Rolling medians and plausibility limits are standard tools there (Lesson 9.3 and the anomaly-detection lab). The rules for experimental response data are stricter, because each value is an independent experimental unit.

Significant figures, rounding and reporting precision

A calculator happily reports a mean of 144.97 g and an SD of 18.138482 g. Neither the balance (0.1 g) nor the biology justify eight digits. Excess digits imply precision that does not exist; too few digits throw information away. Four practical rules:

  1. Round the uncertainty first: quote an SD, SE or confidence half-width to two significant figures (18 g, 5.7 g) — or three if the leading digit is 1 (18.1 g). The Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement recommends at most two significant digits for uncertainties (JCGM, 2008).
  2. Round the estimate to the same decimal place as its uncertainty: 145.0 ± 18.1 g, not 144.97 ± 18.1 g, and not 145 ± 18.138 g.
  3. Keep full precision in calculations; round only when reporting. Rounding intermediate results (e.g., using a rounded mean to compute deviations) accumulates error.
  4. Report p-values to two or three decimals (p = .032), and as p < .001 when smaller (Lesson 3.4). Test statistics to two decimals (t = 2.31).

Units follow the SI conventions (BIPM, 2019): a space between number and unit (148 g, not 148g), unit symbols in upright type, no plural "gs", and derived units written as µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ or µmol/(m² s) — never "µmol/m²/s", which is ambiguous.

A spreadsheet reports for the far-red treatment: mean 166.416667 g, SD 8.650876 g, n = 6, SE 3.531681 g. Write the result for the text of a report, with the SD, and separately with a 95 % CI (t quantile 2.571).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. SD to two significant figures: 8.7 g. Mean to the same decimal place: 166.4 g.
  2. 95 % CI half-width: \(2.571\times3.5317 = 9.08\) g → 9.1 g; interval \(166.4\pm9.1\) g, i.e., 157.3–175.5 g.

Honest graphs and what error bars mean

Graphs are how most readers experience your results; many never read the numbers. Edward Tufte (2001) argued that good graphics show the data, maximise the share of ink devoted to data ("data-ink ratio"), avoid decoration that carries no information ("chartjunk"), and never distort. He quantified distortion with the lie factor.

Tufte's lie factor(3.2.8)
\[ \text{LF} = \frac{\text{size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{size of effect in the data}}, \qquad \text{effect} = \frac{\text{second value}-\text{first value}}{\text{first value}} \]

the lie factor is the relative change a reader perceives from the graphic divided by the relative change actually present in the numbers; an honest graphic has a lie factor close to one.

The classic trap: truncated bar charts

A bar encodes a value by its length, so the bar must start at zero. Suppose W gives 150 g and W+FR 165 g — a 10 % increase. If the y-axis starts at 140 g, the bars are 10 and 25 units tall: a 150 % increase on the page, a lie factor of 15. Dot plots and line graphs encode values by position and may use a truncated axis — but then there must be no bars.

Ratio of two dimensionless relative changes — dimensionless ✓

The dominant chart type in biology papers — the bar graph with an error bar ("dynamite plot") — is also one of the least informative for continuous data. Weissgerber et al. (2015) examined 703 papers in top physiology journals: 85.6 % presented continuous data in bar graphs, which hide the sample size, the distribution and any outliers, while the smallest group in a figure typically contained only about four observations. Their recommendation, now adopted by many journals, is simple: for small samples, show every data point (a dot or strip plot), with the mean or median and a correctly labelled interval.

What can a reader conclude from error bars? For two independent groups of similar size with \(n \ge 10\), Cumming and Finch (2005) give "rules of eye": if the 95 % confidence intervals overlap by about half of one arm, \(p \approx 0.05\); if they just touch, \(p \approx 0.01\). For SE bars, a gap of about one SE between the bars corresponds to \(p \approx 0.05\) and a gap of two SE to \(p \approx 0.01\) (Cumming et al., 2007) — so SE bars that merely do not overlap do not indicate a significant difference. With very small samples (\(n = 3\)) the intervals are so wide that even a full arm of overlap corresponds to \(p \approx 0.05\). For repeated measurements on the same units (paired data), no such visual rule works: the bars show between-unit variation, but the test uses within-unit differences.

Checklist for an honest figure
  • Show the individual data points whenever \(n\) is small (below about 20 per group).
  • Say in the caption what the centre and the bars represent (mean ± SD, median and IQR, mean with 95 % CI) and give \(n\) as the number of experimental units.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit: "Shoot fresh mass (g plant⁻¹)".
  • Start bar charts at zero; use the same axis range for panels that will be compared.
  • No 3D effects, shadows or gradient fills; minimise gridlines; use direct labels instead of legends where possible.
  • Use colour-blind-safe, perceptually uniform colours (avoid rainbow colour maps; Crameri et al., 2020), and never rely on red–green contrast alone.

Why summary statistics are never enough

In 1973, Francis Anscombe constructed four small data sets with identical means (\(\bar x = 9\), \(\bar y = 7.50\)), variances, correlation (\(r = 0.816\)) and regression line (\(y = 3.00 + 0.500x\)) — and completely different shapes (Anscombe, 1973). Matejka and Fitzmaurice (2017) generalised the trick into the "Datasaurus dozen", which includes a dinosaur with the same summary statistics as a featureless cloud. Plot first; summarise second.

Key terms

Scale of measurement
Nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio; determines which summaries and operations are meaningful (Stevens, 1946).
Tidy data
A table in which each variable is a column, each observation a row and each type of observational unit a separate table.
Median
The middle value of the sorted data; a robust measure of location.
Trimmed mean
The mean after discarding a fixed fraction of the smallest and largest observations.
Variance
The average squared deviation from the mean; the sample variance divides the sum of squares by n − 1.
Bessel's correction
Dividing by n − 1 instead of n so that the sample variance is an unbiased estimator of the population variance.
Standard deviation (SD)
The square root of the variance; describes the spread of individual observations.
Standard error of the mean (SE)
The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of the mean, estimated as s/√n; describes the precision of the mean.
Coefficient of variation (CV)
The SD as a percentage of the mean; meaningful only for ratio-scale data.
Interquartile range (IQR)
Q3 − Q1: the range of the middle 50 % of the data.
Median absolute deviation (MAD)
The median of the absolute deviations from the median; a robust measure of spread.
Box plot
A graphic showing the median, quartiles, whiskers to the most extreme values within 1.5 × IQR of the box, and individual outliers.
Kernel density estimate
A smooth estimate of a probability density obtained by summing a kernel (bump) centred on each observation.
z-score
The number of standard deviations an observation lies above or below the mean.
Lie factor
Tufte's ratio of the effect size shown in a graphic to the effect size in the data.
