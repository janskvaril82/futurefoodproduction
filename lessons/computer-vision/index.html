Computer vision and plant phenotyping

Photograph your lettuce from above and you have recorded twelve million measurements. Every pixel is a tiny light-sensor reading, and together they encode the plant's size, colour, health and even how many leaves it has. Computer vision is the engineering of turning those numbers into trustworthy biological quantities — projected leaf area in square centimetres, biomass in grams, the number of ripe tomatoes on a truss, the first signs of downy mildew. This lesson builds the pipeline from the pixel upwards: colour spaces, vegetation indices and a threshold you can derive by hand, the convolutional neural networks behind modern phenotyping, and the traps — lighting, perspective and dataset bias — that make a model that is 99 % accurate in a paper fail in your greenhouse. It ends with a protocol for measuring your own plants without harming them.

  • Convert pixel counts into projected leaf area with a reference object and camera geometry, correct for perspective, and calibrate projected area against biomass with a power-law model (LO4, LO5).
  • Explain convolution, pooling and feature maps in a convolutional neural network, calculate layer output sizes and parameter counts, and explain why transfer learning works (LO5).
  • Evaluate object detectors for counting fruit with intersection over union, non-maximum suppression, precision and recall, and critically assess disease-recognition claims in the light of dataset bias and domain shift (LO5, LO4).
  • Design and apply a photography protocol for non-destructive growth measurement of your own plants, and place it in the wider landscape of hyperspectral, 3D and high-throughput phenotyping (LO4, LO5).

Images as data: pixels, arrays and colour spaces

A digital camera is an array of millions of photodiodes, each behind a red, green or blue filter in a Bayer mosaic, as you saw in Lesson 9.1. After the camera has interpolated the mosaic, every picture element — every pixel — carries three numbers, \(R\), \(G\) and \(B\), usually stored as 8-bit integers from 0 to 255. An image of \(H\) rows and \(W\) columns is therefore a three-dimensional array of \(H\times W\times 3\) numbers: a 4000 × 3000 photo from a phone contains 36 million of them. To a computer, "image analysis" is arithmetic on this array — the same array operations you would write with NumPy in Python or, on this page, with typed arrays in the JavaScript that runs in your browser.

Three facts decide whether those numbers can be trusted as measurements. First, pixel values are relative, not absolute: exposure time, aperture, sensor gain and automatic white balance all rescale them, so the same leaf gives different numbers in two photos unless the settings are fixed. Second, standard JPEG images are gamma-encoded: the stored value is roughly proportional to the 1/2.4 power of the light (the sRGB transfer curve, with a short linear segment near black), so doubling the light does not double the number. Third, colour depends on the illumination spectrum: a leaf reflects the light it receives, so under the magenta light of a red–blue LED fixture a healthy leaf reflects mainly red and blue and looks purple to the camera. For quantitative work you therefore fix the camera settings, include a grey or colour reference card in every image and photograph under white light (Li et al., 2014).

Photograph under white light

Under red–blue LED lighting the green channel is starved: in Worked example 1 a leaf that has chromatic green coordinate \(g = 0.53\) under daylight drops to about \(g = 0.32\) under a red–blue spectrum — the value of brown peat in daylight. Every fixed colour rule fails (in Figure 4 the ExG − ExR > 0 rule then finds no plant at all), and although an adaptive threshold can sometimes still separate plant from background, the colours no longer mean anything physiological and the weak green channel is noisy. Phenotyping systems in LED growth rooms either switch the lamps to white for a few seconds while imaging, use a flash, or measure under a separate white light. Do the same in your project (Section 10.3.9).

Colour spaces

RGB mixes two kinds of information: how much light arrives (brightness) and what kind of light (chromaticity). Most segmentation errors in plant images come from the brightness part — a shadow cast by a neighbouring plant, a sunfleck, a passing cloud, a darker corner of the image. The first remedy is to divide each channel by the sum of all three.

Chromatic (normalised rgb) coordinates(10.3.1)
\[ r = \frac{R}{R+G+B},\qquad g = \frac{G}{R+G+B},\qquad b = \frac{B}{R+G+B},\qquad r+g+b = 1 \]

each chromatic coordinate is the fraction of the pixel's total signal that falls in one channel. Because the three fractions add up to one, only two of them are independent, and multiplying all three channels by the same factor — a shadow, a shorter exposure — leaves them unchanged.

Follow the derivation
  1. A uniform change of illumination or exposure multiplies every channel by the same factor \(k\): \(R' = kR\), \(G' = kG\), \(B' = kB\).
  2. Then \(r' = kR/(kR + kG + kB) = R/(R+G+B) = r\), and likewise for \(g\) and \(b\): the factor cancels. The chromatic coordinates are invariant to intensity.
  3. This invariance also survives gamma encoding: if the stored value is \(V = L^{1/\gamma}\), a light change by \(k\) multiplies every channel by \(k^{1/\gamma}\) — again the same factor for all three.
  4. Geometrically, dividing by the sum projects every point of the RGB cube along a ray through the origin onto the plane \(R+G+B = 1\) (Maxwell's colour triangle). Three-dimensional colour becomes two-dimensional chromaticity; brightness is thrown away on purpose.
Assumptions and validity

The invariance holds for changes of intensity only. A change of colour of the light (sun versus high-pressure sodium lamp versus LED) scales the channels by different factors and shifts \(r, g, b\); that needs white balancing with a grey card. Very dark pixels are unreliable, because dividing small, noisy numbers amplifies noise, and the coordinates are undefined for black (\(R+G+B = 0\)). Saturated (clipped) pixels at 255 also break the invariance.

\(\dfrac{\text{DN}}{\text{DN}+\text{DN}+\text{DN}}\) = dimensionless ✓ — the unknown camera scaling cancels too.

Humans describe colour differently: not as amounts of three primaries but as which colour (hue), how vivid (saturation) and how bright (value). The HSV colour space, introduced for computer graphics by Smith (1978), rearranges RGB into these three intuitive coordinates.

HSV: hue, saturation and value(10.3.2)
\[ V = \max(R,G,B),\qquad m = \min(R,G,B),\qquad S = \frac{V-m}{V} \] \[ H = 60^{\circ}\times\begin{cases}\dfrac{G-B}{V-m}\ \bmod 6 & (V=R)\\[8pt] \dfrac{B-R}{V-m}+2 & (V=G)\\[8pt] \dfrac{R-G}{V-m}+4 & (V=B)\end{cases} \]

the value is the largest of the three channels; the saturation is the difference between the largest and the smallest channel (\(m\)), relative to the largest — zero for greys; the hue is an angle around the colour wheel, from 0° (red) through 60° (yellow), 120° (green) and 240° (blue) back to 360°, found from which channel is largest and how the other two compare.

Follow the derivation
  1. Look at the RGB cube along its grey diagonal (from black to white). Its outline is a hexagon with the six corners red, yellow, green, cyan, blue and magenta, 60° apart. That is Smith's "hexcone" model.
  2. A pixel's hue is its angular position in that hexagon. If green is the largest channel, the pixel lies in the sector between yellow (60°) and cyan (180°), centred on green (120°) — hence the "+2" (2 × 60° = 120°).
  3. Within the sector, the balance of the two other channels, \((B-R)/(V-m)\), runs from −1 (at yellow) to +1 (at cyan) and shifts the angle by up to ±60°.
  4. Saturation measures the distance from the grey axis relative to brightness; value is simply the height in the cone.
Assumptions and validity

Hue is intensity-invariant like \(r, g, b\), but it is undefined for greys (\(V = m\)) and very unstable for weakly saturated pixels such as pale soil, white trays and specular reflections on wet leaves: a change of one digital number can swing the hue by tens of degrees. Hue is also circular — 359° and 1° are neighbours — so it cannot be thresholded naively around red. It works well for vivid, saturated leaves against unsaturated backgrounds.

\(S\): DN/DN = dimensionless; \(H\): degrees × (DN/DN) = degrees ✓.

Neither RGB nor HSV is designed so that equal numerical differences look equally different. The CIE 1976 \(L^*a^*b^*\) space (CIELAB) is: it transforms colour into a lightness \(L^*\) and two opponent axes that mimic how the visual system codes colour — \(a^*\) from green (negative) to red (positive), and \(b^*\) from blue (negative) to yellow (positive). For plants this is extremely useful: \(a^*\) separates green tissue from brown soil and from red or necrotic tissue, and \(b^*\) tracks yellowing (chlorosis), for example in nitrogen deficiency. In Lesson 10.2 you clustered leaves in exactly this \((a^*, b^*)\) plane.

From sRGB to CIELAB(10.3.3)
\[ L^{*} = 116\,f\!\left(\frac{Y}{Y_n}\right)-16 \] \[ a^{*} = 500\left[f\!\left(\frac{X}{X_n}\right)-f\!\left(\frac{Y}{Y_n}\right)\right],\qquad b^{*} = 200\left[f\!\left(\frac{Y}{Y_n}\right)-f\!\left(\frac{Z}{Z_n}\right)\right] \] \[ f(t) = \begin{cases} t^{1/3} & t > \delta^{3}\\ \dfrac{t}{3\delta^{2}}+\dfrac{4}{29} & t\le\delta^{3}\end{cases},\qquad \delta=\frac{6}{29} \] \[ \begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.4124 & 0.3576 & 0.1805\\ 0.2126 & 0.7152 & 0.0722\\ 0.0193 & 0.1192 & 0.9505\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}R_{\text{lin}}\\G_{\text{lin}}\\B_{\text{lin}}\end{pmatrix} \]

Follow the derivation step by step
  1. Linearise. The sRGB standard encodes light \(c_{\text{lin}}\) as \(1.055\,c_{\text{lin}}^{1/2.4}-0.055\) (with a linear toe near black). Inverting gives the formula in the table. Colour mixing is additive only in linear light, so this step is essential.
  2. Change of primaries. The rows of the matrix are the \(X, Y, Z\) contributions of the sRGB red, green and blue primaries. The middle row gives luminance: green contributes 72 % of perceived brightness, blue only 7 %.
  3. Normalise by white. Dividing by the white point (the tristimulus values of a perfect white under the D65 "daylight" illuminant) makes the coordinates relative to the illumination — a built-in white balance.
  4. Compress. Perceived lightness grows roughly as the cube root of luminance, so \(f(t) = t^{1/3}\); the linear piece for very dark colours avoids an infinite slope at zero. The constants 116 and −16 put white at \(L^* = 100\) and black at 0.
  5. Opponent differences. \(a^* \propto f(X)-f(Y)\) is positive when the colour is redder than its luminance suggests and negative when greener; \(b^* \propto f(Y)-f(Z)\) is positive for yellowish, negative for bluish colours. The Euclidean distance \(\Delta E^*_{ab} = \sqrt{\Delta L^{*2}+\Delta a^{*2}+\Delta b^{*2}}\) is a standard measure of colour difference.
Assumptions and validity

The conversion assumes that the camera output really is sRGB under a D65-like white balance. Consumer cameras apply their own colour processing, so CIELAB values from a phone are only approximately calibrated; including a colour checker card and correcting towards its known values makes them comparable between days. Relative comparisons within one well-controlled image series are much more robust than absolute values.

All inputs are normalised (0–1), so \(L^*\), \(a^*\) and \(b^*\) are dimensionless numbers on conventional scales ✓.

Segmentation: vegetation indices and Otsu's threshold

Segmentation means classifying every pixel as plant or background. The result is a binary mask, from which everything else follows: projected leaf area is the number of plant pixels, canopy cover is their fraction, colour statistics are computed inside the mask. The simplest and still the most widely used approach combines a colour index, which makes plant pixels numerically different from background pixels, with a threshold. Woebbecke et al. (1995) compared colour indices for separating weeds from soil and crop residue under different lighting and found the excess-green index to perform best among those they tested; Meyer and Neto (2008) later proposed subtracting an excess-red index to cope with brown and reddish backgrounds, and Hamuda et al. (2016) review the many indices and segmentation methods that followed.

Excess green, excess red and ExG − ExR(10.3.4)
\[ \text{ExG} = 2g-r-b,\qquad \text{ExR} = 1.4\,r-g,\qquad \text{ExG}-\text{ExR} = 3g-2.4\,r-b \]

excess green measures how much the green share of a pixel exceeds the average of its red and blue shares; excess red measures how much the red share exceeds the green share (soil, dry residue, red leaves); their difference combines both, and a pixel is classified as plant when ExG − ExR is greater than zero.

ExRexcess-red index (as used by Meyer & Neto, 2008)–/ −0.13 / +0.25
ExG − ExRcombined index with a fixed threshold of zero–/ +0.72 / −0.27
Follow the derivation — and see why these are linear classifiers
  1. Use \(r+g+b = 1\) to eliminate \(r+b = 1-g\): \(\text{ExG} = 2g-(1-g) = 3g-1\). Excess green is simply a rescaled green chromatic coordinate.
  2. Therefore \(\text{ExG} > 0 \iff g > 1/3\): a pixel counts as "green" when green carries more than a third of its signal — more than a neutral grey does. ExG is 2 for pure green (\(g = 1\)), 0 for any grey, and −1 for pure red or pure blue.
  3. Similarly \(\text{ExG}-\text{ExR} = 3g - 2.4r - (1-r-g) = 4g - 1.4r - 1\). The rule "\(\text{ExG}-\text{ExR} > 0\)" is the straight line \(g > 0.25 + 0.35\,r\) in the \((r, g)\) chromaticity plane: the redder the pixel, the more green it needs to count as plant.
  4. Both rules are straight lines separating two classes in a two-dimensional feature space — exactly the decision boundary of a single perceptron (Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.5), but with weights chosen by experts instead of learned from data (Figure 3).
Assumptions and validity

The indices assume green plants and non-green backgrounds under roughly white light. Red-leaved cultivars (for example 'Lollo Rosso' or red oakleaf lettuce) have low \(g\) and are missed; green algae on rockwool or peat, green plastic and a green shirt are included; yellowish rockwool can reach \(g\) slightly above 1/3. Meyer and Neto (2008) reported that ExG − ExR with its built-in zero threshold achieved a segmentation quality factor of 0.88 ± 0.12 on greenhouse images, compared with 0.53 ± 0.39 for ExG with an Otsu threshold, and was particularly superior on fresh wheat-straw backgrounds.

All terms are chromatic coordinates (dimensionless) with dimensionless weights ✓.

Many indices do not come with a natural threshold: ExG is continuous, and the best cut depends on the background, the light and the camera. In 1979 Nobuyuki Otsu published a method that picks the threshold automatically from the image's own histogram and that is still built into every image-processing library (Otsu, 1979). Its idea is to choose the threshold that makes the two resulting classes as internally uniform — or, equivalently, as mutually different — as possible.

Otsu's threshold: maximum between-class variance(10.3.5)
\[ \sigma_B^{2}(t) = \omega_0(t)\,\omega_1(t)\,\bigl[\mu_0(t)-\mu_1(t)\bigr]^{2},\qquad t^{*} = \arg\max_{t}\ \sigma_B^{2}(t) \] \[ \omega_0(t) = \sum_{i\le t} p_i,\quad \omega_1 = 1-\omega_0,\quad \mu_0(t) = \frac{\sum_{i\le t} i\,p_i}{\omega_0},\quad \mu_1(t) = \frac{\sum_{i> t} i\,p_i}{\omega_1} \]

for every candidate threshold \(t\), split the pixels into a low class (index level up to \(t\)) and a high class. Compute the fraction of pixels in each class and their mean index levels. The between-class variance is the product of the two class fractions and the squared difference of the class means; it is largest when both classes are sizeable and their means are far apart. Otsu's threshold is the level that maximises it.

Follow the derivation
  1. The total variance of the histogram, \(\sigma_T^2 = \sum_i (i-\mu_T)^2 p_i\) with \(\mu_T = \sum_i i\,p_i\), does not depend on \(t\).
  2. Split the sum at \(t\) and add and subtract each class mean: \(\sum_{i\le t}(i-\mu_T)^2p_i = \sum_{i\le t}(i-\mu_0)^2p_i + \omega_0(\mu_0-\mu_T)^2\) (the cross term vanishes because \(\sum_{i\le t}(i-\mu_0)p_i = 0\)). Doing the same for the high class gives \(\sigma_T^2 = \sigma_W^2 + \sigma_B^2\), with the within-class variance \(\sigma_W^2 = \omega_0\sigma_0^2 + \omega_1\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_B^2 = \omega_0(\mu_0-\mu_T)^2 + \omega_1(\mu_1-\mu_T)^2\).
  3. Insert \(\mu_T = \omega_0\mu_0 + \omega_1\mu_1\) and \(\omega_1 = 1-\omega_0\): \(\mu_0-\mu_T = \omega_1(\mu_0-\mu_1)\) and \(\mu_1-\mu_T = \omega_0(\mu_1-\mu_0)\), so \(\sigma_B^2 = \omega_0\omega_1^2(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2 + \omega_1\omega_0^2(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2 = \omega_0\omega_1(\mu_0-\mu_1)^2\).
  4. Because \(\sigma_T^2\) is fixed, maximising \(\sigma_B^2\) is the same as minimising \(\sigma_W^2\): Otsu's threshold makes both classes as homogeneous as possible. This is k-means clustering with \(k = 2\) in one dimension (Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.3) — solved exactly by trying every threshold.
  5. Computation needs only cumulative sums: with \(\mu(t) = \sum_{i\le t} i\,p_i\), \(\sigma_B^2(t) = [\mu_T\,\omega_0(t)-\mu(t)]^2/\{\omega_0(t)[1-\omega_0(t)]\}\) — one pass through 256 bins.
Assumptions and validity

Otsu works best for a bimodal histogram with classes of comparable size. When plants cover only a few per cent of the image (seedlings), the \(\omega_0\omega_1\) factor favours a threshold that splits the large background mode instead, and the result is poor. Strongly uneven illumination smears the modes; then local (adaptive) thresholds, computed in tiles, or a better index are needed. Otsu minimises a variance criterion — it does not know what a plant is, so check it against a few manually traced masks.

Common misconception

“Otsu finds the true edge of the plant.” Otsu finds the threshold that best splits the histogram into two compact groups. If the index is poor — ExG on a red cultivar, any index under magenta light, hue on a pale background — Otsu will faithfully split the wrong thing. Always validate a segmentation pipeline against a handful of manually traced masks and report the agreement (IoU), exactly as Figure 4 does.

Cleaning the mask: morphology and connected components

A thresholded mask is never perfect. Isolated background pixels that happen to be green (algae, a noisy pixel) appear as specks; specular highlights on wet leaves appear as holes; thin gaps open between leaves; neighbouring plants touch. Mathematical morphology, developed by Georges Matheron and Jean Serra in the 1960s for the analysis of mineral grains (Serra, 1982), treats the mask as a set of pixels and probes it with a small shape, the structuring element \(B\) — typically a small disc or square (Szeliski, 2022, ch. 3).

Erosion, dilation, opening and closing(10.3.6)
\[ A\ominus B = \{\,z \mid B_z\subseteq A\,\},\qquad A\oplus B = \{\,z \mid \hat B_z\cap A\neq\varnothing\,\},\qquad A\circ B = (A\ominus B)\oplus B,\qquad A\bullet B = (A\oplus B)\ominus B \]

erosion keeps a pixel \(z\) only if the structuring element, placed with its centre at \(z\), fits entirely inside the object \(A\); dilation marks a pixel if the (reflected) element placed there touches the object anywhere. Opening — erosion followed by dilation — removes objects and protrusions smaller than the element and restores the size of everything else; closing — dilation followed by erosion — fills holes and gaps smaller than the element.

Follow the derivation: why opening is a size filter
  1. Take a disc-shaped object of radius \(R\) and a disc element of radius \(\rho\). The element fits inside the object wherever its centre is at least \(\rho\) from the edge, so erosion leaves a disc of radius \(R-\rho\) (area \(\pi(R-\rho)^2\)) — or nothing if \(R<\rho\).
  2. Dilating that disc by \(\rho\) adds \(\rho\) to its radius again: the opened object has radius \(R\), exactly the original. Objects smaller than the element vanished in the first step and cannot come back.
  3. Hence opening removes specks narrower than about \(2\rho+1\) pixels while leaving large, smooth objects unchanged — a size filter. By the same argument with the background, closing fills holes and gaps narrower than the element.
  4. Opening and closing are idempotent: applying the same opening twice changes nothing more, which makes pipelines predictable.
Assumptions and validity

The element size is in pixels, so its physical size depends on the image scale: a 2-pixel radius removes 0.4 mm specks at 0.2 mm per pixel but 4 mm at 2 mm per pixel. Opening also erodes thin real structures — petioles, grass blades, the thin leaves of seedlings — and closing can weld touching plants into one object. Choose the smallest element that does the job and check the effect on the area.

After cleaning, the mask still contains several objects: two or three plants, perhaps a leaf of a neighbour, a patch of algae. Connected-component labelling gives every separate object its own number. Two plant pixels belong to the same component if a path of plant pixels joins them, where the path may step to the four edge-neighbours (4-connectivity) or also to the four diagonal neighbours (8-connectivity). The classic two-pass algorithm of Rosenfeld and Pfaltz (1966) scans the image once to assign provisional labels and record which labels touch, then once more to merge them; a flood fill from each unlabelled plant pixel does the same job, and is what Figure 4 uses. Once objects are labelled, each can be measured separately and filtered — for example by discarding everything smaller than a seedling.

From pixels to square centimetres — and to grams

A count of plant pixels becomes a projected leaf area — the area of the plant's shadow if the sun were directly overhead — once you know how much object area one pixel represents. That depends on the camera and on the distance, and a simple pinhole model captures it.

Projected area is a proxy. What a grower sells — and what the growth models of Lesson 10.1 describe — is mass. The link between the two is found by calibration: photograph a set of plants spanning the whole size range, harvest and weigh them, and fit a model. In Worked example 1 of Lesson 10.1 you fitted a straight line; it had a negative intercept, which is physically impossible for small plants. A power law is usually the better choice.

Allometric (power-law) calibration of biomass(10.3.8)
\[ W = a\,A^{\,b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ln W = \ln a + b\,\ln A,\qquad \text{RGR}_W = b\ \text{RGR}_A \]

Convolutional neural networks: features learned from data

A colour index and a threshold are enough to find a green plant on brown peat. They are not enough to tell downy mildew from tipburn, a nitrogen-deficient leaf from a naturally pale cultivar, or a ripe strawberry from a red leaf — problems that depend on texture, shape and context. You could feed the raw pixels into the neural networks of Lesson 10.2, but a fully connected layer does not scale: a modest 224 × 224 colour image has 150 528 inputs, and a single dense layer of 1000 neurons would need 150 million weights, most of which would learn nothing useful. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) exploit two properties of images (LeCun et al., 1998). Locality: the pixels that reveal a lesion edge are neighbours. Translation equivariance: a lesion is a lesion wherever it appears. A convolution layer therefore slides small filters over the whole image and computes the same weighted sum at every position.

Convolution layer: feature map, output size and parameter count(10.3.9)
\[ z^{(k)}_{i,j} = b^{(k)} + \sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{m=0}^{K-1}\sum_{n=0}^{K-1} w^{(k)}_{m,n,c}\;x_{\,iS+m,\;jS+n,\;c},\qquad N_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor\frac{N_{\text{in}}-K+2P}{S}\right\rfloor+1,\qquad n_{\text{par}} = (K^{2}C+1)\,F \]

each value of feature map \(k\) is a bias plus the sum, over all input channels and over a \(K\times K\) window, of input values times the filter's weights; the window moves in steps of \(S\) pixels over the input, which has been padded with \(P\) rows and columns of zeros. The output has \(\lfloor(N_{\text{in}}-K+2P)/S\rfloor+1\) positions along each axis. A layer with \(F\) filters has \(K^2C+1\) parameters per filter — independent of the image size.

Follow the derivation — from a neuron to a convolution
  1. Start from one neuron (Eq. 10.2.5): \(z = \sum_j w_jx_j + b\), connected to every input pixel.
  2. Locality. Connect each neuron only to a \(K\times K\times C\) window of the input. Weights per neuron drop from 150 528 to \(9\times3 = 27\) for a 3 × 3 window on an RGB image.
  3. Weight sharing. Use the same weights for the neurons at every position. The layer now computes one number per position with a single small filter: that is a (discrete) convolution — strictly a cross-correlation, but because the weights are learned, flipping the kernel changes nothing.
  4. Output size. Along one axis the padded input has \(N_{\text{in}}+2P\) positions; the window's first position is 0 and its last admissible position is \(N_{\text{in}}+2P-K\); stepping by \(S\) gives \(\lfloor(N_{\text{in}}+2P-K)/S\rfloor+1\) positions.
  5. Parameters. Each filter has \(K\cdot K\cdot C\) weights and one bias; \(F\) filters give \((K^2C+1)F\).
  6. Pooling and depth. A 2 × 2 max-pooling layer (stride 2) keeps the largest value in each block, halving width and height and making the representation tolerant to small shifts. Stacking layers enlarges the receptive field: after \(L\) layers of 3 × 3 convolutions with stride 1, each output depends on a \((2L+1)\times(2L+1)\) patch of the input; every pooling step roughly doubles the growth. Early layers therefore respond to edges and colour blobs, deeper layers to textures, lesion shapes and whole leaves.
Assumptions and validity

Convolution assumes that the same pattern means the same thing everywhere in the image — true for leaves and fruits, less true for images where absolute position matters. Standard CNNs expect a fixed input size, so images are resized or cropped, which changes the pixel scale (a problem if size is informative). Invariance to rotation, brightness and colour is not built in; it is learned from data, usually with data augmentation (random flips, rotations, crops and colour changes during training).

Transfer learning: standing on ImageNet's shoulders

Training a deep CNN from scratch needs very many labelled images. In 2009 Deng and colleagues assembled ImageNet, and its annual challenge (ILSVRC) used 1.28 million training images in 1000 object classes (Deng et al., 2009; Russakovsky et al., 2015). In 2012 a CNN now known as AlexNet — about 60 million parameters, trained on two graphics cards — won the challenge with a top-5 error of 15.3 %, against 26.2 % for the next-best entry (Krizhevsky et al., 2017). Residual networks (ResNets) later made networks with more than 150 layers trainable (He et al., 2016). Yosinski et al. (2014) showed why these networks are useful far beyond ImageNet: the features of the first layers are general (edges, colours, textures) and become specific only in the last layers. Transfer learning exploits this: take a network pre-trained on ImageNet, keep its convolutional base, replace the final classification layer by one for your classes, and train on a few hundred labelled images per class; optionally "unfreeze" the last blocks and fine-tune them with a small learning rate. In the PlantVillage study, networks initialised with ImageNet weights performed better than the same architectures trained from scratch (Mohanty et al., 2016).

Object detection: finding and counting fruit

Classification answers “what is in this image?”. Object detection answers “where, and how many?”: each object gets a bounding box, a class and a confidence score. Semantic segmentation labels every pixel with a class, and instance segmentation separates the individual objects of a class — the pixel-level version of detection. Two families of detectors dominate. Two-stage detectors such as Faster R-CNN first propose candidate regions and then classify each; one-stage detectors such as YOLO (“You Only Look Once”) predict boxes and classes for the whole image in a single pass of one network (Redmon et al., 2016). YOLO divides the image into a grid; every grid cell predicts a few boxes (centre, width, height), a confidence that a box contains an object, and class probabilities. The original model processed 45 images per second, fast enough for video from a moving vehicle or a harvesting robot.

Intersection over union and detection metrics(10.3.10)
\[ \text{IoU} = \frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|} = \frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p|+|B_g|-|B_p\cap B_g|},\qquad \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad \text{AP} = \int_0^1 p(r)\,\mathrm dr \]

the intersection over union of a predicted box and a ground-truth box is the area they share divided by the area they cover together. A detection counts as a true positive if its IoU with an unmatched ground-truth object is at least 0.5; otherwise it is a false positive, and every object without a matching detection is a false negative. Average precision is the area under the precision–recall curve obtained by sweeping the confidence threshold; mAP averages it over classes.

  2. Intersection of two axis-aligned boxes \((x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)\): overlap width \(w = \max(0,\min(x_2,x_2')-\max(x_1,x_1'))\), height likewise; \(|A\cap B| = wh\).
  3. Example. Two 10 × 10 boxes shifted by 5 pixels horizontally overlap in a 5 × 10 strip: IoU = 50/(100 + 100 − 50) = 1/3. A small shift already costs a lot: IoU is a demanding measure of localisation.
  4. Non-maximum suppression (NMS). A detector typically fires several overlapping boxes on the same fruit. Sort the boxes by confidence; keep the best; delete all remaining boxes whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold (often 0.45–0.5); repeat with the next surviving box. Too low a threshold merges neighbouring fruits into one; too high a threshold double-counts.
  5. AP. Lowering the confidence threshold adds detections: recall rises while precision usually falls. The precision–recall curve summarises all thresholds; its area is AP (the PASCAL VOC challenge defined the 0.5 IoU criterion; Everingham et al., 2010).
Assumptions and validity

Metrics are only as good as the ground-truth annotation: annotators disagree on heavily occluded fruit and on box edges. A count from images is not a count of fruit on the plant — fruit hidden behind leaves are missed and fruit seen from two sides or in two video frames are double-counted unless they are tracked. Reported AP values are comparable only for the same dataset, IoU criterion and annotation rules.

Disease recognition: impressive accuracy, fragile generalisation

In 2015 Hughes and Salathé released more than 50 000 expertly curated images of healthy and diseased crop leaves through the PlantVillage platform, with the explicit aim of enabling smartphone diagnosis for farmers (Hughes & Salathé, 2015). The following year Mohanty et al. (2016) trained CNNs on 54 306 of these images — 14 crop species, 26 diseases, 38 classes in total — and reported an accuracy of 99.35 % on a held-out test set. Ferentinos (2018) reached 99.53 % on 87 848 images of 25 plant species in 58 classes. These numbers are correct; what they mean is the question.

The PlantVillage images show single, detached leaves photographed in a laboratory on uniform backgrounds under similar lighting, often several images of the same leaf. The held-out test images came from the same collection. When Mohanty et al. tested their best models on images of the same diseases collected from other sources, accuracy fell to 31.4 % and 31.7 % — far above chance for 38 classes, but a different world from 99 %. Their own discussion notes that the model was constrained to single leaves, facing up, on a homogeneous background. Noyan (2022) then showed how much “shortcut” information the dataset contains: a model given only 8 background pixels of each image — no leaf at all — reached 49.0 % accuracy, against 2.6 % for random guessing. The backgrounds and imaging sessions correlate with the labels, and deep networks happily exploit such correlations; Geirhos et al. (2020) call this shortcut learning.

Independent evidence points the same way. Singh et al. (2020) built PlantDoc, a dataset of 2 598 internet images of 13 species and up to 17 disease classes in realistic conditions, and showed that training with such images increased classification accuracy on field-like images by up to 31 %. Ramcharan et al. (2019) deployed a cassava disease detector in a smartphone app and tested it on 720 diseased leaflets in fields in Tanzania: for pronounced symptoms the F1 score dropped by 32 % compared with the laboratory test, mainly because recall fell. Barbedo (2018) analysed the factors behind such gaps — limited and unrepresentative training data, symptom variation with stage and cultivar, capture conditions, similar-looking disorders and several disorders at once — and made a database of almost 50 000 images available for more realistic testing. The machine-learning concepts behind all of this are the same as in Lesson 10.2: group leakage (images of the same leaf in training and test), distribution shift and the need to test on data collected where the model will be used.

Common misconception

“The app was 99 % accurate in the paper, so it will diagnose my lettuce.” The 99 % describes performance on images like the training images. Your lettuce, your LED light, your background and your camera may be far outside that domain. Use such tools for screening, keep a person in the loop for decisions, and — if you depend on them — collect and label a few hundred images from your own system to test (and fine-tune) the model.

Beyond RGB: phenotyping sensors and platforms

Measuring plant traits — the phenotype — has long been the bottleneck of plant science and breeding: genomes can be sequenced cheaply, but measuring the growth, architecture and stress responses of thousands of plants by hand cannot keep up (Furbank & Tester, 2011; Fiorani & Schurr, 2013). High-throughput phenotyping automates it with imaging sensors that each capture a different part of the plant's interaction with light and heat (Li et al., 2014; Mahlein, 2016).

Phenotyping platforms move either the plants or the sensors. In “plant-to-sensor” greenhouse systems, potted plants travel on conveyors into imaging cabinets with controlled lighting, where they are photographed from the top and several sides and weighed and watered automatically; Golzarian et al. (2011) used such images of 320 wheat plants to show that shoot biomass is predicted far better from projected area plus plant age than from area alone. In “sensor-to-plant” systems the sensors move: the Field Scanalyzer at Rothamsted is a gantry on rails over a field that carries high-resolution visible, chlorophyll-fluorescence and thermal cameras, two hyperspectral imagers and two 3D laser scanners (Virlet et al., 2017); drones and ground robots bring similar sensors to farms (Module 11). At the other end of the scale, smartphone apps and open software put phenotyping into every student's pocket. Canopeo classifies pixels with the colour ratios R/G and B/G and the excess-green index, runs on iOS and Android, and processed images 20–130 times faster than a widely used commercial program while classifying 90 % of pixels the same way as a manual point-sampling method (Patrignani & Ochsner, 2015). Easy Leaf Area uses colour ratios and a red calibration square of known area, so that no distance or ruler measurement is needed; its estimates from camera-phone images were more accurate than ImageJ measurements from a flatbed scanner (Easlon & Bloom, 2014). ImageJ/Fiji (Schneider et al., 2012) and the Python library PlantCV (Gehan et al., 2017) provide the building blocks of this lesson — thresholds, morphology, labelling — as free tools.

Controlled-environment agriculture is adopting these methods quickly. In the third Autonomous Greenhouse Challenge (lettuce, 2021–2022; see Lesson 10.4), each compartment had depth cameras above the crop, and computer-vision models estimated plant height with \(R^2 = 0.976\) and plant coverage with a mean intersection over union of 98.2 %; these traits fed a light-loss indicator for deciding when to space the plants and a harvest indicator (Petropoulou et al., 2023). Zhang et al. (2020) trained a CNN on digital images of greenhouse lettuce to estimate leaf fresh weight, dry weight and leaf area directly (\(R^2\) = 0.89–0.92), and the model kept working on images from another growing season (\(R^2\) = 0.91–0.93) — an important test of generalisation.

Frontier 2024–2026

Three directions are changing plant phenotyping. Vision foundation models such as Segment Anything (Kirillov et al., 2023; Lesson 10.2) segment leaves, fruits and weeds with few or no task-specific labels, shifting effort from annotation to validation. Sensor fusion combines 3D geometry with spectral data so that reflectance can be corrected for leaf angle and position (Paulus, 2019). And image-driven decisions in production — spacing, harvest timing, disease alerts — close the loop between phenotyping and control (Lessons 10.4 and 10.5). The lesson of this section still applies: every new model must be validated on images from the conditions in which it will be used.

Your turn: a protocol for photographing your plants

In the course project you will follow the growth of your plants non-destructively from top-view photographs (monitoring protocol), convert them to projected area and — with a few destructive harvests — to fresh mass, and fit growth curves (Lesson 10.1). Measurement quality is decided at the moment you press the shutter. The following protocol turns a phone into a measuring instrument.

  1. Fix the geometry. Mount the camera on a stand or tripod so that the lens looks straight down (nadir) from the same height for the whole experiment — at least 0.8–1.5 m if possible, to keep the perspective error small (Eq. 10.3.7). Keep the plant near the centre of the frame and do not use the ultra-wide lens or digital zoom.
  2. Put a scale in the plane of the leaves. Place a flat reference card of known size (for example a 5 × 5 cm blue square printed or cut from card) on a small riser at canopy height, and raise it as the plants grow. Measure its side on screen at the beginning of each session.
  3. Control the light. Photograph under diffuse white light — daylight in the shade, or two white lamps from opposite sides. Never under red–blue LED light (switch the grow lights off or to white for the photo) and avoid direct sun, which creates hard shadows and specular highlights.
  4. Fix the camera settings. Use a “pro” or manual mode: fixed exposure (or at least exposure lock), fixed white balance (daylight), no HDR or beauty filters. Include an 18 % grey card in the frame so that colour can be checked and corrected later.
  5. Use a matte, non-green background. A dark blue or grey board under and around the pot makes segmentation easy; cover green algae on the substrate or crop them out.
  6. Label and time-stamp. Put a small label with the plant ID and day after transplanting in the frame, photograph at the same time of day (leaf angles change over the day), and name files consistently (e.g., 2026-10-14_B3_d14.jpg).
  7. Replicate. Take three photos per plant per session, lifting and replacing the plant between shots; the spread of the three areas is your measurement repeatability (coefficient of variation — aim for below 3 %).
  8. Segment and measure. Load each photo into Figure 4 (or the plant vision lab), choose ExG − ExR or −a*, check the outline, measure the reference card and record the area of each object. Check a few masks by eye in the “Mask” view.
  9. Calibrate. At two or three dates, photograph and then harvest and weigh a few extra plants spanning the size range; fit Eq. 10.3.8 in Figure 7. Keep the calibration data in your grow log.
  10. Model. Convert areas to mass, fit growth curves and forecast the harvest (Lesson 10.1, Figure 9 and the model-fitting lab).

Check your understanding

Key terms

Pixel
The smallest element of a digital image, holding one digital number per colour channel.
Chromatic coordinates
Channel values divided by their sum, \(r, g, b\); invariant to uniform changes of illumination intensity.
HSV
Hue–saturation–value colour space; hue is an angle on the colour wheel (120° = green).
CIELAB
Perceptually uniform colour space with lightness \(L^*\) and opponent axes \(a^*\) (green–red) and \(b^*\) (blue–yellow).
Excess green (ExG)
Colour index \(2g-r-b = 3g-1\), positive for pixels greener than grey; ExG − ExR adds a correction for reddish backgrounds.
Segmentation
Classification of every pixel as object or background, producing a binary mask (or a label per pixel).
Otsu's method
Automatic threshold that maximises the between-class variance (minimises the within-class variance) of a histogram.
Mathematical morphology
Erosion, dilation, opening and closing of a mask with a structuring element, used to remove specks and fill holes.
Connected component
A set of foreground pixels joined by paths of foreground pixels (4- or 8-connectivity); one component per object.
Ground sampling distance
The object-side size of one pixel, \(s = pD/f\), usually measured with a reference of known length.
Projected leaf area
Area of the plant's top-view silhouette; a non-destructive proxy for leaf area and biomass.
Convolutional neural network
A neural network whose layers slide small shared filters over the image, producing feature maps of increasing abstraction.
Transfer learning
Reusing a network pre-trained on a large dataset and retraining (or fine-tuning) only its last layers for a new task.
Intersection over union (IoU)
Overlap area of a predicted and a true region divided by the area of their union; the standard localisation criterion.
Domain shift
Difference between the data a model was trained on and the data it meets in use, causing performance to drop.

Check your reasoning

  • I can convert an RGB pixel into chromatic coordinates, HSV hue and CIELAB \(a^*\), and explain what each representation is good for.
  • I can compute ExG and ExG − ExR, explain why they are straight-line classifiers, and name situations where they fail.
  • I can derive Otsu's criterion and find the threshold of a small histogram by hand.
  • I can convert a pixel count into square centimetres with a reference card, correct for perspective, and calibrate area against mass with a power law.
  • I can calculate feature-map sizes and parameter counts of convolution layers and explain transfer learning.
  • I can judge whether a reported detection or disease-recognition accuracy is likely to hold in my own greenhouse.
