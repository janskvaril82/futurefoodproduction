Calibration, uncertainty and data quality

Your logger says that the nutrient solution is at 21.37 °C, that yesterday's daily light integral was 14.4 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ and that the vapour pressure deficit is 0.90 kPa. How sure are you — to the nearest degree, tenth, or hundredth? A number without an uncertainty cannot be compared with another number, cannot be used to decide whether a treatment worked, and cannot be trusted by a controller. This lesson teaches you the internationally agreed way to put an honest ± on every value, to calibrate a sensor with least squares, to keep a sensor honest over months, and to turn a raw, noisy stream of samples into data that others can use.

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish accuracy, trueness, precision and resolution, and classify errors as systematic, random or gross (LO4, LO5).
  • Evaluate standard uncertainties by Type A (statistical) and Type B methods, combine them with the law of propagation of uncertainty — which you can derive from a first-order Taylor expansion — and report an expanded uncertainty with k = 2 (LO3, LO4).
  • Estimate the uncertainty of derived quantities in your project — the DLI from a PAR sensor and the VPD from temperature and humidity sensors — and identify the dominant contributions (LO3, LO5).
  • Build a calibration curve by least-squares regression, judge it by its residuals, and convert a new reading into a calibrated value with its uncertainty (inverse prediction) (LO4, LO5).
  • Choose sampling intervals with the Nyquist criterion, recognise aliasing, and apply moving-average, exponential, median and one-dimensional Kalman filters (LO5).
  • Plan recalibration intervals, apply quality-control flags and record the metadata that make your project's sensor data reusable (LO4, LO5).

Why every number needs an uncertainty

Imagine two project groups that share a greenhouse compartment. One group's logger reports a nutrient-solution temperature of 21.4 °C, the other's 20.8 °C. Which is right? Possibly both: if the first reading is uncertain by ±0.5 °C and the second by ±0.4 °C, the two values are consistent with each other. Possibly neither. Without an uncertainty the question cannot even be asked. This is why the international Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement — universally called the GUM — treats a measurement result as complete only when it is accompanied by a quantitative statement of its uncertainty (JCGM, 2008a).

In Lesson 9.1 you followed a measurement through the chain from measurand to stored value and saw that every stage adds error; in Lesson 9.2 you calibrated pH electrodes and compensated conductivity for temperature. This lesson turns those insights into numbers. It gives you three tools that you will use in the course project and long after it: a vocabulary (accuracy, trueness, precision, resolution); a method — the GUM framework and calibration by regression — for attaching a defensible ± to any value, including derived quantities such as the daily light integral and the vapour pressure deficit; and a toolbox for time series — sampling, filtering, quality flags and metadata — that keeps a stream of numbers trustworthy long after installation.

The words matter, because everyday language uses them loosely. The definitions below follow the International Vocabulary of Metrology (JCGM, 2012), the dictionary used by every national metrology institute and accredited laboratory.

Measurand
The quantity intended to be measured, defined completely enough for the purpose: "temperature of the nutrient solution at 10 cm depth in tank A, averaged over 1 min", not just "temperature".
Measurement error
Measured value minus the true (reference) value. It is never known exactly — if it were, you would correct it.
Systematic error
The component of error that stays constant, or varies predictably, in replicate measurements: a calibration offset, a sensor warmed by the sun. Its estimate is the bias.
Random error
The component that varies unpredictably between replicate measurements: electrical noise, turbulence, quantisation. It averages out; bias does not.
Trueness
Closeness of the average of infinitely many replicate values to the reference value — the absence of systematic error.
Precision
Closeness of replicate values to each other — the absence of random error. Repeatability is precision under the same conditions (same sensor, operator, place, short time); reproducibility under changed conditions.
Accuracy
Closeness of a single measured value to the true value. An accurate measurement is both true and precise. Accuracy is a qualitative idea; uncertainty is its quantitative replacement.
Resolution
The smallest change in the measurand that causes a perceptible change in the indication — one ADC step or one displayed digit (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.4).
Measurement uncertainty
A non-negative parameter that characterises the dispersion of the values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurand, usually expressed as a standard deviation (the standard uncertainty \(u\)) or as a multiple of it (the expanded uncertainty \(U = ku\)).
Key idea: error versus uncertainty

An error is a single, unknowable difference between your value and the truth. An uncertainty is a quantified range that expresses your remaining doubt. You correct the errors you know about (subtract a measured bias, compensate for temperature), and you state the uncertainty that remains. A complete result therefore looks like "14.4 ± 1.0 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (k = 2)", never just "14.4".

Common misconception

"My readings are very stable, so my sensor must be accurate." Stability is precision, not trueness. A humidity sensor that has drifted 4 %RH high reads just as steadily as a new one. The ten stable readings in case (b) of Figure 1 carry exactly the same systematic error, and averaging them removes none of it.

Errors and repeated readings: Type A evaluation

A simple but powerful way to think about any reading \(x_i\) is the error model \(x_i = x_{\text{true}} + b + \varepsilon_i\): the true value, plus a bias \(b\) that is the same in every replicate, plus a random error \(\varepsilon_i\) that differs from reading to reading, with mean zero and standard deviation \(\sigma\). A third category, gross errors (blunders), does not fit the model at all: a probe lifted out of the tank, a loose connector, condensation on a humidity sensor, a number typed in the wrong column. Gross errors are not "uncertainty" — they are mistakes to be detected and removed, and the quality-control tests of the last section of this lesson are designed to catch them.

If the bias is constant, the random part can be characterised statistically from repeated readings under repeatability conditions. The GUM calls any evaluation of uncertainty by statistical analysis of a series of observations a Type A evaluation.

Type A evaluation: mean, experimental standard deviation and standard uncertainty of the mean(9.3.1)
\[ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i - \bar{x}\right)^{2}}, \qquad u(\bar{x}) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \nu = n - 1 \]

the best estimate is the arithmetic mean of the n readings; the experimental standard deviation s measures how much single readings scatter; the standard uncertainty of the mean is s divided by the square root of n, and it is based on n − 1 degrees of freedom.

Follow the derivation
  1. Model. Each reading is \(x_i = \mu + \varepsilon_i\), with \(\mu = x_{\text{true}} + b\), \(\mathrm{E}[\varepsilon_i] = 0\), \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\), and the \(\varepsilon_i\) mutually independent.
  2. The mean is unbiased for μ. \(\mathrm{E}[\bar{x}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum \mathrm{E}[x_i] = \mu\). Note: unbiased for \(\mu\), which contains \(b\) — averaging can never reveal or remove the bias.
  3. Variance of the mean. For independent readings, variances add: \(\mathrm{Var}(\bar{x}) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(x_i) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\). Taking the square root: the standard deviation of the mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\).
  4. Why divide by n − 1? Write \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum (x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{x} - \mu)^2\) (expand and use \(\sum (x_i - \mu) = n(\bar{x}-\mu)\)). Its expectation is \(n\sigma^2 - n\cdot\sigma^2/n = (n-1)\sigma^2\). Dividing the sum by \(n-1\) therefore gives an unbiased estimate \(s^2\) of \(\sigma^2\). One degree of freedom was "used up" by estimating the mean.
  5. Result. Replacing \(\sigma\) by its estimate \(s\) gives \(u(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n}\). Quadrupling the number of readings halves the uncertainty of the mean — the \(\sqrt{n}\) law.
Assumptions and validity — beware of correlated samples

The \(\sqrt{n}\) law requires independent readings. Sixty readings taken one second apart from a slowly varying water temperature are not sixty independent observations: consecutive values share the same slow fluctuations, and \(s/\sqrt{60}\) grossly understates the uncertainty. For a first-order autoregressive process with lag-1 correlation \(\rho\), the effective number of independent readings is roughly \(n_{\text{eff}} \approx n(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)\) — with \(\rho = 0.9\), sixty readings are worth about three. Also remember that \(u(\bar{x})\) describes only the random component; the bias needs a separate (usually Type B) evaluation. With few readings, \(s\) itself is uncertain: with \(n = 5\) its relative standard uncertainty is about 35 %.

\(s\): \(\sqrt{[x]^2} = [x]\); \(u(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n}\): [x] divided by a pure number = [x] — same unit as the readings ✓

How should you collect the readings for a Type A evaluation in practice? The answer depends on which question you want to answer. To characterise the repeatability of a probe, keep everything constant — the same bath, the same immersion depth, the same person — and space the readings far enough apart that each one contains fresh noise: for a DS18B20 in a stirred bath, one reading per minute for ten minutes is better than ten readings in ten seconds. To characterise reproducibility, deliberately vary the conditions that will vary in use: remove and re-insert the probe, let a classmate take the reading, repeat on another day. The spread under changed conditions is almost always larger, and it is the honest number for a measurement protocol that several people will follow during the project.

Worked example 1An ice-point check of a water-temperature probe

Before installing a DS18B20 probe in the nutrient tank, you place it in a stirred mixture of crushed ice and deionised water, which realises 0.00 °C to within about 0.01 °C when made carefully. After 5 minutes you log ten readings (°C, 12-bit resolution = 0.0625 °C): 0.1875, 0.2500, 0.1875, 0.2500, 0.3125, 0.2500, 0.1875, 0.2500, 0.2500, 0.3125. (a) Estimate the bias of the probe and its standard uncertainty. (b) Is the probe within its ±0.5 °C specification? (c) What should you do with the result?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Mean. The sum is \(3\times0.1875 + 5\times0.2500 + 2\times0.3125 = 2.4375\) °C, so \(\bar{x} = 2.4375/10 = 0.2438\) °C.
  2. Standard deviation. Deviations from the mean: −0.05625 (three times), +0.00625 (five times), +0.06875 (twice). Sum of squares: \(3(0.003164) + 5(0.0000391) + 2(0.004727) = 0.019141\) °C². Dividing by \(n - 1 = 9\): \(s^2 = 0.0021267\) °C², \(s = 0.0461\) °C.
  3. Type A standard uncertainty of the mean (Eq. 9.3.1): \(u(\bar{x}) = 0.0461/\sqrt{10} = 0.0146\) °C, with 9 degrees of freedom.
  4. Bias. \(b = \bar{x} - 0.00 = +0.244\) °C. Its uncertainty also includes the ice bath (≈ 0.01 °C, Type B) — combined (Eq. 9.3.3): \(\sqrt{0.0146^2 + 0.01^2} = 0.018\) °C.
  5. Specification. +0.24 °C lies well inside ±0.5 °C: the probe meets its data sheet.
  6. Action. Record the bias in your metadata and subtract 0.24 °C from future readings (a one-point offset correction). Check at a second temperature (e.g., against a reference thermometer at 20–25 °C) to see whether the offset is the same across the range — if not, you need a two-point or multi-point calibration (Worked example 4).
Bias ≈ +0.24 °C, u ≈ 0.018 °C; within specification; correct future readings by −0.24 °C

Sanity check: the scatter \(s = 0.046\) °C is comparable to the resolution-limited uncertainty \(0.0625/\sqrt{12} = 0.018\) °C and to the spread expected from a well-stirred bath — the probe is precise; its dominant error is systematic. That is typical of digital sensors, and it is exactly case (b) of Figure 1.

The GUM framework: Type B, propagation and expanded uncertainty

Most of the uncertainty in a real measurement cannot be evaluated from your own repeated readings: the uncertainty of the calibration performed by the manufacturer, the drift since then, the limited resolution, a specified accuracy of "±1.8 %RH". The GUM calls every evaluation that is not a statistical analysis of your own series of observations a Type B evaluation. It uses all available information — data sheets, calibration certificates, physics, experience — and it is just as legitimate as Type A. The GUM procedure is a recipe of six steps (Figure 3), and its heart is the law of propagation of uncertainty.

Type B evaluation: standard uncertainty from limits, resolution and certificates(9.3.2)
\[ u_{\text{rect}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}}, \qquad u_{\text{tri}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{6}}, \qquad u_{\text{res}} = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{12}}, \qquad u_{\text{cert}} = \frac{U}{k} \]

if all you know is that the error lies somewhere between −a and +a with no value preferred, the standard uncertainty is the half-width a divided by the square root of 3; if values near the centre are more likely (a triangular distribution), divide by the square root of 6; a resolution step δ gives δ divided by the square root of 12; and an expanded uncertainty U quoted on a certificate with coverage factor k gives U divided by k.

Follow the derivation
  1. Rectangular (uniform) distribution. If the error \(e\) is equally likely anywhere in \([-a, a]\), its probability density is \(1/(2a)\). Its variance is \(\sigma^2 = \displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a} e^2\,\frac{1}{2a}\,\mathrm{d}e = \frac{1}{2a}\left[\frac{e^3}{3}\right]_{-a}^{a} = \frac{1}{2a}\cdot\frac{2a^3}{3} = \frac{a^2}{3}\), so \(u = a/\sqrt{3} = 0.577\,a\).
  2. Triangular distribution. Density \((a - |e|)/a^2\) on \([-a, a]\): \(\sigma^2 = 2\displaystyle\int_0^a e^2\frac{a - e}{a^2}\mathrm{d}e = \frac{2}{a^2}\left(\frac{a^4}{3} - \frac{a^4}{4}\right) = \frac{a^2}{6}\), so \(u = a/\sqrt{6} = 0.408\,a\).
  3. Resolution. A reading displayed in steps of \(\delta\) hides the true value somewhere within \(\pm\delta/2\): a rectangular distribution with \(a = \delta/2\), hence \(u = (\delta/2)/\sqrt{3} = \delta/\sqrt{12}\) — the same result as the quantisation noise of an ADC in Lesson 9.1 (Eq. 9.1.4).
  4. Certificates. A calibration laboratory reports \(U = k\,u\) (usually \(k = 2\)); dividing by \(k\) recovers the standard uncertainty.
Assumptions and validity — which distribution should I choose?

When a data sheet says "accuracy ±a" without further information, the GUM recommends the rectangular distribution: it is the most conservative choice that uses only the stated limits. Choose triangular when values near the centre are clearly more likely (for example the difference of two rectangular errors). Manufacturers sometimes quote "typical" values (as Sensirion does for the SHT4x) that are statistical rather than guaranteed limits; the rectangular assumption is then a reasonable, slightly conservative engineering choice — state it in your report. Never add a Type B term twice: if you calibrated the sensor yourself, the manufacturer's accuracy specification is replaced by your calibration uncertainty.

Now we must combine several standard uncertainties into one. Most quantities you care about are not measured directly but calculated from others: the DLI from PPFD and time, the VPD from temperature and relative humidity, EC₂₅ from raw conductivity and temperature. The rule that carries uncertainty through a calculation is the heart of the GUM.

Law of propagation of uncertainty (first order)(9.3.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} u_c^{2}(y) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^{2}u^{2}(x_i) \;+\; 2\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{N}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\,u(x_i,x_j) \\[8pt] \text{for } y = c\prod_i x_i^{\,p_i}\ \text{(independent inputs)}:\qquad \left(\frac{u_c}{y}\right)^{2} = \sum_i\left(p_i\,\frac{u(x_i)}{x_i}\right)^{2} \end{gathered} \]

the square of the combined standard uncertainty of the result equals the sum, over all inputs, of the squared sensitivity coefficient times the squared standard uncertainty of that input, plus twice the sum of cross-products of sensitivities and covariances for inputs that are correlated. For a product of powers of independent inputs, the relative uncertainties add in quadrature, each multiplied by its exponent.

Follow the derivation from a Taylor expansion
  1. Linearise. Write each input as its best estimate plus a small error: \(x_i = \hat{x}_i + \delta_i\). A first-order Taylor expansion of the model around the estimates gives \(y = f(\hat{x}_1 + \delta_1,\dots) \approx f(\hat{x}_1,\dots,\hat{x}_N) + \sum_i c_i\,\delta_i\), with \(c_i = \partial f/\partial x_i\) evaluated at the estimates.
  2. The error of the result is a linear combination of input errors: \(\delta_y \approx \sum_i c_i\delta_i\).
  3. Variance of a linear combination. For any random variables, \(\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_i c_i\delta_i\right) = \sum_i c_i^2\,\mathrm{Var}(\delta_i) + 2\sum_{i<j}c_ic_j\,\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_i,\delta_j)\). This follows from expanding the square \(\left(\sum c_i\delta_i\right)^2\) and taking expectations term by term.
  4. Identify the terms. Variances are squared standard uncertainties, \(u^2(x_i)\); covariances are \(u(x_i,x_j)\). This is Eq. 9.3.3. Independent inputs have zero covariance and only the first sum remains — "adding in quadrature".
  5. Products and quotients. For \(y = c\,x_1^{p_1}x_2^{p_2}\cdots\), take logarithms: \(\ln y = \ln c + \sum p_i\ln x_i\). Differentiating, \(\delta y/y = \sum p_i\,\delta x_i/x_i\): relative errors combine linearly with weights \(p_i\), and for independent inputs the relative uncertainties add in quadrature.
  6. Two limits worth remembering. Independent (\(r = 0\)): \(u_c = \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2}\) for \(y = x_1 + x_2\). Fully correlated (\(r = 1\)): \(u_c = u_1 + u_2\) — the uncertainties add linearly. A calibration error shared by all readings of the same sensor is fully correlated, which is why it survives any amount of averaging.
Assumptions and validity — when to use Monte Carlo instead

The law is exact for linear models and a good approximation when the model is nearly linear over a range of about ±2u around the estimates. It can fail for strongly non-linear models (an exponential in a Nernst or Arrhenius term with large input uncertainty), when one non-normal input dominates, or near a physical limit (a relative humidity close to 100 %). The GUM supplement JCGM 101 (JCGM, 2008b) replaces the analytical propagation by a Monte Carlo simulation: draw thousands of input values from their distributions, compute the result for each, and take the standard deviation and the 2.5 % and 97.5 % quantiles of the results. Figure 4 does exactly that for the VPD.

Each term \(c_i^2u^2(x_i)\): \(([y]/[x_i])^2[x_i]^2 = [y]^2\) ✓; so \(u_c\) has the unit of y. The relative form is dimensionless ✓

The covariance term deserves a moment's thought, because correlation can make uncertainties either larger or smaller. Two inputs are correlated whenever they share an error source. If two tank-temperature probes were both calibrated against the same reference thermometer, the reference's error is inside both corrections; their average does not beat that shared error, but their difference is free of it. Temperature and humidity measured by the same chip may share a self-heating error. Most importantly for your experiment: when two treatments are measured with the same sensor, its calibration error is common to both and cancels in the difference — the reason why a paired design with one instrument can detect smaller treatment effects than a comparison of values from two differently calibrated instruments. In the other direction, ignoring a positive correlation when you add correlated quantities underestimates the uncertainty.

The combined standard uncertainty is a standard deviation. An interval of ±1 standard deviation contains the true value only about two times out of three, which is too weak a statement for most decisions. The result is therefore expanded by a coverage factor.

Expanded uncertainty and coverage probability(9.3.4)
\[ \begin{gathered} U = k\,u_c(y), \qquad Y = y \pm U, \qquad p(k) = \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\ \ \text{(normal distribution)} \\[8pt] \nu_{\text{eff}} = \frac{u_c^{4}(y)}{\sum_i c_i^{4}u^{4}(x_i)/\nu_i} \end{gathered} \]

the expanded uncertainty is the coverage factor k times the combined standard uncertainty; the result is reported as the estimate plus or minus U; for a normally distributed result the probability that the interval covers the true value is the error function of k divided by the square root of 2; and when some inputs rest on few degrees of freedom, the Welch–Satterthwaite formula gives the effective degrees of freedom used to choose k from the t-distribution.

Follow the derivation
  1. If the combined result is normally distributed with standard deviation \(u_c\), the probability of lying within \(\pm k u_c\) of the mean is \(\displaystyle\int_{-k}^{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}\mathrm{d}z = \operatorname{erf}(k/\sqrt{2})\).
  2. Evaluate: \(k = 1\) → 68.27 %; \(k = 1.96\) → 95.00 %; \(k = 2\) → 95.45 %; \(k = 3\) → 99.73 %.
  3. Why is the result approximately normal even if the inputs are rectangular? The central limit theorem: a sum of several independent contributions of comparable size tends to a normal distribution. If one rectangular term dominates, the true coverage of \(\pm 2u_c\) is actually 100 %, and a 95 % interval is narrower (about \(\pm 1.65u_c\)) — Monte Carlo shows this directly.
  4. When a dominant Type A input rests on few readings, replace \(k = 2\) by the Student-t value \(t_{0.975}(\nu_{\text{eff}})\) — for example 2.26 for \(\nu_{\text{eff}} = 9\).
How to report a result

Round the expanded uncertainty to one or two significant digits and round the value to the same decimal place: "DLI = 14.4 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ ± 1.0 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (k = 2, approximately 95 % coverage)". Never write "14.40318 ± 0.9552". State the coverage factor; NIST adopted k = 2 as its default for exactly this reason (Taylor & Kuyatt, 1994). In tables, a column header "DLI (mol m⁻² d⁻¹), U (k = 2)" saves space.

Worked example 2The uncertainty of a daily light integral from a ±5 % PAR sensor

A group logs PPFD under its LED fixture every minute with a full-spectrum quantum sensor whose calibration uncertainty is ±5 % and whose drift is below 2 % per year (Apogee Instruments, n.d.); the sensor was calibrated one year ago. The mean PPFD over the 16 h photoperiod is 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Single readings scatter by 0.5 % (repeatability), and you allow ±2 % for spectral mismatch under your LED spectrum. What is the DLI and its expanded uncertainty? Why do 960 readings not make the calibration uncertainty smaller?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Model. \(\text{DLI} = \dfrac{\Delta t}{10^6}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{PPFD}_i\) with \(\Delta t = 60\) s and \(N = 960\) readings. With a constant mean: \(\text{DLI} = 250\times16\times3600/10^6 = 14.40\) mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (Lesson 4.1, Eq. 4.1.6).
  2. Error model for each reading. \(\text{PPFD}_i = (1 + \gamma)\,\text{PPFD}_{\text{true},i} + \varepsilon_i\), where \(\gamma\) is the relative sensitivity error of this sensor (calibration + drift + spectral mismatch) — the same in every reading — and \(\varepsilon_i\) is independent noise.
  3. Correlated part. Because \(\gamma\) multiplies every term of the sum, \(\text{DLI}_{\text{measured}} = (1+\gamma)\,\text{DLI}_{\text{true}}\): the relative uncertainty of the DLI equals the relative uncertainty of \(\gamma\), however many readings you take (the fully correlated limit of Eq. 9.3.3).
  4. Type B terms (Eq. 9.3.2, rectangular): calibration \(5/\sqrt3 = 2.89\,\%\); drift \(2/\sqrt3 = 1.15\,\%\); spectral \(2/\sqrt3 = 1.15\,\%\). These are independent of each other, so they add in quadrature: \(\sqrt{2.89^2 + 1.15^2 + 1.15^2} = 3.32\,\%\).
  5. Random part. The noise terms are independent: \(u = \frac{\Delta t}{10^6}\sqrt{N}\,\sigma = \frac{60}{10^6}\sqrt{960}\times1.25 = 0.0023\) mol m⁻² d⁻¹ — only 0.016 % of the DLI. Negligible.
  6. Combine and expand. \(u_c = 0.0332\times14.40 = 0.478\) mol m⁻² d⁻¹; \(U = 2\times0.478 = 0.96\) mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (Eq. 9.3.4).
DLI = 14.4 ± 1.0 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (k = 2); the calibration term dominates

Lessons: (1) averaging kills noise but not a shared calibration error; (2) to compare two treatments measured with the same sensor, the common calibration error cancels in the difference — only the random and position-dependent terms remain. Comparing two groups' DLIs measured with different sensors, by contrast, carries both calibration uncertainties. Sanity check: an expanded uncertainty of about ±7 % is what the manufacturer's ±5 % becomes once drift and spectral effects are added; Ross and Sulev (2000) review these and further error sources of PAR measurements.

Calibration curves by linear regression

Calibration is "the operation that, under specified conditions, establishes a relation between the quantity values provided by measurement standards, with their measurement uncertainties, and the corresponding indications" (JCGM, 2012). In plain words: you expose your sensor to several known values of the measurand, record what it says, and establish the relation that converts future indications into measured values. Three terms are often confused. Calibration establishes the relation; adjustment changes the instrument so that it reads correctly (turning a trimmer, writing new coefficients into firmware); verification checks that the instrument meets a specification without changing it. The reference values must themselves be traceable (Figure 5).

A good calibration is designed, not improvised. Five rules cover most situations in the project. Span the range of use: calibrate a nutrient-tank probe from about 10 to 30 °C, not at room temperature only, because Eq. 9.3.6 shows that uncertainty grows quickly outside the calibrated range. Use enough points to test the model: two points determine a line but cannot reveal curvature; three buffers or five baths can. Wait for equilibrium: after each change, allow at least four to five time constants of the slowest element in the chain (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.2) — for a sheathed probe in a stirred bath, a couple of minutes. Approach points from both sides (rising and falling) if hysteresis is possible, as with polymer humidity sensors. Choose the model from physics: a straight line for a Pt100 or a pH electrode, Steinhart–Hart for a thermistor, the Topp polynomial or a substrate-specific curve for a dielectric moisture probe. Record everything — date, reference, raw indications, fitted coefficients — so that the calibration can be audited and repeated.

With several calibration points, the relation between reference value \(x\) and sensor indication \(y\) is found by least squares. You already met regression in Lesson 3.3; here we focus on what a calibration needs: the line, its scatter, and the uncertainty of a value read back through it.

Least-squares calibration line(9.3.5)
\[ \begin{gathered} \hat{y} = a + b\,x, \qquad b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}, \qquad s_{y/x} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{n-2}} \\[8pt] u(b) = \frac{s_{y/x}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \qquad u(a) = s_{y/x}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}} \end{gathered} \]

A coefficient of determination of 0.9999 says almost nothing — Anscombe (1973) constructed four data sets with identical R² whose residual plots are completely different. Drift and recalibration intervals

A calibration is a snapshot. Sensors age: the pH glass loses slope, the reference junction clogs, a polymer humidity film absorbs contaminants, a quantum sensor's diffuser yellows, an NDIR lamp dims. The change of the characteristic with time is called drift. Data sheets specify it as a maximum rate — for example "less than 2 % per year" for a quantum sensor (Apogee Instruments, n.d.) — and you should expect it to accumulate between calibrations (Figure 7).

Uncertainty growth by drift and the longest recalibration interval(9.3.7)
\[ u^{2}(t) = u_0^{2} + \frac{(r\,t)^{2}}{3} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t_{\max} = \frac{\sqrt{3\left[\left(U_{\max}/k\right)^{2} - u_0^{2}\right]}}{r} \]

the squared standard uncertainty a time t after calibration equals the squared uncertainty just after calibration plus the drift that could have accumulated, treated as a rectangular distribution of half-width r times t; setting k times this uncertainty equal to the largest acceptable expanded uncertainty gives the longest interval before recalibration.

Drift is rarely steady: electrodes degrade faster in dirty solutions, humidity sensors after condensation. How do you know whether a sensor is drifting before its next calibration? Three habits make drift visible. Keep a calibration history: record the "as found" error before every adjustment and the "as left" error after it — a slope that falls a little at every pH calibration is drift you can extrapolate. And where it matters, install redundant sensors: two independent probes that slowly diverge reveal drift that neither could reveal alone.

Sampling, noise and aliasing

A logger does not see a continuous signal; it sees samples taken every \(\Delta t\) seconds or minutes. Choosing \(\Delta t\) is a design decision with consequences: sample too slowly and you miss events or, worse, see events that did not happen; sample too fast and you fill memory, drain batteries and send redundant data over the network (Lesson 9.4). Between the samples, the signal also carries noise: thermal noise in resistors, quantisation (Eq. 9.1.4), radio-frequency interference and, very commonly, 50 Hz pick-up from mains wiring in Europe. The mathematics of sampling was worked out by Nyquist (1928) and Shannon (1949).

Sampling theorem (Nyquist criterion) and the alias frequency(9.3.8)
\[ f_s = \frac{1}{\Delta t} > 2\,f_{\max}, \qquad f_N = \frac{f_s}{2}, \qquad f_a = \left|\,f - f_s\cdot\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right)\right| \]

the sampling frequency, the reciprocal of the sampling interval, must exceed twice the highest frequency present in the signal; half the sampling frequency is called the Nyquist frequency; a sinusoid of frequency f sampled at fs appears at the alias frequency, the absolute difference between f and the nearest whole multiple of fs.

Follow the derivation
  1. Sample a sinusoid \(x(t) = \cos(2\pi f t)\) at times \(t_n = n\Delta t = n/f_s\): \(x_n = \cos(2\pi f n/f_s)\).
  2. Now consider a different frequency \(f' = f - k f_s\) for any integer \(k\): \(x'_n = \cos(2\pi (f - kf_s)n/f_s) = \cos(2\pi f n/f_s - 2\pi kn) = x_n\), because \(kn\) is an integer and the cosine has period \(2\pi\). The two sinusoids produce identical samples.
  3. Also \(\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta\), so \(f'\) and \(|f'|\) are indistinguishable. Among all the candidates \(|f - kf_s|\), the one with the smallest value (\(k = \operatorname{round}(f/f_s)\)) lies between 0 and \(f_s/2\) — this is the frequency your data will show: the alias.
  4. Only if every frequency in the signal is below \(f_s/2\) is the alias equal to the true frequency. Shannon (1949) proved the converse: a signal containing no frequencies at or above \(f_s/2\) is completely determined by its samples.
Assumptions and practice — anti-aliasing

Real signals are not band-limited, so the rule is applied with a safety factor: sample at 5–10 times the highest frequency you care about, and remove faster fluctuations before sampling. Three anti-aliasing filters are available to you: the analogue RC filter in front of the ADC (Lesson 9.1, Figure 4); the sensor's own time constant, which attenuates a fluctuation of angular frequency ω by \(1/\sqrt{1 + (\omega\tau)^2}\) (Eq. 9.1.2); and oversampling with block averaging — measure every second and store the 1-min or 10-min mean. Averaging over a whole number of mains periods (20 ms at 50 Hz) also cancels mains pick-up, which is why integrating and many sigma–delta ADCs offer 50 Hz rejection settings.

\(f_s = 1/\Delta t\): s⁻¹ = Hz ✓; \(f/f_s\) dimensionless, so \(f_a\) has the unit of \(f\) ✓

Notice two special cases in the calculator. When \(\Delta t\) equals the period (or a whole multiple of it), every sample catches the oscillation at the same phase: the alias frequency is zero, the logged signal looks perfectly constant, and the calculator shows a dash. When \(\Delta t\) is slightly longer or shorter than the period, the alias period becomes very long — a slow, entirely fictitious wave. Both are common in greenhouse data logged at round-number intervals.

Worked example 6A phantom hourly oscillation

A student notices that the air temperature in the group's growth chamber seems to rise and fall with a period of exactly one hour. The logger samples every 10 min.   1. Frequencies (per hour): cooling cycle \(f = 60/12 = 5\) h⁻¹; sampling \(f_s = 60/10 = 6\) h⁻¹; Nyquist frequency \(f_N = 3\) h⁻¹. Since \(f > f_N\), the cycle is under-sampled.

Digital filtering: from moving averages to the Kalman filter

Once the samples are stored, noise can be reduced by combining neighbouring samples — at the price of lag (the filtered signal responds late) and smoothing (real short events are flattened). Every filter is a trade-off between these two. Four filters cover almost every need in a growing project:

  • The moving average (boxcar) replaces each value by the mean of the last N samples.
  • Exponential smoothing (the exponential moving average, EMA) mixes each new sample with the previous output; it needs a single stored number, which makes it ideal for microcontrollers.
  • The median filter replaces each value by the median of a window. It is non-linear: a spike shorter than half the window disappears completely, and a genuine step passes through undistorted. It is the tool of choice against isolated spikes.
  • The Kalman filter treats filtering as estimation: it combines a model prediction and a measurement, each weighted by its uncertainty, and reports its own uncertainty as it goes.
Moving average and exponential smoothing: noise reduction and lag(9.3.9)
\[ \begin{gathered} y_n^{\text{MA}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x_{n-j}, \qquad \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \\[8pt] y_n^{\text{EMA}} = \alpha\,x_n + (1-\alpha)\,y_{n-1}, \qquad \frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_x} = \sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}}, \qquad \alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau} \end{gathered} \]

the moving average of the last N samples reduces white noise by the square root of N; exponential smoothing adds a fraction α of each new sample to (1 − α) times the previous output, reduces the noise to the square root of α over (2 − α), and behaves exactly like a first-order sensor with time constant τ when α is one minus the exponential of minus the sampling interval over τ.

Follow the derivation
  1. Moving average. The output is the mean of N independent noisy samples, so by Eq. 9.3.1 its noise is \(\sigma_x/\sqrt{N}\). A step in the true signal takes N samples to pass through completely, and the output lags by \((N-1)\Delta t/2\) on average.
  2. EMA as weighted sum. Unrolling the recursion: \(y_n = \alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-\alpha)^j x_{n-j}\) — geometrically decreasing weights that sum to \(\alpha/(1-(1-\alpha)) = 1\).
  3. EMA noise. For independent noise, \(\sigma_y^2 = \alpha^2\sigma_x^2\sum_j (1-\alpha)^{2j} = \dfrac{\alpha^2\sigma_x^2}{1-(1-\alpha)^2} = \dfrac{\alpha^2\sigma_x^2}{\alpha(2-\alpha)} = \dfrac{\alpha}{2-\alpha}\sigma_x^2\).
  4. Equivalence of the two. Setting \(1/N = \alpha/(2-\alpha)\) gives \(N = (2-\alpha)/\alpha\): an EMA with α = 0.18 removes as much noise as a 10-point moving average.
  5. EMA is a digital first-order sensor. The first-order equation \(\tau\,\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}t + y = x\) (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.2), solved exactly over one interval with \(x\) held constant at \(x_n\), gives \(y_n = x_n + (y_{n-1} - x_n)e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\), i.e. \(y_n = \alpha x_n + (1-\alpha)y_{n-1}\) with \(\alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\). For \(\Delta t \ll \tau\), \(\alpha \approx \Delta t/\tau\). Its lag after a ramp is τ, exactly as for a slow sensor.
Assumptions and validity — noise is not always white

The \(1/\sqrt{N}\) law assumes white (uncorrelated) noise. Slow, correlated fluctuations (drift, 1/f noise) are reduced much less. Linear filters also smear gross errors: one +10 °C spike in a 10-point moving average becomes a +1 °C bump lasting ten samples. Remove spikes first (median or Hampel filter, last section), then smooth. Keep the raw data; store the filtered series as a separate, documented variable.

The Kalman filter (Kalman, 1960) generalises exponential smoothing. Instead of a fixed α, it computes, at every step, how much to trust the new measurement relative to its own prediction — and it does so from two numbers that have a physical meaning: how fast the true value can change (the process noise Q) and how noisy the sensor is (the measurement noise R). The one-dimensional version below is all you need for a single sensor; the matrix version fuses many sensors and models and is the engine of navigation systems, weather forecasting and the digital twins of Lesson 10.5.

One-dimensional Kalman filter (random-walk model)(9.3.10)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{predict:}\quad \hat{x}_k^{-} = \hat{x}_{k-1}, \qquad P_k^{-} = P_{k-1} + Q \\[8pt] \text{update:}\quad K_k = \frac{P_k^{-}}{P_k^{-} + R}, \qquad \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_k^{-} + K_k\left(z_k - \hat{x}_k^{-}\right), \qquad P_k = \left(1 - K_k\right)P_k^{-} \end{gathered} \]

predict: the best guess for the new step is the previous estimate, and its variance grows by the process noise Q; update: the Kalman gain is the predicted variance divided by the sum of the predicted variance and the measurement noise variance R; the new estimate moves from the prediction towards the measurement by the gain times the innovation; and the variance shrinks by the factor one minus the gain.

Follow the derivation — optimal weighting of two estimates
  1. Model. The true value follows a random walk, \(x_k = x_{k-1} + w_k\) with \(\mathrm{Var}(w_k) = Q\); the sensor measures \(z_k = x_k + v_k\) with \(\mathrm{Var}(v_k) = R\); all noises independent.
  2. Predict. The best guess of \(x_k\) before measuring is \(\hat{x}_{k-1}\); its error is the old error plus \(w_k\), and independent variances add: \(P_k^- = P_{k-1} + Q\).
  3. Combine the prediction and the measurement linearly: \(\hat{x}_k = (1-K)\hat{x}_k^- + Kz_k\). Its error variance is \(P = (1-K)^2P^- + K^2R\) (Eq. 9.3.3 with independent errors).
  4. Optimise. \(\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}K = -2(1-K)P^- + 2KR = 0 \Rightarrow K = P^-/(P^- + R)\) — the inverse-variance weighting you would also use to average two thermometers of different quality.
  5. Posterior variance. Substituting K: \(P = (1-K)P^-\), which is smaller than both \(P^-\) and \(R\).
  6. Steady state. With constant Q and R the variance converges; solving \(P^- = (1-K)P^- + Q\) with \(K = P^-/(P^-+R)\) gives \((P^-)^2 = QP^- + QR\), so \(P^-_\infty = \tfrac12\left(Q + \sqrt{Q^2 + 4QR}\right)\) and \(K_\infty = P^-_\infty/(P^-_\infty + R)\). In steady state the Kalman filter is an EMA with \(\alpha = K_\infty\) — but with α derived from physics, not guessed.
Assumptions and extensions

The filter is optimal for linear models with independent Gaussian noise and correctly chosen Q and R; wrong values make it either sluggish (Q too small) or noisy (Q too large). A spike violates the Gaussian assumption and pulls the estimate — gate it out when the innovation \(|z - \hat{x}^-|\) exceeds, say, \(3\sqrt{P^- + R}\). Richer models include a known input (the heater is on, so temperature rises at a known rate), several state variables (temperature and its rate of change), several sensors (sensor fusion), and non-linear models (the extended and ensemble Kalman filters). Welch and Bishop (2004) give a readable introduction.

Worked example 7Two steps of a Kalman filter by hand

A nutrient-tank temperature is logged every minute. The sensor noise has a standard deviation of 0.3 °C (R = 0.09 °C²), and the true temperature changes by about 0.05 °C per minute (Q = 0.0025 °C²). You start with \(\hat{x}_0 = 20.0\) °C and a large uncertainty, \(P_0 = 1.0\) °C². The first two measurements are 20.6 and 20.3 °C. Compute the estimates, their standard uncertainties, and the steady-state gain.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Step 1, predict: \(\hat{x}_1^- = 20.0\); \(P_1^- = 1.0 + 0.0025 = 1.0025\).
  2. Step 1, update: \(K_1 = 1.0025/(1.0025 + 0.09) = 0.918\) — the prediction is poor, so the filter trusts the measurement. \(\hat{x}_1 = 20.0 + 0.918\times(20.6 - 20.0) = 20.551\) °C; \(P_1 = (1 - 0.918)\times1.0025 = 0.0826\) °C² (u = 0.29 °C).
  3. Step 2, predict: \(\hat{x}_2^- = 20.551\); \(P_2^- = 0.0826 + 0.0025 = 0.0851\).
  4. Step 2, update: \(K_2 = 0.0851/(0.0851 + 0.09) = 0.486\); \(\hat{x}_2 = 20.551 + 0.486\times(20.3 - 20.551) = 20.429\) °C; \(P_2 = 0.514\times0.0851 = 0.0437\) °C² (u = 0.21 °C).
  5. Steady state (Eq. 9.3.10): \(P^-_\infty = \tfrac12\left(0.0025 + \sqrt{0.0025^2 + 4\times0.0025\times0.09}\right) = 0.0163\); \(K_\infty = 0.0163/(0.0163 + 0.09) = 0.153\), and the steady-state uncertainty is \(\sqrt{(1-0.153)\times0.0163} = 0.12\) °C.
x̂₁ = 20.55 °C (u 0.29), x̂₂ = 20.43 °C (u 0.21); K∞ ≈ 0.15, u∞ ≈ 0.12 °C

Interpretation: after a few steps the filter behaves like an EMA with α ≈ 0.15 (equivalent to a moving average of about (2 − 0.15)/0.15 ≈ 12 samples), and it reports that the filtered value is uncertain by 0.12 °C — less than half the single-reading noise. The live calculator of Eq. 9.3.10 reproduces K∞.

Which filter should you use? For a display or a slow trend — the tank temperature shown on your dashboard — an EMA is simple and adequate; choose α from the time constant you are willing to accept. For control, every second of lag costs stability (Lesson 10.4): filter as little as possible, and remove spikes with a short median rather than smoothing heavily. For data that will be analysed later, store the raw values and filter afterwards; offline you may use centred (non-causal) windows that have no lag at all. When you know the sensor noise and something about the process — as in Worked example 7 — the Kalman filter turns that knowledge into the best weighting and also tells you how uncertain the result is. And whatever you choose, document it: a filtered series without its filter parameters is an unrepeatable measurement.

Frontier — estimation instead of measurement

Modern greenhouses increasingly estimate what they cannot measure directly. Kalman-type filters combine climate sensors with a crop model to estimate transpiration or biomass (a "soft sensor"), and data assimilation keeps digital twins synchronised with reality — the approach you can try in the lettuce digital-twin laboratory. The quality of such estimates depends on realistic Q and R, that is, on honest uncertainty budgets for the sensors.

Data quality: flags, metadata and a checklist for your project

Environmental sensor networks produce far more data than anyone can inspect by eye, and experience from ecological observatories shows that automated quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) must be designed in from the start (Campbell et al., 2013). QA is everything you do to prevent problems (calibration, shielding, maintenance); QC is the detection and labelling of problems in the data. The key principles are simple: never overwrite raw data; run automatic tests; attach a quality flag to every value; and record the metadata without which nobody — including you in three months — can interpret the numbers.

Sensor streams versus experimental data

Automatic removal of flagged points is appropriate for high-frequency sensor streams, where a spike is clearly an artefact. For your experimental response data — the fresh weight of a lettuce, a sensory score — the rules are stricter: a surprising value is not an error until you find its cause, and deleting it to improve a result is research misconduct (Lesson 3.2).

Many observatories organise their data in processing levels, and the same structure works for a student project. Level 0 is the raw file exactly as the logger wrote it — never edited, backed up in two places. Level 1 adds calibration corrections and quality flags; every value can still be traced back to its raw counterpart. Level 2 contains derived and aggregated products — 10-min means, daily light integrals, VPD, EC₂₅ — computed only from values with acceptable flags, with the uncertainty budget stated. Your statistical analysis and your report use level 2; your reviewer, if in doubt, can reproduce it from level 0. A small script that turns level 0 into level 2 is worth more than any amount of manual spreadsheet editing, because it can be rerun when you discover a calibration error in week 6.

Finally, metadata: data about the data. The FAIR principles — data should be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable — were formulated for research data in general (Wilkinson et al., 2016), and they apply to a student's CSV file as much as to a national archive. Key terms

Trueness
Closeness of the mean of many replicate measured values to the reference value; the absence of systematic error (bias).
Precision
Closeness of agreement between replicate measured values; the absence of random error. Repeatability and reproducibility are precision under same and changed conditions.
Bias
An estimate of the systematic error of a measurement; corrected by calibration, not reduced by averaging.
Standard uncertainty
Uncertainty of a result expressed as a standard deviation, \(u\).
Type A evaluation
Evaluation of a standard uncertainty by statistical analysis of a series of observations, e.g. \(u(\bar{x}) = s/\sqrt{n}\).
Type B evaluation
Evaluation of a standard uncertainty by any other means — data sheets, certificates, resolution, physics — e.g. \(u = a/\sqrt{3}\) for a rectangular distribution.
Law of propagation of uncertainty
The first-order GUM rule \(u_c^2 = \sum c_i^2u^2(x_i) + \text{covariance terms}\) that carries input uncertainties through a measurement model.
Expanded uncertainty
\(U = k\,u_c\); with coverage factor k = 2 it defines an interval with about 95 % coverage probability for a normal distribution.
Calibration
Establishing, with reference standards of known uncertainty, the relation between a sensor's indications and the values of the measurand.
Metrological traceability
Property of a result that can be related to the SI through an unbroken, documented chain of calibrations, each contributing to the uncertainty.
Inverse prediction
Converting a new indication back into a value of the measurand through a calibration line, with an uncertainty given by Eq. 9.3.6.
Drift
Slow change of a sensor's characteristic with time; controlled by recalibration intervals and in-service checks.
Aliasing
The appearance of a false, lower frequency when a signal is sampled at less than twice its highest frequency (the Nyquist criterion).
Exponential smoothing
Recursive filter \(y_n = \alpha x_n + (1-\alpha)y_{n-1}\), the digital equivalent of a first-order sensor with \(\alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\).
Kalman filter
A recursive estimator that combines model predictions and measurements with weights set by their variances (Q and R), and reports the uncertainty of its estimate.
Quality flag
A code stored with each value (e.g. 1 pass, 3 suspect, 4 fail, 9 missing) recording the outcome of quality-control tests.
