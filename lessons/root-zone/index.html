The root zone: oxygen, temperature and microbiology

Lift the raft of a healthy lettuce pond and you see a curtain of white, fine roots. Lift the raft of a warm tank whose air pump failed two days ago and you find brown, slimy roots that smell of fermentation. The difference is a few milligrams of oxygen per litre and a few degrees Celsius. This lesson follows oxygen from the air into the solution and into the root, shows how temperature squeezes the supply while it raises the demand, and opens the door to the microbial world — Pythium and the beneficial microbes that keep it in check — that lives in every recirculating system. You will leave with the equations to design and test the aeration of your own system, and a practical routine to keep its root zone healthy.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain why roots need a continuous supply of oxygen, and estimate the oxygen uptake rate of a crop from respiration stoichiometry, root mass and temperature (Q₁₀) (LO2, LO3).
  • Calculate dissolved-oxygen saturation with Henry's law and the Benson–Krause equation, and predict the effects of temperature, altitude, salinity and pure oxygen (LO2, LO3).
  • Derive the tank oxygen balance \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = k_La(C^{*}-C)-\text{OUR}\), use it to size aeration and to predict hypoxia after a failure, and measure \(k_La\) and OUR in your own system (LO3, LO4).
  • Recognise hypoxia and Pythium root rot, explain the physiology behind their symptoms, and justify a root-zone temperature target for lettuce (LO2).
  • Compare heat, UV, ozone and slow sand filtration for disinfecting recirculating solution — including a UV-dose calculation — and explain the roles of beneficial microbes, biofilms and root exudates (LO2, LO3, LO7).
  • Plan root-zone monitoring, hygiene and a failure plan for your project system (LO4).

Roots breathe: the oxygen demand of the root zone

A root is a living, non-photosynthetic organ that runs entirely on imported sugar. Sucrose made in the leaves travels down the phloem, and the root burns it in its mitochondria to make ATP. That ATP drives almost everything a root does: the proton pumps (H⁺-ATPases) that energise the uptake of nitrate, phosphate and potassium against their concentration gradients; cell division and elongation at the root tips; the synthesis of proteins, membranes and cell walls; and the maintenance of the living cells whose aquaporins let water into the root. Burning sugar efficiently requires oxygen, and a root cannot make its own: every molecule must arrive from outside (Drew, 1997).

In soil, oxygen reaches roots through the network of air-filled pores (Lesson 5.1), where it diffuses about ten thousand times faster than in water. In solution culture the roots sit in water. Oxygen must first dissolve in the solution, then be carried towards the root by mixing, and finally diffuse through a thin, nearly stagnant layer of water at the root surface before the root cells can use it. Figure 1 shows this oxygen cascade. Each step can limit the supply, and this lesson quantifies every one of them — starting with the demand.

How much oxygen does a root need? The chemistry of aerobic respiration gives the exchange rate between sugar and oxygen, and the root mass and its specific activity give the rate:

Root respiration: stoichiometry and oxygen demand(5.3.1)
\[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6\,O_2 \longrightarrow 6\,CO_2 + 6\,H_2O} \\[8pt] \text{OUR}_{\text{tank}} = \frac{n\;q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\;m_{\text{root}}}{V} \end{gathered} \]

one molecule of glucose is oxidised by six molecules of oxygen to six of carbon dioxide and six of water. The oxygen uptake rate of a whole tank, per litre of solution, is the number of plants times the specific respiration rate of their roots times the root dry mass per plant, divided by the solution volume.

Follow the derivation
  1. Balance the reaction. Glucose contains 6 C, 12 H and 6 O. Six CO₂ take 12 O atoms and six H₂O take 6 O atoms: 18 O atoms in total, of which 6 come from glucose, so 12 atoms = 6 O₂ molecules must be supplied.
  2. Mass ratio. \(6\times32.00/180.16 = 1.066\) g of O₂ per g of glucose respired. Because one CO₂ is released for each O₂ consumed, the respiratory quotient RQ = 6/6 = 1: measuring CO₂ release or O₂ uptake is equivalent for carbohydrate substrates.
  3. Why oxygen matters so much. With oxygen as the final electron acceptor, one glucose yields about 30 ATP (older textbooks quote 36–38); without oxygen, glycolysis followed by fermentation yields only 2 ATP (Drew, 1997). A root that loses its oxygen supply therefore loses about 93 % of its energy supply at once (Section 5.3.4).
  4. Scale up. Respiration per plant is proportional to how much active root tissue there is: \(\text{OUR}_{\text{plant}} = q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,m_{\text{root}}\) (mg h⁻¹). All \(n\) plants draw on the same solution, so the tank loses \(n\,q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,m_{\text{root}}\) mg of O₂ per hour.
  5. Per litre. Dividing by the volume gives the rate at which the dissolved-oxygen concentration would fall if nothing replaced it — the quantity that aeration must balance in Eq. 5.3.5.
Assumptions and validity

\(q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\) is not a constant: it is highest in young, fast-growing root tips, depends on the growth rate of the plant and on how much nitrate and potassium the roots are pumping, and rises with temperature (Eq. 5.3.2). Microorganisms living in the solution and in biofilms also consume oxygen — a biochemical oxygen demand that becomes important when root debris, algae or organic fertilisers are present. The specific respiration rate \(q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\) is the uncertain number. Before you have a measurement, you can estimate it from the plant's carbon budget — the kind of order-of-magnitude reasoning an engineer uses to size equipment before the data exist.

Worked example 1How much oxygen does a lettuce take from the solution?

A butterhead lettuce close to harvest adds about 0.6 g of dry matter per day (≈ 12 g of fresh weight at 5 % dry matter). Plant dry matter is about 42 % carbon. Assume that the plant respires about as much carbon as it builds into new biomass — a carbon-use efficiency of about one half, typical of fast-growing plants (Lambers et al., 2008) — and that its roots account for a quarter of whole-plant respiration. Estimate the root oxygen uptake rate in mg O₂ h⁻¹. Repeat for a young plant that grows 0.15 g of dry matter per day.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Carbon built into new growth: \(0.6\ \text{g d}^{-1}\times0.42 = 0.252\) g C d⁻¹.
  2. Whole-plant respiration ≈ the same amount of carbon: \(0.252/12.01 = 21.0\) mmol CO₂ d⁻¹.
  3. Root share: \(0.25\times21.0 = 5.25\) mmol CO₂ d⁻¹. With RQ = 1 (Eq. 5.3.1) the roots consume 5.25 mmol O₂ d⁻¹ = \(5.25\times32.00 = 168\) mg O₂ d⁻¹.
  4. Per hour: \(168/24 = 7.0\) mg O₂ h⁻¹ per plant. The young plant grows four times more slowly: ≈ 1.75 mg O₂ h⁻¹.
  5. Sanity checks. A lettuce root system near harvest weighs perhaps 1–1.5 g of dry matter, so \(q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\approx\) 5–7 mg O₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹ — inside the range of Eq. 5.3.1. Six such plants in a 40 L tank consume 42 mg h⁻¹, i.e. 1.05 mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹: without aeration, air-saturated solution (≈ 9 mg L⁻¹) would fall to 4 mg L⁻¹ in under five hours. Respiration, like most enzyme-driven processes, speeds up with temperature. Physiologists summarise this with the temperature coefficient \(Q_{10}\), the factor by which the rate increases for a 10 °C rise:

Temperature scaling of root respiration (Q₁₀ rule)(5.3.2)
\[ \text{OUR}(T) = \text{OUR}_{20}\;Q_{10}^{\,(T-20)/10} \]

the oxygen uptake rate at solution temperature T equals the rate at 20 °C multiplied by Q₁₀ raised to the power of the temperature difference from 20 °C divided by ten.

Follow the derivation
  1. Definition. \(Q_{10} = R(T+10)/R(T)\): the rate ratio for a 10 °C step.
  2. Many small steps. If each 10 °C multiplies the rate by \(Q_{10}\), then each 1 °C multiplies it by \(Q_{10}^{1/10}\), and \(\Delta T\) degrees multiply it by \(Q_{10}^{\Delta T/10}\). With \(\Delta T = T-20\) this is Eq. 5.3.2.
  3. Per degree. For \(Q_{10}=2\): \(2^{0.1} = 1.072\), i.e. respiration rises by about 7 % per °C. From 20 to 27 °C it rises by \(2^{0.7} = 1.62\), i.e. by 62 %.
  4. Link to Arrhenius. The Arrhenius law \(R\propto e^{-E_a/(RT)}\) gives \(Q_{10} = \exp\!\big(10E_a/(RT(T+10))\big)\) with \(T\) in kelvin: \(Q_{10}\) is not a fundamental constant but declines slowly as temperature rises.
Assumptions and validity

A \(Q_{10}\) of about 2 is a good default between 15 and 30 °C, but measured values vary, \(Q_{10}\) tends to decline at higher temperatures, and plants partly acclimate their respiration to a new temperature within days (Atkin & Tjoelker, 2003). Above about 30–35 °C, damage to membranes and enzymes dominates and the rule no longer applies. The course library implements the rule as q10().

The exponent \((T-20)/10\) is °C divided by °C — dimensionless, as an exponent must be — so \(Q_{10}^{(T-20)/10}\) is a pure number and mg h⁻¹ × (–) = mg h⁻¹ ✓
Key idea · a race between supply and demand

Almost everything in this lesson follows from one comparison: the rate at which oxygen can be delivered to the root surface versus the rate at which roots consume it. Warm water holds less oxygen and roots consume it faster; a failed air pump removes the delivery but not the consumption; a stagnant root mat lengthens the diffusion path. When demand wins, roots become hypoxic — and hypoxic roots are an open door for pathogens.

How much oxygen can water hold? Henry's law and temperature

Oxygen is a sparingly soluble gas. Dry air contains 20.95 % oxygen by volume; at sea level this is a partial pressure of about 21 kPa and 8.7 mol of O₂ per cubic metre of air. Water in equilibrium with that air at 20 °C holds only 0.28 mol m⁻³, or 9.1 mg L⁻¹ — about thirty times less oxygen per unit volume than the air above it. The link between the gas phase and the dissolved concentration was found experimentally by William Henry in 1803: at a given temperature, the amount of a gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid.

Henry's law for dissolved oxygen(5.3.3)
\[ C^{*} = k_H\,p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = k_H\;x_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,\big(P - p_w(T)\big) \]

the saturation (equilibrium) concentration of dissolved oxygen equals Henry's solubility constant times the partial pressure of oxygen in the gas. The partial pressure is the mole fraction of oxygen in the dry gas times the total pressure minus the pressure of water vapour, because the gas right above the water is saturated with vapour.

Follow the derivation
  1. A dynamic equilibrium. O₂ molecules from the gas continually strike the water surface and some dissolve; dissolved molecules continually escape. By kinetic theory, the entry rate is proportional to the number of gas molecules hitting the surface, i.e. to \(p_{\mathrm{O_2}}\); the escape rate is proportional to the dissolved concentration \(C\).
  2. Equal rates. At equilibrium, \(k_{\text{in}}\,p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = k_{\text{out}}\,C^{*}\), hence \(C^{*} = (k_{\text{in}}/k_{\text{out}})\,p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = k_H\,p_{\mathrm{O_2}}\). Thermodynamics gives the same linear law from the equality of chemical potentials of O₂ in the gas and in a dilute solution.
  3. The partial pressure. Dalton's law: \(p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = x_{\mathrm{O_2}}\times\) (pressure of the dry part of the gas). The air touching the water is saturated with vapour, so the dry part is \(P - p_w(T)\).
  4. Units. 1 mol m⁻³ = 1 mmol L⁻¹, and O₂ has a molar mass of 32.00 g mol⁻¹, so mol m⁻³ × 32.00 = g m⁻³ = mg L⁻¹. Constants are also tabulated as mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹: 1.37 × 10⁻⁵ mol m⁻³ Pa⁻¹ = 1.39 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹.
  5. Check at 20 °C. \(p_{\mathrm{O_2}} = 0.2095\times(101\,325 - 2\,340) = 20\,740\) Pa; \(C^{*} = 1.37\times10^{-5}\times20\,740 = 0.284\) mol m⁻³ = 9.09 mg L⁻¹ ✓ (the \(k_H\) values in the table are back-calculated from the Benson–Krause equation, Eq. 5.3.4, and agree with the compilation of Sander, 2023).
Assumptions and validity

Henry's law holds for dilute solutions of gases that do not react with water — an excellent approximation for O₂. Salinity lowers solubility ("salting out"), but a nutrient solution of EC ≈ 2 dS m⁻¹ contains only about 1 g of salts per kg, which lowers \(C^{*}\) by about 0.6 % (Benson–Krause salinity term) — negligible. Hydrostatic pressure at the bottom of a 30 cm tank adds only 2.9 kPa (+3 %) to the gas pressure in a bubble there. Altitude matters more: at 1000 m the total pressure is 11 % lower and so is \(C^{*}\).

The strongest effect on \(C^{*}\) in a hydroponic system is temperature. Dissolving oxygen releases heat — about 12 kJ per mole — so, by Le Chatelier's principle, warming the water pushes the equilibrium back towards the gas. Warm water holds less oxygen.

Common misconception

"Bubbling air through the tank supersaturates it with oxygen." Aeration can only drive the dissolved oxygen towards the saturation value \(C^{*}\) set by air — it never pushes it beyond. A DO meter reading well above 100 % in an air-aerated tank usually means an uncalibrated sensor or a temperature mismatch between probe and solution — or algae photosynthesising in a translucent container. Only pure oxygen (Worked example 2b) or photosynthesis raises DO above air saturation.

Getting oxygen in: mass transfer and aeration design

The saturation concentration is the ceiling. How fast a tank approaches it — and where it settles while roots consume oxygen — is a question of mass transfer. Oxygen crosses a gas–liquid interface wherever the solution touches air: at the surface of rising bubbles, at the free surface of the tank or channel, and at the surface of the thin film that covers the roots in the humid air gap of a Kratky tank. Lewis and Whitman's (1924) two-film theory pictures each interface as two thin, stagnant films — one in the gas and one in the liquid — across which molecules can move only by diffusion, while the bulk phases on either side are well mixed (Figure 2).

Combining the two-film flux with a mass balance of the tank gives the central equation of aeration engineering — the same equation used for fish tanks, wastewater aeration basins and bioreactors (Lesson 12.4):

Oxygen mass balance of a hydroponic tank(5.3.5)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_La\,\big(C^{*} - C\big) - \text{OUR}_{\text{tank}} \\[8pt] C_{ss} = C^{*} - \frac{\text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}}{k_La}, \qquad C(t) = C_{ss} + \big(C_0 - C_{ss}\big)\,e^{-k_La\,t} \end{gathered} \]

the rate of change of the dissolved-oxygen concentration equals the transfer into the solution — the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient times the distance from saturation — minus the consumption by the roots. At steady state the concentration settles below saturation by the uptake rate divided by the transfer coefficient; after any disturbance it approaches that steady state exponentially.

Follow the derivation
  1. Control volume. Take the solution in the tank, volume \(V\), well mixed so that it has one concentration \(C\). Mass of dissolved O₂ = \(VC\).
  2. Balance. Accumulation = transfer in − consumption: \(\mathrm{d}(VC)/\mathrm{d}t = N\,A - V\,\text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}\), where \(N\) is the flux per unit interface area and \(A\) the total interface area (bubbles + surface).
  3. Two-film flux. For a sparingly soluble gas the liquid film controls: \(N = K_L(C^{*}-C)\), with \(K_L\approx D/\delta_L\) (m s⁻¹). Dividing by the constant \(V\): \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = K_L\,(A/V)\,(C^{*}-C) - \text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}\).
  4. Lump the unknowns. \(K_L\) and \(a = A/V\) are hard to measure separately (who can count the bubbles?), so they are combined into one measurable coefficient \(k_La\) (s⁻¹ or h⁻¹). This gives the first form of Eq. 5.3.5.
  5. Steady state. Set \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = 0\): \(k_La(C^{*}-C_{ss}) = \text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}\), so \(C_{ss} = C^{*} - \text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}/k_La\). The "oxygen deficit" \(C^{*}-C_{ss}\) is the price paid to drive the flux.
  6. Transient. Let \(y = C - C_{ss}\). Then \(\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}t = -k_La\,y\), whose solution is \(y = y_0e^{-k_La\,t}\): the third form. After a time \(1/k_La\) the remaining deficit has fallen to 37 %, after \(3/k_La\) to 5 %.
  7. Aeration failure. With the air pump off, \(k_La\) drops to the small surface value and \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t\approx-\text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}\): the concentration falls linearly and reaches a critical value \(C_{\text{crit}}\) after \(\tau = (C_0 - C_{\text{crit}})/\text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}\) — exactly the "buffer over rate" estimate of Eq. 5.1.8 in Lesson 5.1.
  8. Design rule. To keep \(C \ge C_{\text{target}}\), the aeration must provide \(k_La \ge \text{OUR}_{\text{tank}}/(C^{*}-C_{\text{target}})\).
Assumptions and validity

The tank is assumed perfectly mixed; in a real tank DO is lowest in dense root masses far from the air stone, so measure at several positions. OUR is taken as constant, whereas roots slow their respiration when DO becomes very low (the simulator below includes this). \(k_La\) depends on air flow, bubble size, depth, diffuser fouling, surfactants and temperature (it rises about 2.4 % per °C, \(\theta\) = 1.024; ASCE, 2007), and a small extra \(k_La\) comes from the free surface.

\(k_La\,(C^{*}-C)\): h⁻¹ × mg L⁻¹ = mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹, the same unit as \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t\) and OUR ✓; \(\text{OUR}/k_La\): (mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹)/h⁻¹ = mg L⁻¹ ✓

How large is \(k_La\) for the aquarium air pump and air stone that most project groups will use? The answer follows from how much oxygen the pump pushes into the tank and what fraction of it dissolves before the bubbles burst at the surface.

Aeration capacity of an air pump and air stone(5.3.6)
\[ \begin{gathered} \text{OTR}_{\max} = E_{\mathrm{O_2}}\;\rho_{\mathrm{O_2,air}}\;Q_{\text{air}}, \qquad k_La \approx \frac{\text{OTR}_{\max}}{V\,C^{*}} \\[8pt] k_La(T) = k_La(20)\;\theta^{\,T-20} \end{gathered} \]

Dividing by the tank volume and the saturation concentration gives the volumetric transfer coefficient. The coefficient increases by a factor θ per degree of warming.

The last millimetre: why mixing matters at the root surface

Even in a well-aerated tank, the water touching a root is not well mixed. Friction slows the flow near any surface, leaving an unstirred (diffusion boundary) layer through which oxygen moves only by molecular diffusion — the third step of the cascade in Figure 1. Fick's first law, already met for aeroponic root films in Lesson 7.1 (Eq. 7.1.9), tells us how thick this layer may be before it limits the root:

Oxygen supply through the unstirred layer at the root(5.3.7)
\[ J = D_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,\frac{C_b - C_s}{\delta} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta_{\max} = \frac{D_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,\big(C_b - C_{s,\min}\big)\,A_{\text{root}}}{\text{OUR}_{\text{plant}}} \]

the oxygen flux per unit root area equals the diffusion coefficient times the concentration difference between the bulk solution and the root surface, divided by the thickness of the unstirred layer. Setting this supply equal to the demand per unit root area gives the thickest layer the root can tolerate.

The average hides the extremes. That is why DWC roots deep inside a mat, or at the bottom of a sluggish NFT channel, can be hypoxic although the bulk DO looks fine — and why bubbles and flow, which thin the boundary layer, matter beyond their contribution to \(k_La\). This last millimetre explains why the three project systems solve the oxygen problem so differently. DWC relies on bubbles to keep \(C_b\) high and to stir the root mass. That is also why a Kratky tank must never be topped up: raising the level drowns the oxygen roots.

When oxygen runs out: hypoxia and its symptoms

Plant physiologists distinguish three states of the root: normoxia, in which oxygen is sufficient for full aerobic respiration; hypoxia, in which oxygen partly limits ATP production; and anoxia, in which there is no oxygen and only fermentation remains (Drew, 1997). The transitions are gradual and happen at different times in different tissues: because every cell on the way consumes oxygen, the centre of a thick root, a dense root mat or a root tip becomes hypoxic while its surface still looks fine. Figure 5 follows a root cell as its oxygen runs out.

The energy crisis explains the symptoms a grower sees — including the most confusing one, wilting in the middle of water. A lettuce with its roots in solution can wilt at midday when its roots are hypoxic. Tournaire-Roux et al. (2003) showed that anoxia acidifies the cytosol of root cells and that this acidosis closes (gates) the aquaporins — the water channels of the cell membranes — so that the hydraulic conductivity of the root falls. Water can no longer enter fast enough to match transpiration, and the leaves lose turgor: the soil–plant–atmosphere chain of Lesson 2.2 breaks at the root. At the same time the proton pumps slow, uptake of nitrate and potassium falls and roots can leak potassium; growth stalls within hours, root tips die first, and longer episodes kill whole roots, which turn brown and soft and invite infection (Section 5.3.6). Morard and Silvestre (1996) review how quickly these injuries develop in soilless crops.

Common misconception

"My plants are wilting, so they need more water." In soil that is often true. In hydroponics the roots are already in water — wilting of a hydroponic crop is a symptom of a root problem: hypoxia, root rot, a very high EC, or cold solution that lowers root conductance. Check DO, temperature and the roots before you touch anything else. And in a Kratky tank, never "fix" wilting by raising the solution level: that drowns the oxygen roots and makes it worse (Kratky, 2009).

Root-zone temperature: the master variable

Solution temperature touches every part of this lesson at once. It sets the oxygen ceiling (Table 1: about −1.8 % per °C near 20 °C) and the roots' oxygen demand (about +7 % per °C for \(Q_{10}\) = 2); it controls the speed of nutrient uptake and root growth and the viscosity of water; and it sets the growth rate of the pathogens that attack stressed roots (Section 5.3.6). Because a tank of water has a large heat capacity, root-zone temperature is also the environmental variable that is easiest to hold steady — which makes it a powerful lever for the grower.

What is the right temperature for lettuce? In the Cornell floating system, Thompson et al. (1998) crossed three pond temperatures (17, 24 and 31 °C) with three air temperatures for 35 days. Maximum dry mass was produced at 24 °C air with a 24 °C pond, and a 24 °C pond maintained marketable heads even in 31 °C air — cooling only the roots compensated for hot shoots. Levine et al. (2023) held the root zone of red-leaf lettuce at 15, 25 or 35 °C under 25/22 °C air: growth was greatest at 25 °C; 35 °C reduced biomass but strongly increased anthocyanins, carotenoids and chlorophyll; 15 °C gave intermediate growth. Extension guidance for hydroponic leafy greens recommends a root-zone target of 20–24 °C, partly for disease reasons: lower temperatures favour Pythium dissotocum, higher temperatures P. aphanidermatum (Mattson, 2018). For lettuce, therefore: aim for about 20–24 °C, avoid sustained temperatures much above that, and remember that cold solution slows growth and water uptake.

Why does the tank drift away from the air temperature at all? Because energy flows in and out. A submersible pump turns all of its electrical power into heat inside the water — in a small tank it is often the largest heat source. Lamps warm the lid and any exposed solution, heaters and chillers add or remove heat deliberately, and evaporation cools open surfaces. A simple energy balance predicts the result.

Pythium and other root pathogens in recirculating water

Pythium root rot is the most common, yield-limiting disease of hydroponic leafy greens and herbs (Amrhein et al., 2025). Pythium species are oomycetes ("water moulds"). They look like fungi, with thread-like hyphae, but they are more closely related to brown algae and diatoms, and their cell walls are built from cellulose-like glucans rather than chitin — one reason why many fungicides do not work against them. What makes oomycetes so dangerous in hydroponics is their swimming spore, the zoospore.

An infected root forms sporangia that release zoospores. Each zoospore swims with two flagella and is attracted chemically to the exudates of young roots (chemotaxis). On contact it encysts, germinates and penetrates the root, and the pathogen spreads through the cortex. Thick-walled oospores formed in dead tissue survive in root debris, growing media and dust and restart the cycle later (Sutton et al., 2006). In soil, zoospores depend on thin water films between particles and spread slowly; in a recirculating system the pump carries them to every plant within minutes. Stanghellini and Rasmussen (1994) titled their classic review, ironically, "Hydroponics: a solution for zoosporic pathogens". Figure 7 puts the disease triangle and the life cycle together.

Four species dominate reports from hydroponic leafy greens and herbs: P. aphanidermatum, P. dissotocum, P. myriotylum and P. oopapillum (Amrhein et al., 2025). Their temperature preferences differ, which is why solution temperature is a disease-management tool: lower temperatures favour the establishment of P. dissotocum, higher temperatures the development of P. aphanidermatum (Mattson, 2018). Warm solution attacks from three directions at once — faster pathogen growth, less dissolved oxygen, and roots made more susceptible by hypoxic stress — so the Pythium problem and the oxygen problem of Sections 5.3.1–5.3.5 are the same problem.

Early infections are often invisible. Sutton et al. (2006) emphasise that root infection reduces leaf-area growth and carbon accumulation even while photosynthesis per unit leaf area is maintained — a "silent" loss of yield. Visible symptoms follow: roots turn tan to brown and feel soft and water-soaked, lose their fine laterals and root hairs, and the outer cortex slips off when a root is pulled between the fingers, leaving only the thin central strand; shoots are stunted, wilt in the afternoon and may turn yellow. Other water-borne pathogens behave similarly in closed systems — Phytophthora species (also oomycetes), Fusarium species, bacteria, and viruses such as tomato mosaic virus and cucumber green mottle mosaic virus, which are easily transmitted in recirculation water (Runia, 1994a).

Biofilms: the slime on every wet surface

Every wet surface of a hydroponic system — tank walls, pipes, channels, emitters, the air stone and the roots themselves — is soon covered by a biofilm: a community of bacteria, fungi and algae embedded in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances, mostly polysaccharides, proteins and DNA (Flemming & Wingender, 2010). Biofilms matter to the grower in three ways. They consume oxygen, adding a microbial demand to the root demand of Eq. 5.3.1. They clog air stones, filters and drip emitters (Lesson 5.4). And their matrix shields the embedded cells, including pathogens, from disinfectants that would kill them in suspension (Flemming & Wingender, 2010). Not all biofilms are harmful: the biologically active top layer of a slow sand filter and the microbial coat of a healthy root are biofilms too (Section 5.3.7). The practical rule is to clean and disinfect equipment between crops, when the system is empty, because a running system cannot be sterilised without harming the crop.

The helpful majority: beneficial microbes, suppressiveness and root exudates

A running hydroponic system is never sterile, and it should not be. Within days of planting, the solution, the roots and the growing medium host large and diverse microbial communities, fed by root exudates (Vallance et al., 2011). Most are harmless, many are beneficial, and together they can make a system suppressive: a pathogen introduced into it causes less disease than it would in a sterile system. Postma et al. (2000) demonstrated this for cucumber on stone wool. Used, non-sterilised stone wool reduced root and crown rot caused by P. aphanidermatum by 52–100 % compared with the same stone wool after autoclaving; suppressiveness was associated with the fungal community and with distinct bacterial community structures, not with the total number of bacteria. Sterile is fragile: a pathogen that arrives in an empty microbial niche meets no competition.

Three groups of microorganisms are used deliberately as biological control agents in soilless culture (Table 3; Vallance et al., 2011; Lee & Lee, 2015). They work best preventively, colonising roots and media before a pathogen arrives. What feeds all these microbes? Root exudates: sugars, amino acids, organic acids, phenolic compounds, proteins and mucilage released by living roots, plus the cells they slough off. In soil, exudates are diluted and decomposed quickly. In a closed hydroponic loop they can accumulate from crop to crop. Some are toxic to the plant that produced them — autotoxicity, a form of allelopathy. Yu and Matsui (1994) identified phytotoxic substances in the root exudates of cucumber, a crop known for autotoxicity in closed systems, and Lee et al. (2006) reported that phytotoxic organic acids accumulated in lettuce nutrient solution reused during hydroponic cultivation. Exudates also attract Pythium zoospores (step ④ in Figure 7). The practical responses are simple: renew the solution between crops, remove root debris, and consider biologically active slow filters, which degrade dissolved organic matter as well as retaining pathogens.

Frontier · steering the microbiome instead of sterilising it

DNA-based methods now reveal which microorganisms live in recirculating solutions, and lab-confirmed diagnostic guides for Pythium in hydroponic leafy greens (Amrhein et al., 2025) are making early diagnosis routine. The research question has shifted from "how do we kill everything?" to "how do we assemble and maintain a microbial community that protects the crop?" — through inoculants, biologically active filters and the management of the solution's own carbon. Expect this to change hydroponic practice over the next decade.

Disinfecting the recirculating solution

Closing the loop saves water and fertiliser (Lesson 5.1, Lesson 5.2) but lets one diseased plant infect all the others. Commercial closed systems therefore treat the drain water before it returns to the crop. The requirements are demanding: inactivate or remove oomycetes, fungi, bacteria and — ideally — viruses; leave the nutrients intact; keep up with the drain flow; and remain affordable (Ehret et al., 2001; van Os, 2010). Two lessons emerge. First, no method is perfect: heat and UV are reliable against fungi and oomycetes but cost energy; slow filtration is cheap and gentle but partial; oxidants react with the nutrient solution itself. Second, disinfection kills friends as well as foes — it strips the returning solution of the suppressive community of Section 5.3.7, one argument for combining disinfection with biological control (Vallance et al., 2011). For UV disinfection, which numerous growers already used in closed systems in the early 1990s (Runia, 1994a), the physics can be condensed into one design equation.

UV dose of a lamp reactor and log inactivation(5.3.9)
\[ \bar{D} = \frac{P_{\text{UV}}\;\ell_{\text{eff}}}{Q}, \qquad \ell_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1 - 10^{-A\,d}}{A\,\ln 10}, \qquad \log_{10}\frac{N_0}{N} = \frac{\bar{D}}{D_{10}} \]

the average UV dose (fluence) delivered to the water equals the UV power of the lamp times an effective path length, divided by the flow. The effective path length accounts for absorption of UV by the water over the layer thickness. The number of tenfold (log) reductions of a microbe equals the dose divided by the dose needed for one log reduction.

Follow the derivation
  1. Dose is irradiance × time: mW cm⁻² × s = mJ cm⁻².
  2. Irradiance in an annular reactor. A long lamp of arc length \(L\) and sleeve radius \(r\) emits \(P_{\text{UV}}\). By Beer–Lambert, the power still travelling outwards at radius \(\rho\) is \(P_{\text{UV}}10^{-A(\rho-r)}\), spread over a cylinder of area \(2\pi\rho L\): \(E(\rho) = P_{\text{UV}}10^{-A(\rho-r)}/(2\pi\rho L)\).
  3. Average over the water volume \(V = \pi L\big((r+d)^2-r^2\big)\): \(\bar{E} = \frac{1}{V}\int_r^{r+d}E(\rho)\,2\pi\rho L\,\mathrm{d}\rho = \frac{P_{\text{UV}}}{V}\int_0^{d}10^{-As}\,\mathrm{d}s = \frac{P_{\text{UV}}\,\ell_{\text{eff}}}{V}\). The \(2\pi\rho L\) of the volume element cancels the geometric dilution exactly.
  4. Residence time \(t = V/Q\), so \(\bar{D} = \bar{E}\,t = P_{\text{UV}}\ell_{\text{eff}}/Q\): the reactor volume cancels too. The average dose depends only on UV power, water clarity and flow.
  5. Inactivation kinetics (Chick's law): each increment of dose inactivates the same fraction of survivors, \(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}D = -kN\), so \(N = N_0e^{-kD}\) and \(\log_{10}(N_0/N) = D/D_{10}\) with \(D_{10} = \ln10/k\).
  6. Check with data. Runia (1994a) found 1 log (−90 %) at 28 mJ cm⁻² and 3 logs (−99.9 %) at 84 mJ cm⁻² for Fusarium conidia: exactly first-order with \(D_{10}\) = 28 mJ cm⁻². For tomato mosaic virus, 2 logs at 100 mJ cm⁻² gives \(D_{10}\approx\) 50 mJ cm⁻².
Assumptions and validity

Eq. 5.3.9 gives the average dose for ideal mixing across the water layer. Real reactors have a dose distribution: water that short-circuits along the outer wall receives much less, which is why Runia's early, slow, laminar units performed poorly even at high nominal doses and why manufacturers rate reactors by a biologically validated dose. Lamp output falls with age and sleeve fouling, so design with a margin. Survival curves can "tail" when spores clump or hide behind particles. Iron chelates absorb UV and are photodegraded (cf. Albano & Miller, 2001), which both lowers the UVT and removes iron.

Hygiene. Before planting, wash tanks, lids, net pots and tubing with water and detergent, disinfect them with a household disinfectant (for example a chlorine product or hydrogen peroxide) diluted as its label specifies, rinse thoroughly and let everything dry. Use new or disinfected growing media. During the crop, use separate tools for each replicate tank — replicate tanks are independent experimental units, and a shared thermometer or net can carry zoospores from one to another and destroy both the plants and the independence of your data (Lesson 3.1). Keep lids light-proof, remove dead leaves and roots, and wash your hands before touching roots. If a tank develops root rot, do not move water or plants between tanks; lower the temperature, increase aeration, document everything and decide with your teacher whether to discard the unit.

Experiments that the root zone offers. Aerated versus non-aerated DWC (with Kratky as a third treatment) is a classic, strong-effect design; solution temperature (for example 18 versus 24 °C with aquarium heaters) is another; a weekly OUR curve measured with the dynamic method is a growth measurement that needs no destructive harvest. Plan replication and randomisation as in Lesson 3.1.

Safety

Water and electricity meet in every hydroponic system: plug pumps and heaters into an outlet protected by a residual-current device (RCD), route cables with a drip loop so water cannot run along them into a socket, and never handle mains equipment with wet hands. Wear gloves and eye protection when diluting disinfectants; never mix chlorine products with acids (toxic chlorine gas is released) or with other cleaners. Ozone generators and concentrated hydrogen peroxide are not for student systems.

Key idea

The root zone is a small ecosystem with two engineering constraints. Oxygen: supply (\(C^{*}\), \(k_La\), mixing) must exceed demand (roots and microbes), with a safety margin that survives warm days and pump failures. Microbiology: keep pathogens out and roots unstressed, so that the helpful majority of microbes stays in charge. Temperature links the two — keep lettuce roots cool and aerated, and most root problems never start.

Key terms

Root respiration
Oxidation of sugars in root mitochondria to make ATP; consumes O₂ and releases CO₂ (respiratory quotient ≈ 1).
Oxygen uptake rate (OUR)
Rate at which roots and microbes consume dissolved oxygen, per plant (mg h⁻¹) or per litre of solution (mg L⁻¹ h⁻¹).
Q₁₀
Factor by which a rate increases for a 10 °C rise in temperature; about 2 for root respiration.
Henry's law
At equilibrium, the concentration of a dissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure, \(C^{*} = k_H\,p\).
Dissolved-oxygen saturation (C*)
Equilibrium DO with air: 11.3 mg L⁻¹ at 10 °C, 9.1 at 20 °C, 7.6 at 30 °C (Benson–Krause).
Volumetric mass-transfer coefficient (kLa)
Product of the liquid-film transfer coefficient and the interfacial area per volume; sets how fast aeration restores DO (h⁻¹).
Unstirred (diffusion boundary) layer
Thin, nearly stagnant water layer at a root surface through which oxygen moves only by diffusion.
Hypoxia, anoxia
Oxygen partly limiting, or absent; roots switch to fermentation, which yields about 2 instead of about 30 ATP per glucose.
Oomycete
Fungus-like "water mould" (e.g. Pythium, Phytophthora) with cellulose-like cell walls and swimming zoospores.
Zoospore
Motile, flagellated spore of oomycetes; attracted to root exudates and spread by recirculating solution.
Biofilm
Microbial community embedded in a self-produced polymer matrix on a wet surface; consumes O₂, clogs equipment and protects cells from disinfectants.
Disease suppressiveness
Capacity of a system's microbial community to limit disease caused by an introduced pathogen.
Autotoxicity
Inhibition of a plant by compounds released from its own roots; such exudates can accumulate in closed systems.
UV dose (fluence)
UV energy received per unit area, irradiance × exposure time, in mJ cm⁻²; log reduction = dose / D₁₀.
Slow sand filtration
Filtration through sand at about 0.1 m h⁻¹, in which a biologically active top layer removes many root pathogens.
