Electrochemical sensing: pH, EC and dissolved oxygen

A hydroponic grower checks three numbers every day: pH, electrical conductivity and dissolved oxygen. Behind each of them is a small electrochemical cell in which ions, electrons and molecules of oxygen cross an interface and produce a voltage or a current. These sensors are the most informative — and the most temperamental — instruments in soilless cultivation. This lesson derives the Nernst equation from thermodynamics, takes the glass pH electrode apart, shows how to calibrate it and why temperature matters, explains how conductivity cells work and why "ppm" readings disagree, and compares Clark, galvanic and optical oxygen sensors and the ion-selective electrodes that promise to measure individual nutrients.

What you will be able to do

  • Derive the Nernst equation from the Gibbs free energy and use it to explain the 59.16 mV per pH unit slope of a glass electrode and its temperature dependence (LO2, LO5).
  • Describe the construction of a combination pH electrode and the origin of its errors: asymmetry potential, junction potential, alkaline and acid errors, ageing (LO5).
  • Perform and evaluate a two- or three-point pH calibration (slope %, offset) and apply automatic temperature compensation (LO4, LO5).
  • Calculate conductivity from cell constant and resistance, compensate it to 25 °C, and explain why TDS "ppm" values from different meters disagree (LO3, LO5).
  • Explain how Clark, galvanic and optical (luminescence-quenching) dissolved-oxygen sensors work, and use the Stern–Volmer equation (LO2, LO5).
  • Use the Nikolsky–Eisenman equation to judge whether ion-selective electrodes can measure nitrate or potassium in a nutrient solution (LO3, LO5).

Why electrochemistry rules the root zone

Roots live in water, and the chemistry of that water decides whether a crop thrives. The pH controls which forms of phosphate, iron and manganese are available to plants (Lesson 5.2); the electrical conductivity tells you, in one number, how much fertiliser is dissolved; and dissolved oxygen decides whether roots respire or suffocate (Lesson 5.3). In aquaponics the same three variables decide the welfare of the fish (Lesson 6.3). All three are measured with sensors in which the signal is generated by an electrochemical process at an interface.

Electrochemical sensors fall into three classes, and the distinction explains much of their behaviour:

Big idea

Potentiometric sensors measure activity (effective concentration) through a logarithmic law: every tenfold change in activity changes the voltage by the same number of millivolts. That makes them wonderfully wide-ranging — pH spans 10⁻¹ to 10⁻¹³ mol L⁻¹ of H⁺ — but it also means that a 1 mV error corresponds to a fixed percentage error in concentration, however large or small the concentration is.

Electrode potentials and the Nernst equation

When a metal or a membrane is in contact with a solution, charged species cross the interface until the tendency to cross in each direction is balanced. Balance is reached when the chemical driving force (a concentration difference) is exactly offset by an electrical driving force (a potential difference). The Nernst equation quantifies this balance.

Nernst equation for an ion-selective interface(9.2.1)
\[ E = E^{0} + \frac{R\,T}{z_i F}\,\ln a_i = E^{0} + \frac{\ln(10)\,R\,T}{z_i F}\,\log_{10} a_i \]

the electrode potential equals a standard potential plus the gas constant times the absolute temperature divided by the charge number of the ion times the Faraday constant, multiplied by the natural logarithm of the ion's activity. Per tenfold change in activity the potential changes by \(\ln(10)RT/(z_iF)\).

Follow the derivation from thermodynamics
  1. Electrochemical potential. The molar Gibbs energy of an ion in a phase at electrical potential \(\varphi\) is \(\tilde\mu = \mu^0 + RT\ln a + zF\varphi\): a chemical part (which grows with activity) and an electrical part (the work of bringing charge \(zF\) per mole to potential \(\varphi\)).
  2. Equilibrium across an interface. Consider a membrane permeable only to ion \(i\), separating a sample (activity \(a_s\), potential \(\varphi_s\)) from an internal solution (\(a_{\text{int}}\), \(\varphi_{\text{int}}\)). No net transfer occurs when \(\tilde\mu\) is equal on both sides: \(RT\ln a_s + z F\varphi_s = RT\ln a_{\text{int}} + zF\varphi_{\text{int}}\).
  3. Solve for the potential difference. \(\varphi_{\text{int}} - \varphi_s = \dfrac{RT}{zF}\ln\dfrac{a_s}{a_{\text{int}}}\).
  4. Collect constants. The internal activity and the potentials of the two reference electrodes are fixed, so the measured cell voltage is \(E = E^0 + \dfrac{RT}{zF}\ln a_s\).
  5. Same result from a redox reaction. For Ox + \(z\)e⁻ ⇌ Red, \(\Delta G = \Delta G^0 + RT\ln(a_{\text{Red}}/a_{\text{Ox}})\) and electrical work gives \(\Delta G = -zFE\). Dividing by \(-zF\): \(E = E^0 - \dfrac{RT}{zF}\ln\dfrac{a_{\text{Red}}}{a_{\text{Ox}}}\) — the form used for ORP electrodes.
  6. Decadic slope. Using \(\ln x = \ln(10)\log_{10}x\), the change per decade is \(s = \ln(10)RT/(zF)\). At 25 °C and \(z = 1\): \(2.302585\times8.314463\times298.15/96\,485.33 = 0.05916\) V = 59.16 mV per decade; for \(z = 2\) it is 29.58 mV.
Assumptions and validity

The equation assumes a membrane (or electrode reaction) that responds to only one ion and is at equilibrium. Real membranes also respond weakly to other ions (Eq. 9.2.10). The electrode responds to activity \(a_i = \gamma_i c_i/c^0\), not concentration; in a nutrient solution with ionic strength around 0.03 mol L⁻¹ the activity coefficient of a monovalent ion is about 0.85 (Davies equation), and about 0.52 for a divalent ion.

\(\dfrac{RT}{zF}\): \(\dfrac{\text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}}{\text{C mol}^{-1}} = \dfrac{\text{J}}{\text{C}} = \text{V}\) ✓ (the logarithm is dimensionless)

The glass pH electrode

The pH of a solution is defined (IUPAC; Buck et al., 2002) as \(\text{pH} = -\log_{10} a_{\text{H}^+}\). The instrument that measures it is one of the oldest and most successful sensors ever made: a thin bulb of special glass that behaves as a membrane selective for hydrogen ions. Modern electrodes are almost always combination electrodes, with the pH-sensing half-cell and the reference half-cell in one body (Figure 1).

How does glass "sense" hydrogen ions? When a pH glass (a lithium- or sodium-rich silicate) is soaked in water, its surfaces hydrate into gel layers only tens of nanometres thick. Silanol groups there exchange protons with the solution: ≡SiOH ⇌ ≡SiO⁻ + H⁺. The fraction of sites that are deprotonated — and therefore the surface charge and surface potential — depends on the H⁺ activity in the adjacent solution. The internal surface faces a buffer of fixed pH, so its potential is constant; the external surface potential follows the sample. Inside the membrane, charge is carried by alkali ions, not protons, so no H⁺ actually crosses the glass. The result is a membrane potential that obeys Eq. 9.2.1 with \(z = +1\), and because \(\log_{10} a_{\text{H}^+} = -\text{pH}\):

Glass-electrode response and its temperature dependence(9.2.2)
\[ E = E_{\text{iso}} - \eta\,s(T)\,(\text{pH} - \text{pH}_{\text{iso}}), \qquad s(T) = \frac{\ln(10)\,R\,T}{F} = 0.19841\,T\ \ \text{mV per pH} \]

the measured voltage equals the voltage at the isopotential point minus the electrode's efficiency times the theoretical Nernst slope times the distance in pH from the isopotential point; the theoretical slope is 0.19841 millivolts per pH unit for every kelvin of absolute temperature.

Follow the derivation
  1. Eq. 9.2.1 with \(z = 1\) and \(\log_{10}a_{\text{H}^+} = -\text{pH}\) gives \(E = E^0 - s(T)\,\text{pH}\).
  2. The internal buffer of a pH electrode is chosen (usually pH 7 with chloride) so that the inner and outer Ag/AgCl elements cancel at pH ≈ 7: an ideal electrode reads 0 mV at pH 7 at every temperature. Rewriting around this point gives \(E = E_{\text{iso}} - s(T)(\text{pH} - 7)\).
  3. Real membranes produce slightly less than the theoretical slope; the ratio is the slope efficiency \(\eta\). A small nonzero \(E_{\text{iso}}\) — the asymmetry potential plus reference and junction potentials — is the offset.
  4. The constant: \(\ln(10)R/F = 2.302585\times8.314463/96\,485.33 = 1.9841\times10^{-4}\) V K⁻¹ = 0.19841 mV K⁻¹. At 25 °C: \(0.19841\times298.15 = 59.16\) mV; at 10 °C: 56.18 mV; at 35 °C: 61.14 mV.
  5. Because \(E\) is positive in acid and negative in alkaline solution, the voltage falls by about 59 mV for each pH unit increase. The course library function electrodeMV(pH, T, slopeEff, offset) implements this equation, and nernstSlope(T) the slope.
Errors of real glass electrodes

Alkaline (sodium) error: above about pH 11–12, and especially at high Na⁺ activity, the gel layer also exchanges Na⁺ and the electrode reads too low. Acid error: below about pH 1 the electrode reads too high. Neither matters for nutrient solutions (pH 5.5–6.5) but both matter for acid and alkali cleaning or stock solutions. Liquid-junction potential: where the KCl electrolyte meets the sample, ions diffuse at different speeds and create a diffusion potential. KCl is used because K⁺ and Cl⁻ have almost equal mobilities, keeping this potential small in ordinary samples — but in very low-ionic-strength water (rain, reverse-osmosis water) readings become slow and unstable. High impedance: the glass membrane has a resistance of tens to hundreds of megaohms, so the meter must have an input impedance of at least 10¹² Ω and the cable must be shielded; a pH probe cannot be wired directly to a microcontroller ADC.

\(\eta\,s\,(\text{pH} - \text{pH}_{\text{iso}})\): 1 × mV pH⁻¹ × pH = mV ✓; \(\ln(10)R T/F\): V (see Eq. 9.2.1) ✓

Calibrating a pH electrode — and keeping it alive

Because \(E_{\text{iso}}\) and \(\eta\) differ between electrodes and change as an electrode ages, every pH measurement relies on a calibration with buffer solutions of known pH. A two-point calibration determines the two unknowns of Eq. 9.2.2; a third buffer tests whether the response is really linear (Figure 3).

Two-point calibration: slope, slope efficiency, offset and sample pH(9.2.3)
\[ S = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{\text{pH}_2 - \text{pH}_1},\qquad \eta = \frac{|S|}{s(T_{\text{cal}})}\times100\,\%,\qquad E_{\text{iso}} = E_1 + S\,(7 - \text{pH}_1),\qquad \text{pH}_x = \text{pH}_1 + \frac{E_x - E_1}{S} \]

the practical slope is the change in voltage divided by the change in pH between the two buffers; its magnitude divided by the theoretical Nernst slope is the slope efficiency in percent; the offset is the voltage the calibration line predicts at pH 7; and a sample's pH is the first buffer's pH plus the sample's voltage difference from that buffer divided by the slope.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(E_1, E_2\)stable voltages in buffers 1 and 2mV
\(\text{pH}_1, \text{pH}_2\)pH of the buffers at the calibration temperature–
\(S\)practical slope (negative)mV pH⁻¹
\(\eta\)slope efficiency ("slope %")%
\(E_x\), \(\text{pH}_x\)sample voltage and resulting pHmV, –
Follow the derivation
  1. Eq. 9.2.2 is a straight line \(E = a + S\,\text{pH}\) with two unknowns, intercept \(a\) and slope \(S = -\eta s\).
  2. Two buffers give two equations, \(E_1 = a + S\,\text{pH}_1\) and \(E_2 = a + S\,\text{pH}_2\). Subtracting eliminates \(a\) and gives \(S\).
  3. The line through buffer 1 is \(E = E_1 + S(\text{pH} - \text{pH}_1)\). At pH 7 this is the offset \(E_{\text{iso}}\).
  4. For a sample, solve the same line for pH: \(\text{pH}_x = \text{pH}_1 + (E_x - E_1)/S\). With three or more buffers, fit the line by least squares (Lesson 9.3) and inspect the residuals.
Assumptions and good practice

Choose buffers that bracket the samples: for nutrient solutions pH 4.01 and 7.00. Buffers have their own temperature dependence (a phosphate buffer labelled 7.00 at 25 °C has a slightly higher pH in a cold room) — use the value from the table printed on the bottle for the actual temperature. Wait for a stable reading (drift below about 0.1 mV per 10 s), rinse with deionised water between buffers and blot — do not rub — the membrane. Never pour used buffer back. As a practical guide, one manufacturer rates an electrode with slope 95–105 % and offset within ±15 mV as in good condition, 90–94 % or ±15–35 mV as needing cleaning, and below 90 % or beyond ±35 mV as faulty (Mettler-Toledo, n.d.).

\(S\): mV/pH; \(\eta\): (mV pH⁻¹)/(mV pH⁻¹) = 1 → % ✓; \(\text{pH}_x\): pH + mV/(mV pH⁻¹) = pH ✓

Reveal the reasoning

Temperature affects a pH measurement in two distinct ways, and students often confuse them. The first is the electrode effect: the Nernst slope is proportional to absolute temperature. Meters with a temperature sensor correct this automatically (ATC) by rescaling the calibrated slope. The second is the solution effect: the true pH of the sample itself changes with temperature, because dissociation equilibria (of water, phosphate, carbonate) shift. ATC does not correct this — it is real chemistry. That is why pH values should be reported together with the measurement temperature.

Automatic temperature compensation (ATC)(9.2.4)
\[ \text{pH}_x = \text{pH}_{\text{iso}} + \frac{E_{\text{iso}} - E_x}{\eta\,s(T_x)} = 7 + \frac{E_{\text{iso}} - E_x}{\eta\;0.19841\,(t_x + 273.15)} \]

the sample pH equals the isopotential pH plus the difference between the offset voltage and the sample voltage, divided by the calibrated slope efficiency times the Nernst slope evaluated at the sample temperature.

\(t_x\)sample temperature measured by the probe's thermistor or Pt1000°C
\(\eta\), \(E_{\text{iso}}\)slope efficiency and offset from the calibration (Eq. 9.2.3)–, mV
Follow the derivation
  1. Solve Eq. 9.2.2 for pH: \(\text{pH} = \text{pH}_{\text{iso}} + (E_{\text{iso}} - E)/(\eta s(T))\).
  2. The calibration provided \(\eta\) and \(E_{\text{iso}}\) at \(T_{\text{cal}}\). Assuming both are independent of temperature (the isopotential assumption), only \(s(T)\) must be re-evaluated at the sample temperature.
  3. Without ATC, the meter keeps \(s(T_{\text{cal}})\). The resulting error is \(\Delta\text{pH} = (\text{pH}_x - 7)\left(\dfrac{T_x}{T_{\text{cal}}} - 1\right)\): zero at pH 7, and proportional to the distance from pH 7 and to the temperature difference.
  4. Example: pH 4.00 at 10 °C measured with a 25 °C slope: \(\Delta\text{pH} = (4 - 7)(283.15/298.15 - 1) = +0.15\). The meter shows 4.15.
Assumptions and validity

Real electrodes have an isopotential point that is not exactly at pH 7 and 0 mV, and their offset drifts slightly with temperature; for the most accurate work, calibrate at the sample temperature. Temperature equilibrium matters too: a probe moved from a 25 °C buffer into a 12 °C tank needs time before both the glass and the temperature sensor have settled (Lesson 9.1, Eq. 9.1.2).

Care and ageing of pH electrodes

A glass electrode is a consumable. Its gel layer, its reference electrolyte and its junction all change with time and use, and the symptoms are visible in the calibration record: the slope falls, the offset wanders and the response becomes slow. Most problems of students' pH data can be traced to a few habits:

  • Keep the membrane wet in the manufacturer's storage solution (typically 3 mol L⁻¹ KCl). Storage in deionised or RO water leaches ions from the gel layer and the reference electrolyte; a dried-out electrode must be rehydrated for hours before it responds properly.
  • Keep the junction open. In nutrient solutions and aquaponic water, precipitates (calcium phosphate), biofilm and proteins clog the diaphragm; the reference potential then drifts and readings creep for minutes. Clean with the recommended cleaning solutions (e.g., dilute acid for mineral deposits, enzyme solution for proteins) and refill refillable electrodes.
  • Mind the silver. Ag⁺ from the reference electrolyte precipitates with sulfide in some wastewaters and blackens the junction; double-junction electrodes avoid this.
  • Calibrate often and log it. For the course project: calibrate at the start of every measurement session with 4.01 and 7.00 buffers, record slope and offset in your grow log, and check against a buffer at the end. The calibration record is your early warning of an ageing probe.
  • Isolate inline probes. A pH and an EC probe in the same tank can interfere: the EC excitation current flows through the solution and shifts the pH reading. Use galvanically isolated interface boards and a single ground point.

Electrical conductivity: measuring the ion load

The electrical conductivity (EC) of a nutrient solution is the grower's shorthand for its total nutrient concentration (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009). Ions carry current when an electric field is applied: each contributes in proportion to its concentration, its charge and its mobility. The conductivity \(\kappa\) (S m⁻¹; horticulture uses dS m⁻¹ = mS cm⁻¹) is an intrinsic property of the solution; the sensor measures the conductance between its electrodes and converts it with the cell constant.

Conductance, cell constant and conductivity(9.2.5)
\[ G = \frac{1}{R} = \kappa\,\frac{A}{d}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \kappa = G\,K_{\text{cell}} = \frac{K_{\text{cell}}}{R},\qquad K_{\text{cell}} = \frac{d}{A} \]

the conductance, which is the reciprocal of the measured resistance, equals the conductivity times the electrode area divided by the electrode spacing; so the conductivity equals the conductance times the cell constant, which is the spacing divided by the area.

Follow the derivation
  1. A column of solution of length \(d\) and cross-section \(A\) has resistance \(R = \rho d/A\), where the resistivity \(\rho\) is an intrinsic property — exactly as for a wire.
  2. Conductivity is the reciprocal of resistivity, \(\kappa = 1/\rho\), so \(R = d/(\kappa A)\) and \(\kappa = (d/A)/R = K_{\text{cell}}/R\).
  3. Real cells have fringing fields, so the effective \(d/A\) differs from the geometric one. The cell constant is therefore determined by calibration in a standard solution of known conductivity: \(K_{\text{cell}} = \kappa_{\text{std}}R_{\text{std}}\). A 0.01 mol L⁻¹ KCl solution (1413 µS cm⁻¹ at 25 °C) is the usual standard for nutrient-range cells.
  4. Choose \(K_{\text{cell}}\) so that the resistance is in a convenient range (roughly 100 Ω to 10 kΩ): a small cell constant for low-conductivity water, a large one for concentrated solutions.
Where does conductivity come from? Kohlrausch's law

In dilute solution each ion contributes independently: \(\kappa = \sum_i c_i\lambda_i\), where \(\lambda_i\) is the molar ionic conductivity per unit charge. At infinite dilution and 25 °C typical values (S cm² mol⁻¹) are K⁺ 73.5, NH₄⁺ 73.5, Na⁺ 50.1, ½Ca²⁺ 59.5, ½Mg²⁺ 53.0, NO₃⁻ 71.4, Cl⁻ 76.3, ½SO₄²⁻ 80.0 — and the exceptional H⁺ 349.8 and OH⁻ 198.6, which move by proton hopping. At nutrient-solution strength interionic attraction lowers the effective values, so EC is only approximately proportional to the sum of ions; the useful rule of thumb EC (dS m⁻¹) ≈ sum of cations (mmolc L⁻¹)/10 is implemented in the course library as ecFromCations (Lesson 5.2). Note what EC cannot tell you: which ions are present. A solution depleted of nitrate but enriched in sodium can have the same EC as a fresh one.

\(K_{\text{cell}}/R\): cm⁻¹/Ω = S cm⁻¹ ✓ (1 S = 1 Ω⁻¹; 1 S cm⁻¹ = 1000 mS cm⁻¹)

Measuring the resistance of an electrolyte is not as simple as measuring a resistor. A direct current would electrolyse the solution at the electrodes and charge the electrical double layers there — polarisation — which adds an extra, time-dependent resistance and ruins the reading within milliseconds. Conductivity meters therefore excite the cell with alternating current (typically hundreds of hertz to tens of kilohertz), so that no net reaction occurs. Two cell designs dominate (Figure 4).

Conductivity rises strongly with temperature — by roughly 2 % per kelvin — because the viscosity of water falls and ions move faster. A solution that reads 1.8 mS cm⁻¹ at 15 °C reads about 2.2 mS cm⁻¹ at 25 °C without any change in its composition. To compare measurements, meters report conductivity referenced to 25 °C.

Linear temperature compensation of conductivity(9.2.6)
\[ \kappa_{25} = \frac{\kappa_T}{1 + \alpha\,(T - 25\ ^{\circ}\text{C})} \]

the conductivity at the reference temperature of 25 °C equals the conductivity measured at temperature T divided by one plus the temperature coefficient times the temperature difference from 25 °C.

  1. Over a moderate range, conductivity increases approximately linearly with temperature: \(\kappa_T = \kappa_{25}[1 + \alpha(T - 25)]\), where \(\alpha = (\mathrm{d}\kappa/\mathrm{d}T)/\kappa_{25}\) is the relative slope at 25 °C.
  2. Divide both sides by the bracket to obtain \(\kappa_{25}\). The course library implements this as ec25(ecT, T, alpha = 0.019).
  3. For natural waters the relative temperature coefficient is close to 2 % per kelvin; Hayashi (2004) examines how well the linear model describes real water samples over environmental temperatures. Standards such as ISO 7888 (ISO, 1985) use a non-linear function for natural waters.
Assumptions and validity

α depends on the ionic composition: strong acids and bases (dominated by H⁺ and OH⁻) have smaller coefficients, pure water a much larger one. Using a wrong α matters more the further you measure from 25 °C: with α = 0.019 set but a true value of 0.021, a reading at 10 °C is about 4 % wrong. Some meters let you disable compensation; if you do, always log the temperature.

\(\alpha(T - 25)\): K⁻¹ × K = dimensionless, so \(\kappa_{25}\) has the unit of \(\kappa_T\) ✓

Finally, many cheap meters display "TDS" in "ppm" instead of EC. Total dissolved solids (mg L⁻¹) cannot be measured by conductivity — it is estimated by multiplying EC by a conversion factor that depends on which salts are assumed to be dissolved. Three conventions are common, and they give different numbers for the same solution.

TDS ("ppm") estimated from conductivity(9.2.7)
\[ \text{TDS}\ (\text{mg L}^{-1}) = k_e\times\kappa_{25}\ (\mu\text{S cm}^{-1}),\qquad k_e = 0.50\ (\text{"500 scale"}),\ 0.64\ (\text{"640 scale"}),\ 0.70\ (\text{"700 scale"}) \]

the estimated total dissolved solids in milligrams per litre equals a conversion factor times the conductivity at 25 °C in microsiemens per centimetre; different meters use factors of 0.5, 0.64 or 0.7.

Why the factors differ
  1. For a pure salt, TDS is the mass of salt per litre, and EC depends on how many ions that mass contains and how mobile they are. Light, mobile ions (Na⁺, Cl⁻) give more conductivity per milligram than heavy ones (SO₄²⁻ with its counter-ions, phosphates).
  2. Meter makers calibrate with a reference salt: NaCl solutions give a factor near 0.5 and KCl solutions near 0.55; the "700" scale (loosely called the KCl scale) and blends that mimic natural waters (the "442" standard of sodium sulfate, sodium bicarbonate and sodium chloride) use factors near 0.65–0.7. Standard Methods quotes 0.55–0.7 for natural waters (Lipps et al., 2023).
  3. A hydroponic nutrient solution is none of these reference solutions, so none of the displayed "ppm" values is its true dissolved-solids content.
Assumptions and advice

Report EC in mS cm⁻¹ (or dS m⁻¹) at 25 °C, never ppm. If a recipe from the internet gives "ppm", find out which scale it used before converting: 1000 ppm on the 500 scale is 2.0 mS cm⁻¹, but on the 700 scale it is only 1.43 mS cm⁻¹ — a 40 % difference in fertiliser.

mg L⁻¹ per µS cm⁻¹ × µS cm⁻¹ = mg L⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 3Three meters, one solution

A student measures a lettuce nutrient solution at 18 °C with an uncompensated conductivity probe: 1.95 mS cm⁻¹. (a) What is EC₂₅ (α = 0.019 K⁻¹)? (b) What would TDS meters on the 500, 640 and 700 scales display? (c) A recipe says "keep the solution at 1100 ppm". What does that mean in EC?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Compensate: \(\kappa_{25} = 1.95/[1 + 0.019(18 - 25)] = 1.95/0.867 = 2.249\) mS cm⁻¹ = 2249 µS cm⁻¹.
  2. TDS scales: 0.50 × 2249 = 1125 ppm; 0.64 × 2249 = 1439 ppm; 0.70 × 2249 = 1574 ppm.
  3. The recipe: 1100 ppm means 2.20 mS cm⁻¹ on the 500 scale, 1.72 on the 640 scale and 1.57 on the 700 scale. Without knowing the scale, the recipe is ambiguous by ±17 %.
  4. Sanity check: the compensated value is higher than the raw value because the solution was colder than 25 °C ✓.
EC₂₅ ≈ 2.25 mS cm⁻¹; 1125, 1439 or 1574 "ppm"; the recipe is ambiguous

Redox potential (ORP)

An ORP (oxidation–reduction potential) sensor is a potentiometric sensor with an inert platinum or gold indicator electrode instead of a glass membrane. The metal exchanges electrons with all redox couples in the solution and settles at a mixed potential described by the redox form of the Nernst equation (step 5 of the derivation of Eq. 9.2.1): oxidising conditions (dissolved oxygen, ozone, hypochlorite) give high positive potentials, reducing conditions (sulfide, anoxic sludge) low or negative ones. Readings are usually reported against the built-in Ag/AgCl reference; adding about +210 mV (Ag/AgCl in 3 mol L⁻¹ KCl at 25 °C) converts them to the standard hydrogen electrode scale, Eh.

ORP is useful as a process indicator: in disinfection systems (ozone, chlorine) it tracks the oxidising capacity of the water, and in aquaponics and recirculating aquaculture it reveals anoxic zones in biofilters and sludge where denitrification or, undesirably, sulfide formation occur. It is not a concentration measurement: because the potential is a mixture of many couples, often not at equilibrium, there is no general way to convert millivolts into mg L⁻¹ of any substance. Platinum surfaces also need time and cleaning to respond reproducibly; treat ORP trends as more reliable than absolute values.

Dissolved oxygen: Clark, galvanic and optical sensors

Oxygen is sparingly soluble. By Henry's law the equilibrium concentration is proportional to the partial pressure of O₂, and the proportionality falls with temperature and salinity: fresh water in equilibrium with air holds 11.3 mg L⁻¹ at 10 °C, 9.1 at 20 °C and 8.3 at 25 °C (Benson & Krause, 1984; computed with the course function doSaturation). A warm hydroponic tank with dense roots can fall far below saturation within hours (Lesson 5.3). Figure 6 shows the saturation curve and how altitude and salinity shift it.

Amperometric sensors: Clark and galvanic

The membrane-covered oxygen electrode grew out of Leland Clark's work on measuring oxygen in blood in the 1950s (Clark et al., 1953). A noble-metal cathode (platinum or gold) and a silver anode sit in a potassium chloride electrolyte behind a thin gas-permeable membrane (PTFE or FEP). Polarised at about −0.6 to −0.8 V relative to the anode, the cathode reduces every oxygen molecule that reaches it:

cathode: O₂ + 2 H₂O + 4 e⁻ → 4 OH⁻      anode: 4 Ag + 4 Cl⁻ → 4 AgCl + 4 e⁻

Because the cathode consumes oxygen as fast as it arrives, the O₂ partial pressure at the cathode is essentially zero, and the current is limited by diffusion through the membrane. A galvanic sensor uses a more reactive anode (zinc or lead) so that the cell drives the reduction itself, without an external polarising voltage; it needs no warm-up but its anode is consumed.

Membrane-limited current of an amperometric O₂ sensor(9.2.8)
\[ i = \frac{n\,F\,A\,P_m}{b}\;p_{\text{O}_2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad C = C_{\text{sat}}(T, S, P)\;\frac{i - i_0}{i_{\text{air}} - i_0} \]

the sensor current equals the number of electrons per O₂ molecule times the Faraday constant times the cathode area times the membrane permeability divided by the membrane thickness, multiplied by the oxygen partial pressure; in practice the concentration is found from a two-point calibration: saturation concentration times the zero-corrected current as a fraction of the zero-corrected current in air-saturated water.

\(i\)sensor currentA (nA–µA)
\(n\)electrons transferred per O₂ molecule (4)–
\(A\)cathode aream²
\(P_m\)O₂ permeability of the membranemol m⁻¹ s⁻¹ Pa⁻¹
\(b\)membrane thicknessm

\(i_0\), \(i_{\text{air}}\)currents in zero-oxygen solution and in air-saturated waterA

Follow the derivation
  1. Diffusion through the membrane (Fick's first law). The flux of O₂ across a membrane of thickness \(b\) is \(J = P_m\,\Delta p/b\), where \(P_m\) (the permeability) combines the solubility and the diffusion coefficient of O₂ in the membrane polymer.
  2. The cathode reduces oxygen instantly, so \(p = 0\) on the inside and \(\Delta p = p_{\text{O}_2}\), the partial pressure in the sample.
  3. Faraday's law. Each O₂ molecule takes 4 electrons, so the current is \(i = nFAJ = nFAP_m\,p_{\text{O}_2}/b\).
  4. The sensor therefore measures partial pressure (or % saturation). Henry's law converts it into concentration, \(C = C_{\text{sat}}\,p_{\text{O}_2}/p_{\text{O}_2,\text{sat}}\), which is where temperature, salinity and barometric pressure enter.
  5. Because \(n, F, A, P_m, b\) are not known precisely and change with time, the sensor is calibrated: in air-saturated water (or water-saturated air) it reads 100 % saturation, and optionally in a zero-oxygen solution (sodium sulfite) to find \(i_0\). The linear two-point relation follows.
Assumptions and practical consequences

The sensor consumes oxygen: the sample near the membrane is depleted unless water flows past it, so Clark and galvanic sensors need a minimum flow (or stirring), and read low in still water. The membrane permeability increases strongly with temperature (several percent per kelvin), so sensors contain a thermistor for compensation. The electrolyte and anode are consumed (the silver anode is gradually coated with AgCl), so membranes and electrolyte must be replaced periodically. A polarographic sensor also needs a warm-up after switching on while the cathode polarises.

\(nFAP_m p/b\): 1 × C mol⁻¹ × m² × mol m⁻¹ s⁻¹ Pa⁻¹ × Pa / m = C s⁻¹ = A ✓

Optical sensors: luminescence quenching

In an optical ("luminescent" or "LDO") sensor, a luminophore — typically a ruthenium(II) complex or a platinum or palladium porphyrin — is immobilised in a thin polymer film on the sensor cap (Wang & Wolfbeis, 2014). Blue or green light excites the dye; it relaxes by emitting red light, with an excited-state lifetime of microseconds. When an oxygen molecule collides with an excited dye molecule, the energy is transferred to the oxygen without light emission: the luminescence is quenched. More oxygen means dimmer, shorter-lived luminescence.

Stern–Volmer equation(9.2.9)
\[ \frac{I_0}{I} = \frac{\tau_0}{\tau} = 1 + K_{SV}\,[\text{O}_2]\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad [\text{O}_2] = \frac{1}{K_{SV}}\left(\frac{I_0}{I} - 1\right) \]

the ratio of the luminescence intensity without oxygen to that with oxygen — equal to the ratio of the lifetimes — rises linearly with the oxygen concentration, with slope equal to the Stern–Volmer constant; so the oxygen concentration is the ratio minus one, divided by that constant.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(I_0, \tau_0\)luminescence intensity and lifetime without oxygencounts, µs
\(I, \tau\)intensity and lifetime in the samplecounts, µs
\(K_{SV}\)Stern–Volmer constant \(= k_q\tau_0\)L mg⁻¹ (or per % saturation)
\(k_q\)bimolecular quenching rate constantL mg⁻¹ s⁻¹
\([\text{O}_2]\)oxygen concentration (or partial pressure)mg L⁻¹
Follow the derivation
  1. An excited dye molecule D* returns to the ground state either by itself (emitting light or heat) with rate constant \(k_0 = 1/\tau_0\), or by collision with O₂ with rate \(k_q[\text{O}_2]\).
  2. Under constant illumination the excited population is at steady state: excitation rate \(=\) \((k_0 + k_q[\text{O}_2])[\text{D}^*]\), so \([\text{D}^*] \propto 1/(k_0 + k_q[\text{O}_2])\).
  3. The emitted intensity is proportional to \([\text{D}^*]\). Taking the ratio without and with oxygen: \(\dfrac{I_0}{I} = \dfrac{k_0 + k_q[\text{O}_2]}{k_0} = 1 + k_q\tau_0[\text{O}_2]\).
  4. The lifetime is the reciprocal of the total decay rate, \(\tau = 1/(k_0 + k_q[\text{O}_2])\), so \(\tau_0/\tau\) obeys the same law. Commercial sensors measure the lifetime (as the phase shift of sinusoidally modulated luminescence), which is insensitive to dye bleaching and light-intensity drift.
Assumptions and validity

In real polymer films the dye molecules sit in different micro-environments, and Stern–Volmer plots curve downwards; manufacturers use a "two-site" model or empirical curves. \(K_{SV}\) depends on temperature (diffusion of O₂ in the film), so temperature compensation is essential. Advantages over Clark cells: no oxygen consumption (no flow dependence), no electrolyte or polarisation, and long calibration stability; the sensing cap is a consumable that must be replaced periodically.

\(K_{SV}[\text{O}_2]\): L mg⁻¹ × mg L⁻¹ = dimensionless ✓
Worked example 4Calibrating an optical DO sensor

An optical DO sensor gives 1000 counts in a zero-oxygen sodium sulfite solution and 400 counts in air-saturated fresh water at 20 °C and sea-level pressure. (a) Find \(K_{SV}\). (b) In a deep-water-culture tank at 20 °C the sensor reads 520 counts. What are the DO concentration and the % saturation? (c) Is the root zone well oxygenated?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Saturation value: doSaturation(20) = 9.09 mg L⁻¹ (Benson & Krause, 1984).
  2. KSV: \(K_{SV} = (I_0/I - 1)/[\text{O}_2] = (1000/400 - 1)/9.09 = 1.5/9.09 = 0.165\) L mg⁻¹.
  3. Sample: \([\text{O}_2] = (1000/520 - 1)/0.165 = 0.923/0.165 = 5.59\) mg L⁻¹.
  4. % saturation: 5.59/9.09 = 61.5 %.
  5. Interpretation: the solution holds about 60 % of what it could; roots are not yet anoxic, but in a warm, densely rooted DWC system this is a warning sign: increase aeration and check the trend (Lesson 5.3).
KSV ≈ 0.165 L mg⁻¹; DO ≈ 5.6 mg L⁻¹ ≈ 62 % saturation

Ion-selective electrodes for nitrate and potassium

If glass can be made selective for H⁺, why not build membranes selective for NO₃⁻, K⁺ or Ca²⁺ and measure every nutrient continuously? This is the promise of ion-selective electrodes (ISEs). Most use a plasticised PVC membrane containing an ionophore — a molecule that binds one ion selectively (valinomycin for K⁺, for example) — or an ion exchanger (quaternary ammonium salts for NO₃⁻) (Bakker et al., 1997). They obey the Nernst equation, but their selectivity is finite, and that is where the difficulties begin.

Nikolsky–Eisenman equation(9.2.10)
\[ E = E^{0} + \frac{R\,T}{z_i F}\,\ln\!\Big(a_i + \sum_{j\neq i} K^{\text{pot}}_{ij}\,a_j^{\,z_i/z_j}\Big) \]

the electrode potential follows the Nernst equation, but the activity inside the logarithm is the activity of the target ion plus, for every interfering ion, its activity (raised to the ratio of the charges) weighted by a potentiometric selectivity coefficient.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(a_i\), \(z_i\)activity and charge of the primary (target) ion–
\(a_j\), \(z_j\)activity and charge of interfering ion \(j\)–
\(K^{\text{pot}}_{ij}\)potentiometric selectivity coefficient (smaller is better; often quoted as \(\log K\))–
Follow the reasoning
  1. An ideal membrane responds only to ion \(i\) (Eq. 9.2.1). A real membrane also exchanges other ions, less readily.
  2. The empirical Nikolsky extension adds each interferent to the target activity, weighted by \(K_{ij}\) — the ratio of activities of \(i\) and \(j\) that give the same potential. \(K_{ij} = 10^{-3}\) means the electrode is 1000 times more sensitive to \(i\) than to \(j\).
  3. The exponent \(z_i/z_j\) makes the equation dimensionally consistent when the charges differ (e.g., Ca²⁺ interfering on a K⁺ electrode).
  4. The relative error caused by interference is approximately \(K_{ij}a_j/a_i\) (for equal charges). Selectivity coefficients are measured and reported following IUPAC recommendations (Umezawa et al., 2000).
Why ISEs are hard to use in nutrient solutions

Interference: nitrate electrodes respond to other lipophilic anions (Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻, NO₂⁻), K⁺ electrodes to NH₄⁺. Logarithmic resolution: a 1 mV error is a 4 % concentration error for a monovalent ion and 8 % for a divalent ion (slope 29.6 mV per decade). Drift: PVC membranes lose plasticiser and ionophore and their potentials drift, so frequent recalibration is needed. Activity: they measure activity, so the ionic strength of standards and samples must match (ionic-strength adjusters). Automated systems with ISE arrays and frequent two-point recalibration have been demonstrated for hydroponic macronutrients (Kim et al., 2013), but most commercial nutrient management is still based on EC and pH plus periodic laboratory analysis (Sonneveld & Voogt, 2009).

\(K_{ij}\,a_j^{z_i/z_j}\) is dimensionless like \(a_i\); \(RT/(z_iF)\) is in V ✓
Worked example 5Can a nitrate electrode be trusted in a recirculating system?

Suppose a nitrate ISE has a chloride selectivity coefficient \(K_{\text{NO}_3,\text{Cl}} = 0.005\) (equal charges). (a) A fresh nutrient solution contains 12 mmol L⁻¹ NO₃⁻ and 1 mmol L⁻¹ Cl⁻. (b) After weeks of recirculation with chloride-rich tap water and plant uptake of nitrate, the solution contains 2 mmol L⁻¹ NO₃⁻ and 8 mmol L⁻¹ Cl⁻. Estimate the interference error in each case. (c) If, in addition, the electrode has drifted by 2 mV since calibration, what concentration error does that add?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Apparent activity \(= a_{\text{NO}_3} + K a_{\text{Cl}}\): the relative error is \(0.005\times1/12 = 0.04\,\%\) — negligible. (Activity coefficients are the same for both monovalent ions and cancel in the ratio.)
  2. (b) \(0.005\times8/2 = 2.0\,\%\) — noticeable, and growing exactly when the nitrate is running out, which is when you most need the reading.
  3. (c) A 2 mV drift on a 59.16 mV per decade slope is \(10^{2/59.16} = 1.081\): an 8 % error, larger than the interference.
  4. Conclusion: with low selectivity coefficients, interference is manageable; drift and calibration are the real limitations. Frequent automatic recalibration (as in Kim et al., 2013) is essential.
(a) ≈ 0.04 %; (b) ≈ 2 %; (c) a 2 mV drift alone gives ≈ 8 %
Frontier — toward nutrient-specific sensing

Solid-contact ISEs (without an inner solution), sensor arrays combined with machine-learning calibration ("electronic tongues"), optical nitrate sensing by UV absorbance, and inline ion chromatography are all being developed to close the loop between measured nutrient concentrations and automatic dosing. The engineering challenge is less the first measurement than the thousandth: drift, fouling and recalibration in a warm, biologically active nutrient solution.

Common misconception

"EC tells me how much nitrogen is in my solution." EC measures the total ionic strength weighted by mobility. In recirculating systems, ions that plants take up slowly (Na⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻) accumulate while nitrate and potassium are depleted — the EC can stay constant while the nutrient balance deteriorates. EC is a necessary but not a sufficient measurement.

Key terms

Nernst equation
Relation between an electrode potential and the logarithm of an ion's activity; slope \(\ln(10)RT/(zF)\), 59.16 mV per decade at 25 °C for z = 1.
Activity
The thermodynamically effective concentration of an ion, \(a = \gamma c/c^0\); what potentiometric sensors actually respond to.
Combination electrode
A pH (or ISE) sensing half-cell and a reference half-cell built into one body.
Reference electrode
A half-cell with a stable potential, usually Ag/AgCl in KCl, connected to the sample through a liquid junction.
Asymmetry potential / offset
The voltage of a real pH electrode at pH 7 (ideally 0 mV); grows as the electrode ages.
Slope efficiency
Practical electrode slope as a percentage of the theoretical Nernst slope; a key indicator of electrode health.
Isopotential point
The pH at which the electrode voltage is independent of temperature (≈ pH 7); the pivot for temperature compensation.
ATC
Automatic temperature compensation: rescaling the Nernst slope to the measured sample temperature. It does not correct the true temperature dependence of the sample's pH.
Cell constant
\(K = d/A\) of a conductivity cell, determined with a standard solution; converts conductance to conductivity.
EC₂₅
Electrical conductivity referenced to 25 °C with a temperature coefficient α ≈ 0.019 K⁻¹.
TDS scale
A convention (factor 0.5, 0.64 or 0.7) for estimating dissolved solids from EC; different scales give different "ppm" for the same solution.
Clark electrode
Membrane-covered amperometric oxygen sensor whose current is proportional to O₂ partial pressure; consumes oxygen.
Stern–Volmer equation
\(I_0/I = \tau_0/\tau = 1 + K_{SV}[\text{O}_2]\): the basis of optical oxygen sensing by luminescence quenching.
Selectivity coefficient
\(K^{\text{pot}}_{ij}\) in the Nikolsky–Eisenman equation: how strongly an interfering ion \(j\) affects an electrode for ion \(i\).
