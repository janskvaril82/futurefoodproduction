The aquaponic ecosystem and the nitrogen cycle

Feed a fish and you have fed a whole ecosystem. The protein in a pellet becomes fish flesh, ammonia on the gills, nitrite and nitrate in a biofilm of invisible bacteria, and finally leaves and roots in a floating raft of lettuce. This lesson follows a single nitrogen atom through that journey — with the chemistry balanced, the oxygen and alkalinity costs counted, the bacteria's growth kinetics quantified, and the whole system written as coupled mass balances that an engineer can design with.

  • Size a biofilm surface from TAN production, and formulate the fish–bacteria–plant system as coupled nitrogen mass balances to compute steady-state nitrate and nitrogen use efficiency (LO3, LO7).

One loop, three kingdoms

Aquaponics combines a recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) with soilless plant production (hydroponics) so that the water — and the nutrients dissolved in it — are shared. Rakocy, Masser and Losordo (2006) define aquaponic systems simply as "recirculating aquaculture systems that incorporate the production of plants without soil". Palm et al. (2018) proposed a stricter definition that is now widely used in Europe: a system counts as aquaponics when the majority (more than 50 %) of the nutrients used by the plants derive from waste of the fish, whether uneaten feed, faeces or soluble excretions (see also Eck, Körner & Jijakli, 2019).

Three groups of organisms do the work. Fish convert feed into biomass and excrete waste nitrogen, mostly as ammonia across the gills. Bacteria — nitrifiers in biofilms on every wetted surface, plus heterotrophs that decompose organic matter — oxidise that ammonia to nitrate and release nutrients from solids. Plants take up nitrate and other ions and turn them into a second harvest. In a recirculating fish farm, nitrate and phosphate are wastes that must be diluted by water exchange or removed; in aquaponics they are fertiliser. That is the central promise: two crops from one input (fish feed), with lower water use and lower nutrient discharge (Goddek et al., 2015).

Figure 1 already contains every theme of this module. The feed arrow at the top is the driver: everything else — ammonia production, oxygen demand, acid production, solids, plant nutrients — scales with the mass of feed added per day. The sequence of components follows the chemistry: solids must be removed before they clog the biofilter and plant roots; ammonia must be oxidised before it poisons the fish; the plants sit where the water is richest in nitrate and poorest in particles. Lesson 6.2 turns this picture into a design procedure; Lesson 6.3 looks at the water from the fish's point of view.

Key idea

An aquaponic system is an engineered ecosystem driven by feed. Almost every design quantity — nitrifier surface, oxygen supply, base dosing, plant area, sludge handling — is proportional to the daily feed input. If you know how much feed goes in, and what happens to its nitrogen, you can size the whole system.

From rice fields to raft tanks: a short history

Integrating fish with plants is old; recirculating aquaponics is young. Separating the two is important if we are to understand what modern systems can and cannot do.

c. 1400–1150 BCE

Pond culture of common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is thought to have begun in China at the end of the Shang dynasty (Halwart & Gupta, 2004).

25–220 CE

Clay models of rice fields with figurines of common carp, crucian carp and grass carp date to the later Han dynasty; archaeological and written records trace rice–fish culture in China over 1,700 years (Halwart & Gupta, 2004).

c. 900 CE

Liu Xun describes grass carp fingerlings released into flooded fields that eat the weeds: "This method not only fertilizes the fields, but produces fish as well" (quoted in Halwart & Gupta, 2004).

pre-1521 CE

Chinampas — raised planting beds built in the shallow lakes of the Basin of Mexico — sustain intensive agriculture for Aztec-period cities (Morehart, 2016).

1969

The New Alchemy Institute is founded on Cape Cod, Massachusetts, by John Todd, Nancy Jack Todd and William McLarney; its solar "Arks" combine fish tanks (notably tilapia) with greenhouse crops (Todd, 1977).

1970s–1980s

North Carolina State University develops integrated fish–vegetable systems; water consumption is only about 5 % of that of pond culture for tilapia (Somerville et al., 2014; McMurtry, 1990).

early 1980s →

James Rakocy and colleagues start the University of the Virgin Islands (UVI) raft system, the progenitor of most modern commercial aquaponics (Lennard & Goddek, 2019).

2014–2018

COST Action FA1305 "The EU Aquaponics Hub" builds a European research network (chair Benz Kotzen), leading to the open-access book Aquaponics Food Production Systems (Goddek et al., 2019).

2015

Complete ammonia oxidisers (comammox) are discovered in the genus Nitrospira, overturning a century-old two-step paradigm of nitrification (Daims et al., 2015; van Kessel et al., 2015).

History — how "ancient" is aquaponics?

Popular accounts often call chinampas and rice–fish paddies "ancient aquaponics". Lennard and Goddek (2019) urge caution: historically, fish in rice fields were mostly wild fish that entered with the flood water and lived at densities far too low to fertilise the crop substantially, and deliberate stocking was not widespread until relatively recently; the fertility of chinampas probably came from nutrient-rich lake sediments rather than from an integrated fish unit. What these systems do share with modern aquaponics is the idea of nutrient cycling between an aquatic animal compartment and a plant compartment. Modern aquaponics — high fish densities, nitrifying biofilters, soilless plants and recirculation — dates from the 1970s.

The UVI system deserves a closer look because its numbers still anchor design rules today (Table 1). It was developed in St Croix, where fresh water is scarce and groundwater too salty, so it runs on collected rainwater (Rakocy et al., 2006). Its design philosophy is instructive: remove all solids early, let the large raft area double as the biofilter, and supplement the three nutrients that fish feed supplies poorly — potassium, calcium and iron.

Fish: species, protein and ammonia excretion

Most commercial aquaponic farms raise tilapia: in an international survey of 257 aquaponic operations, 69 % kept tilapia, followed by ornamental fish (43 %), catfish (25 %), perch (16 %), bluegill (15 %) and trout (10 %) (Love et al., 2015a). From feed protein to nitrogen

Fish feed is essentially a nitrogen fertiliser wrapped in a meal for the fish. A conventional pellet contains roughly 6–8 % nitrogen, 1.2 % phosphorus and 40–45 % organic carbon; herbivorous and omnivorous fish get feeds with about 25–35 % crude protein, carnivores up to about 55 % (Eck et al., 2019). "Crude protein" is itself calculated from a nitrogen analysis: food chemists measure total nitrogen (Kjeldahl or Dumas methods) and multiply by 6.25, because the average protein is about 16 % nitrogen by mass. Mariotti, Tomé and Mirand (2008) show that the appropriate factor differs between foods and is often below 6.25, but because crude protein is defined with the 6.25 factor, dividing by 6.25 recovers the measured nitrogen exactly.

Nitrogen input with feed(6.1.1)
\[ \dot N_{\text{feed}} = F\cdot PC\cdot 0.16 = \frac{F\cdot PC}{6.25} \]

the mass of nitrogen entering the system per day equals the mass of feed per day, times the protein fraction of the feed, times 0.16 grams of nitrogen per gram of protein.

Uneaten feed still brings its nitrogen into the system — it simply reaches the water by decomposition instead of via the fish.

What the fish does with nitrogen

Inside the fish, dietary protein is digested to amino acids. Part of the absorbed amino acids is built into new body protein (growth); the rest is deaminated and its carbon skeleton used for energy. Fish are ammonotelic: they get rid of the surplus amino nitrogen mainly as ammonia across the gills, with a smaller fraction as urea (Masser, Rakocy & Losordo, 1999; Randall & Tsui, 2002). Excretion is not uniform in time: the ammonia output rises for several hours after each meal, which is why TAN and oxygen demand peak after feeding. Undigested protein leaves with the faeces and reaches the water only after bacteria have decomposed (mineralised) it.

Following Figure 2 step by step turns the biology into an engineering equation. The nitrogen that reaches the water as dissolved ammonia is the product of the feed nitrogen and three fractions: absorbed, excreted, and excreted as TAN. Chemists call the sum of the two ammonia species, NH₃ + NH₄⁺, total ammonia nitrogen (TAN), always expressed as mass of nitrogen.

TAN production rate of a fish stock(6.1.2)
\[ P_{\text{TAN}} = F\cdot PC\cdot 0.16\cdot f_{\text{abs}}\, f_{\text{exc}}\, f_{\text{TAN}} \;\approx\; 0.092\,F\cdot PC \]

the daily production of total ammonia nitrogen equals the feed nitrogen multiplied by the fraction absorbed in the gut, the fraction of absorbed nitrogen that is excreted rather than retained, and the fraction of excreted nitrogen that is ammonia; with the standard design fractions this is 0.092 times the feed rate times the protein fraction.

Assumptions and validity

A daily average: instantaneous excretion peaks a few hours after feeding. Urea and faecal N are not "lost": they are converted to ammonia by bacteria (ureolysis, mineralisation), so in a system with solids retained, the TAN load on the biofilter is higher than Eq. 6.1.2 predicts. Retention in growth depends on species, fish size, temperature and diet quality — the 0.092 factor is a conservative design value, not a physiological constant.

Nitrification: balancing the chemistry

Ammonia is removed in aquaponics by nitrification, the aerobic oxidation of ammonium to nitrate by chemolithoautotrophic microorganisms: they gain energy from oxidising an inorganic nitrogen compound ("litho") and build their cells from CO₂ ("autotrophs"). For about a century nitrification was taught as a strict division of labour. Ammonia-oxidising bacteria (AOB) — Nitrosomonas, Nitrosospira and relatives — oxidise ammonia to nitrite; nitrite-oxidising bacteria (NOB) — Nitrobacter, Nitrospira, Nitrococcus, Nitrospina — oxidise nitrite to nitrate. Ammonia-oxidising archaea (AOA) were added to the picture in the 2000s. All of these groups occur in aquaculture and aquaponic biofilters (Wongkiew et al., 2017; Eck et al., 2019).

The key to the stoichiometry is the oxidation state of nitrogen, which climbs from −3 in ammonium to +3 in nitrite and +5 in nitrate. Each unit of oxidation state is one electron removed; the electrons end up on oxygen, which is reduced to water. Figure 3 shows the "electron ladder".

Balancing a redox reaction is a bookkeeping exercise in four currencies: nitrogen atoms, electrons, charge and hydrogen/oxygen. The half-reaction method makes each step transparent.

Follow the derivation (half-reaction method)
  1. Oxidation half-reaction. N goes from −3 to +3: six electrons are released. Balance O with water and H with H⁺: \(\text{NH}_4^+ + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{NO}_2^- + 8\,\text{H}^+ + 6\,e^-\). Check charge: left +1; right −1 + 8 − 6 = +1 ✓.
  2. Reduction half-reaction. Each O₂ accepts four electrons: \(\text{O}_2 + 4\,\text{H}^+ + 4\,e^- \rightarrow 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\). To accept six electrons we need 1.5 O₂: \(1.5\,\text{O}_2 + 6\,\text{H}^+ + 6\,e^- \rightarrow 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\).
  3. Add and cancel. Electrons cancel (6 = 6); H⁺: 8 − 6 = 2 remain on the right; water: 3 − 2 = 1 remains on the right. Result: \(\text{NH}_4^+ + 1.5\,\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{NO}_2^- + 2\,\text{H}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O}\).
  4. Final check. N 1 = 1; H 4 = 2 + 2; O 3 = 2 + 1; charge +1 = −1 + 2 ✓.

Common misconception

"The plants remove the ammonia." Plants can take up ammonium directly, and roots do host nitrifiers, but in almost all aquaponic systems the bulk of the ammonia is oxidised by bacteria first — using oxygen and destroying alkalinity — and the plants harvest the resulting nitrate. A system with plants but no functioning nitrifiers still poisons its fish.

Frontier — comammox and the end of a paradigm

In 2015 two teams independently showed that some Nitrospira bacteria carry the genes for both ammonia and nitrite oxidation and convert ammonia to nitrate on their own — complete ammonia oxidation (Daims et al., 2015; van Kessel et al., 2015). The first pure culture, Nitrospira inopinata, turned out to have a very high affinity for ammonia, a low maximum oxidation rate and a high growth yield — a lifestyle adapted to low, fluctuating substrate levels (Kits et al., 2017). That is exactly the situation in a well-run biofilter, where TAN is kept below 1 mg L⁻¹. Indeed, a freshwater RAS biofilter studied over a production cycle hosted a stable nitrifying community of ammonia-oxidising archaea and comammox Nitrospira rather than classical Nitrosomonas (Bartelme, McLellan & Newton, 2017). Textbook statements that "Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter do the work in aquaponics" are therefore an oversimplification.

Growth kinetics of nitrifiers and life in a biofilm

Stoichiometry tells us how much; kinetics tells us how fast. Nitrifiers are notoriously slow growers, because oxidising ammonia yields little energy and fixing CO₂ is expensive. Their growth rate follows the saturation curve introduced by Jacques Monod for bacterial cultures: proportional to substrate at low concentration, levelling off at a maximum when the enzymes are saturated. In a biofilter two substrates can limit growth at once — ammonium (the electron donor) and oxygen (the acceptor) — so the double-Monod form is used.

Dual-substrate Monod kinetics of nitrifiers(6.1.8)
\[ \mu = \mu_{\max}\,\theta^{\,T-20}\,\frac{S}{K_S+S}\,\frac{C_{\text{O}_2}}{K_O+C_{\text{O}_2}} - b, \qquad r_S = \frac{\mu + b}{Y}\,X \]

the net specific growth rate of the nitrifiers equals their maximum rate, corrected for temperature, times a saturation factor for ammonium and another for oxygen, minus a decay rate; the rate at which they consume ammonium is their gross growth rate divided by their yield, times their biomass.

Follow the derivation and interpretation
  1. Monod's empirical law for one limiting substrate is \(\mu = \mu_{\max}S/(K_S+S)\): at \(S = K_S\) the cells grow at half their maximum rate.
  2. When a second resource can limit, the two saturation terms are multiplied (an "interactive" model): if either TAN or O₂ is scarce, growth slows.
  3. Temperature enters through \(\theta^{T-20}\): with \(\theta = 1.07\), the rate doubles for every \(\ln2/\ln1.07 \approx 10\) °C, the familiar \(Q_{10}\approx2\).
  4. Cells also die and respire their own reserves at rate \(b\); the net growth rate is \(\mu\). Substrate is consumed in proportion to gross growth: \(r_S = (\mu+b)X/Y\).
  5. Doubling time at maximum rate: \(t_d = \ln2/\mu\). With \(\mu \approx 0.8\) d⁻¹ at 20 °C, \(t_d \approx\) 0.9 d; at 12 °C about 1.5 d — compared with 20–60 minutes for fast heterotrophs.
Assumptions and validity

The parameter values are illustrative orders of magnitude of the kind tabulated in environmental-biotechnology texts (Rittmann & McCarty, 2020); real values vary widely between organisms, temperatures and pH (Chen, Ling & Blancheton, 2006). Comammox Nitrospira have a much smaller apparent half-saturation constant for ammonia and a lower maximum rate than canonical AOB (Kits et al., 2017). In biofilms the concentrations felt by cells are lower than in the bulk water because of diffusion (Figure 5).

d⁻¹ × (mg L⁻¹)/(mg L⁻¹) × (mg L⁻¹)/(mg L⁻¹) − d⁻¹ = d⁻¹ ✓; rS: d⁻¹ × mg L⁻¹ / (g g⁻¹) = mg N L⁻¹ d⁻¹ ✓

Slow growth has a very practical consequence. A brand-new system has almost no nitrifiers, so ammonia accumulates until the ammonia oxidisers have multiplied; only then does nitrite appear — and nitrite accumulates until the nitrite oxidisers, which could not grow earlier for lack of substrate, catch up. The result is the characteristic sequence of an ammonia peak followed by a nitrite peak, well known from biofilter start-ups (Masser et al., 1999; DeLong & Losordo, 2012). Stocking fish before the nitrite peak has passed is the classic cause of "new tank syndrome". Explore it in Figure 4.

Where the bacteria live: biofilms and specific surface area

Nitrifiers do not float freely in the water; they grow as biofilms — layers of cells embedded in extracellular polymers — on every wetted surface: filter media, tank walls, pipes, the undersides of rafts and plant roots. A biofilm is a diffusion-limited reactor. Ammonium and oxygen must diffuse from the bulk water through a stagnant boundary layer into the film, and they are consumed as they go, so the deepest cells may be starved of oxygen. Heterotrophic bacteria, which grow several times faster than nitrifiers when organic carbon is available, colonise the outer layer and compete for oxygen and space — the reason why solids and dissolved organic matter must be kept out of biofilters (Eck et al., 2019).

Because the active biomass sits on surfaces, biofilter capacity scales with area. Designers express it as an areal TAN removal rate (g TAN m⁻² d⁻¹) and choose media with a large specific surface area (SSA, m² of surface per m³ of bulk media volume). Somerville et al. (2014) give about 300–400 m² m⁻³ for volcanic tuff gravel, 200–300 for pumice, 250–300 for expanded clay (LECA) and 500–700 for plastic "bio-balls"; moving-bed carriers of the Kaldnes K1 type offer a protected area of the order of 500 m² m⁻³ (Rusten et al., 2006). Achievable areal rates depend strongly on temperature, TAN and oxygen concentration and on organic loading (Chen et al., 2006); design values for warm-water systems are commonly of the order of a few tenths of a gram up to about 1 g TAN m⁻² d⁻¹, lower in cold water.

the biofilm area needed equals the daily TAN production divided by the rate at which each square metre of biofilm removes TAN; the volume of media equals that area divided by the specific surface area of the media.

Plants: the nitrogen sink

Plants absorb nitrogen as nitrate (NO₃⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺). Nitrate dominates in aquaponics simply because the nitrifiers convert most ammonium before the roots can take it. The form matters for pH: when a root takes up an anion such as nitrate, it releases OH⁻ or HCO₃⁻ to stay electrically neutral, so nitrate uptake raises the pH of the water — partially offsetting the acidity of nitrification (Goddek et al., 2015). Uptake of ammonium does the opposite. Delaide et al. (2016) note that at pH 6.8 nitrate and ammonium are absorbed about equally, that nitrate is preferred under acid and ammonium under alkaline conditions, and that small ammonium fractions (5–25 % of total N) can enhance nitrate uptake. Plant roots are also habitat: tomato roots, with their large surface, supported about four times more nitrifiers than pak choi roots in the experiments of Hu et al. (2015).

The ecosystem as coupled mass balances

An engineer describes an aquaponic system as a set of well-mixed compartments (pools) connected by flows. For nitrogen the pools are the fish, particulate organic N (faeces, uneaten feed, sludge), TAN, nitrite, nitrate and plants; the flows are excretion, mineralisation, the two nitrification steps, uptake, denitrification, sludge removal and water exchange (Figure 6). Writing "rate of change = inputs − outputs" for each dissolved pool gives a system of coupled differential equations — the same structure used in the aquaponics simulator and in published aquaponics models (Keesman et al., 2019).

Coupled nitrogen balances of the water(6.1.11)
\[ \begin{aligned} V\frac{dC_{\text{TAN}}}{dt} &= P_{\text{TAN}} + r_{\text{min}} - r_{\text{AOB}} - r_{\text{up,NH}_4} - Q_{\text{ex}}C_{\text{TAN}}\\ V\frac{dC_{\text{NO}_2}}{dt} &= r_{\text{AOB}} - r_{\text{NOB}} - Q_{\text{ex}}C_{\text{NO}_2}\\ V\frac{dC_{\text{NO}_3}}{dt} &= r_{\text{NOB}} - r_{\text{up,NO}_3} - r_{\text{den}} - Q_{\text{ex}}C_{\text{NO}_3} \end{aligned} \]

for each dissolved nitrogen form, the system volume times the rate of change of its concentration equals everything that produces it minus everything that consumes it, including the amount flushed out with exchanged water.

Following the nitrogen: a Sankey diagram

How efficiently is feed nitrogen turned into food? The nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) of an aquaponic system is the fraction of feed nitrogen recovered in harvested fish and plants. Measured values are sobering: Hu et al. (2015) found 41.3 % in tomato-based and 34.4 % in pak-choi-based systems, with 1.5–1.9 % of the nitrogen input emitted as the greenhouse gas N₂O; Zou et al. (2016) found 50.9 % at pH 6.0, 47.3 % at pH 7.5 and 44.7 % at pH 9.0. Denitrification can remove 25–60 % of the nitrogen (Eck et al., 2019, citing Hu et al., 2015, and Zou et al., 2016). Wongkiew et al. (2017) review the pathways and ways to improve NUE.

\[ \text{NUE} = \frac{N_{\text{fish}} + N_{\text{plants}}}{N_{\text{feed}}}\times100\,\% \]

the nitrogen use efficiency is the nitrogen harvested in fish and plants as a percentage of the nitrogen supplied with the feed over the same period.

Sustainability link

The same nitrogen can be counted as a resource or as pollution. In a flow-through fish farm, the dissolved N in Figure 7 would be discharged to a lake or the Baltic Sea and contribute to eutrophication. Aquaponics converts part of it into vegetables — but the gaseous losses include N₂O, a greenhouse gas about 270 times more potent than CO₂ over 100 years. A full sustainability assessment therefore needs the whole balance, not just "plants eat fish waste" (see Lesson 1.3).

Key terms

Aquaponics
Integrated production of fish in recirculating aquaculture and plants in soilless culture, in which the majority of plant nutrients derive from fish waste.
Total ammonia nitrogen (TAN)
The sum of un-ionised ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium (NH₄⁺), expressed as mass of nitrogen.
Nitrification
Aerobic, two-step oxidation of ammonium to nitrite and then nitrate by chemolithoautotrophic microorganisms; consumes oxygen and alkalinity.
Ammonia-oxidising bacteria (AOB)
Bacteria such as Nitrosomonas and Nitrosospira that oxidise ammonia to nitrite (step 1).
Nitrite-oxidising bacteria (NOB)
Bacteria such as Nitrobacter and Nitrospira that oxidise nitrite to nitrate (step 2).
Comammox
Complete ammonia oxidation to nitrate by a single organism; discovered in 2015 in Nitrospira.
Alkalinity
The acid-neutralising capacity of water, usually expressed as mg L⁻¹ CaCO₃; mostly bicarbonate at pH 6–8.
Biofilm
A layer of microorganisms embedded in extracellular polymers on a surface; the home of most nitrifiers in aquaponics.
Specific surface area (SSA)
Surface area of filter media per unit bulk volume (m² m⁻³); determines how much biofilm a biofilter volume can hold.
Monod kinetics
Saturating relation between a microbial growth rate and a limiting substrate, \(\mu = \mu_{\max}S/(K_S+S)\).
Cycling (biofilter start-up)
The weeks-long establishment of nitrifier populations, marked by an ammonia peak followed by a nitrite peak.
Mineralisation
Microbial breakdown of organic matter (faeces, feed, sludge) that releases inorganic nutrients such as ammonium and phosphate.
Denitrification
Anoxic, mostly heterotrophic reduction of nitrate to N₂ (and partly N₂O); a nitrogen loss from aquaponic systems.
Nitrogen use efficiency (NUE)
Fraction of feed nitrogen recovered in harvested fish and plants.
