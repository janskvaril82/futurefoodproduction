Spectral quality and photomorphogenesis

A plant does not only count photons — it reads them. Long before a seedling can photosynthesise, it uses light to decide when to germinate, how tall to grow, which way to bend, when to open its stomata, whether to invest in sunscreen pigments and when to flower. A handful of photoreceptors translate the colour of light into these decisions. For a grower with multi-channel LEDs, spectrum has therefore become a design variable: add far-red and lettuce leaves expand, add blue and seedlings stay compact, add UV and red lettuce blushes. This lesson gives you the physics and biology behind these effects, the quantitative tools to predict them — the phytochrome photostationary state, the red:far-red ratio, yield photon flux and extended PAR — and the critical sense to judge the many "light recipes" on the market.

Light as a signal, not only as energy

In Lesson 4.1 light was fuel: every photon in the 400–700 nm band counted equally, because each can drive one electron through photosynthesis. But plants also use light as information. A buried seed must know whether it is near the soil surface; a seedling must know whether it is shaded by a neighbour; a leaf must know whether the sun is strong enough to damage it; a lettuce plant must know whether the days are getting longer. For these decisions plants use photoreceptors — pigment–protein complexes present in tiny amounts, which do not harvest energy but act as molecular switches that change gene expression, ion transport and growth. The developmental responses they control are called photomorphogenesis.

Two features distinguish signalling from energy capture. First, sensitivity: some phytochrome responses are triggered by total photon doses below 10⁻³ µmol m⁻² — a millisecond of dim light — whereas photosynthesis needs hundreds of µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for hours. That is why the photoperiodic night breaks of Lesson 4.2 need only a few µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Second, spectral selectivity: each photoreceptor absorbs a characteristic waveband, so the ratio between wavebands carries information — for instance, how much red light has already been absorbed by leaves above. Plant scientists express this selectivity with action spectra (response per incident photon versus wavelength). By the Grotthuss–Draper principle — only absorbed light can drive a photochemical change — an action spectrum resembles the absorption spectrum of the pigment responsible, which is how photoreceptors were first identified.

Every spectral metric in this lesson is built the same way: multiply the photon spectrum by a weighting function and add up.

The photoreceptor families

Four families of photoreceptors — plus a few smaller ones — cover the spectrum from UV-B to far-red (Figure 2, Table 1). They share a design principle: a protein holds a light-absorbing chromophore; absorbing a photon changes the chromophore's shape or chemistry, which changes the protein's shape, which changes what it binds to. Most of the resulting signals converge on a few hubs: the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex COP1–SPA, which in darkness marks growth-promoting light-signalling proteins for destruction; the transcription factor HY5, which activates many light-induced genes, including those of flavonoid and anthocyanin synthesis; and the PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs), which promote elongation growth (Casal, 2013; Taiz et al., 2022).

History — lettuce seeds and a reversible switch

Phytochrome was discovered with lettuce. At the US Department of Agriculture in Beltsville, Harry Borthwick, Sterling Hendricks and colleagues found that red light (around 660 nm) promoted the germination of light-requiring lettuce seeds, that far-red light (around 730 nm) cancelled the effect, and that after a series of alternating red and far-red exposures only the last one mattered (Borthwick et al., 1952). They inferred a pigment that exists in two photo-interconvertible forms. Seven years later Butler, Norris, Siegelman and Hendricks detected it spectrophotometrically in plant tissue, partially purified it and named it phytochrome (Butler et al., 1959). The blue-light receptor cryptochrome was identified only in 1993, from an Arabidopsis mutant with long hypocotyls in blue light (Ahmad & Cashmore, 1993); phototropins followed later in the 1990s (Christie, 2007), and the UV-B receptor UVR8 in 2011 (Rizzini et al., 2011).

Phytochrome and the photostationary state

Phytochrome is synthesised in the red-absorbing form Pr, which is biologically inactive. Absorbing a red photon isomerises a double bond of its chromophore, and the protein relaxes into the far-red-absorbing form Pfr, the active form: Pfr moves into the nucleus, binds PIFs and triggers their degradation, switching off elongation-promoting genes. Absorbing a far-red photon sends Pfr back to Pr. Pfr also reverts to Pr slowly in darkness — faster at higher temperature, a property we return to in Section 4.4.4. Figure 3 summarises the cycle.

Because the absorption spectra of Pr and Pfr overlap (Figure 1), light of almost any colour converts some Pr to Pfr and some Pfr to Pr at the same time. Under constant light the two reactions reach a dynamic equilibrium, the phytochrome photostationary state (PSS, also called the phytochrome photoequilibrium, PPE): the fraction of phytochrome in the active form. Sager et al. (1988) showed how to compute it from a measured spectrum; their method is now standard in horticultural lighting research and in the spectrum-mixer laboratory.

Phytochrome photostationary state (Sager et al., 1988)(4.4.2)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}P_{fr}}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_1\,P_r - k_2\,P_{fr}, \qquad k_1 = \sum_\lambda q(\lambda)\,\sigma_R(\lambda)\,\Delta\lambda,\quad k_2 = \sum_\lambda q(\lambda)\,\sigma_{FR}(\lambda)\,\Delta\lambda \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \mathrm{PSS} = \frac{P_{fr}}{P_{tot}} = \frac{k_1}{k_1 + k_2} \]

active phytochrome is formed at a rate proportional to the inactive pool and destroyed at a rate proportional to the active pool; each rate constant is the photon spectrum weighted by the photoconversion cross-section of the form that absorbs; at steady state the active fraction equals the forward rate constant divided by the sum of both.

Follow the derivation
  1. Rate of one photoreaction. A molecule with photoconversion cross-section \(\sigma\) (m² mol⁻¹) in a photon flux \(q\) (mol m⁻² s⁻¹) is converted with probability per unit time \(k = \sigma q\) (m² mol⁻¹ × mol m⁻² s⁻¹ = s⁻¹). Physically, \(\sigma\) is the molar absorption coefficient (converted to natural-log base and to m² mol⁻¹) times the quantum yield of the photoreaction.
  2. Many wavelengths. Photons of different wavelengths act independently, so rates add: \(k_1 = \sum q(\lambda)\sigma_R(\lambda)\Delta\lambda\) and \(k_2 = \sum q(\lambda)\sigma_{FR}(\lambda)\Delta\lambda\) — Eq. 4.4.1 with the cross-sections as weights.
  3. Two-state balance. Ignoring synthesis, destruction and dark reversion, \(\mathrm{d}P_{fr}/\mathrm{d}t = k_1 P_r - k_2 P_{fr}\) with \(P_r = P_{tot} - P_{fr}\).
  4. Steady state. Set the derivative to zero: \(k_1(P_{tot} - P_{fr}) = k_2 P_{fr}\), hence \(P_{fr}/P_{tot} = k_1/(k_1+k_2)\).
  5. Intensity cancels. Multiplying \(q(\lambda)\) by any constant multiplies \(k_1\) and \(k_2\) equally, so the PSS depends only on the shape of the spectrum — a dim red light and a bright red light establish the same PSS.
  6. Check with the cross-sections at the peaks: pure 660 nm light gives \(4209/(4209+553) = 0.884\); pure 730 nm gives \(36.3/(36.3+1209) = 0.029\). Pure blue (450 nm) gives \(159/(159+195) = 0.45\) — blue light alone is a moderate "shade" signal for phytochrome.

How fast is the equilibrium reached? The two-state equation is linear, so the approach is exponential with a single time constant.

Approach to photoequilibrium(4.4.3)
\[ P_{fr}(t) = \mathrm{PSS}\,P_{tot} + \big(P_{fr,0} - \mathrm{PSS}\,P_{tot}\big)\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{k_1 + k_2} = \frac{1}{\big(\bar\sigma_R + \bar\sigma_{FR}\big)\,Q} \]

The red:far-red ratio

Before spectroradiometers and cross-section tables were common, plant physiologists summarised the phytochrome-relevant part of a spectrum with a single ratio. Harry Smith defined the red:far-red ratio as the photon flux in a 10 nm band centred on 660 nm divided by that in a 10 nm band centred on 730 nm (Smith, 1982). In open daylight it is about 1.1–1.2; under a leaf canopy it can fall below 0.2.

Red:far-red ratio and PSS of a two-source mixture(4.4.4)
\[ \zeta = \mathrm{R{:}FR} = \frac{\int_{655}^{665} q\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\int_{725}^{735} q\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}, \qquad \mathrm{PSS}(\zeta) = \frac{\zeta\,\sigma_{R,660} + \sigma_{R,730}}{\zeta\big(\sigma_{R,660}+\sigma_{FR,660}\big) + \sigma_{R,730}+\sigma_{FR,730}} \]

Shade avoidance: far-red as a design variable

Leaves absorb about 85–90 % of red and blue photons but transmit or reflect most far-red. Light that has passed through or bounced off vegetation is therefore depleted in red relative to far-red, and the R:FR ratio becomes a reliable signal of neighbours: about 1.1–1.2 in open daylight, often below 0.2 under a dense canopy (Smith, 1982). Remarkably, plants detect neighbours before being shaded: far-red reflected from adjacent leaves lowers the R:FR reaching a plant's stems and triggers early responses (Ballaré et al., 1990). Morgan and Smith (1976) showed that the stem extension rate of plants grown under simulated natural radiation increased linearly as the PSS fell — a quantitative link between Eq. 4.4.2 and growth.

The shade-avoidance syndrome is an investment decision: a plant that is about to be overtopped reallocates resources from leaves and branches to height, at the expense of sturdiness, defence and sometimes yield. Molecularly, low Pfr releases the PIF transcription factors from degradation; PIFs activate auxin biosynthesis and signalling, which, together with gibberellins and brassinosteroids, drives cell elongation (Casal, 2013). Blue-light depletion in shade, sensed by cryptochromes, adds to the signal.

For a grower, the syndrome can be a nuisance or a tool. Leggy, weak transplants are a nuisance, and high PSS (little far-red) together with some blue keeps them compact. But in young leafy crops, the extra leaf expansion is an advantage: larger leaves intercept more light, and early growth is limited by light interception. Park and Runkle (2017) showed that adding far-red promoted seedling growth by increasing leaf expansion and whole-plant net assimilation. Zhen and Bugbee (2020b) replaced about 15 % of a 350 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ photon flux with far-red in long-term lettuce studies: canopy quantum yield (per absorbed photon, 400–750 nm) was unchanged, leaf area increased by 36–43 % and biomass by 29–31 %, because the larger plants captured more photons. There are trade-offs: far-red can reduce pigmentation, and Li and Kubota (2009) found that supplemental far-red increased the growth of red baby-leaf lettuce but lowered its anthocyanin and chlorophyll concentrations, whereas supplemental blue raised anthocyanins. Timing offers a third tool: a short pulse of far-red at the end of the photoperiod (end-of-day far-red) lowers Pfr for the whole night and promotes elongation with very few photons.

Frontier — phytochrome as a thermometer

In 2016 two groups showed that phytochrome B also measures temperature: the thermal reversion of Pfr to Pr speeds up as temperature rises, so at the same light a warmer plant has less active phyB, more PIF4 activity and longer stems (Jung et al., 2016; Legris et al., 2016). Light quality and temperature therefore act through the same switch. In controlled environments this means that a far-red recipe tested at 20 °C may give taller plants at 26 °C, and that temperature drops at the start of the day — long used by growers to keep plants compact — act partly through the same pathway.

Blue light: compact plants, open stomata, richer leaves

Blue photons are sensed by cryptochromes and phototropins, and also — as Eq. 4.4.2 showed — by phytochrome, for which pure blue light establishes a PSS of only about 0.45. Blue light has four effects that matter in cultivation. First, morphology: through cryptochromes, blue inhibits stem and hypocotyl elongation and reduces leaf expansion, producing compact plants. Second, stomata: phototropins in guard cells respond to low levels of blue light superimposed on red by activating the plasma-membrane proton pump; potassium uptake and water influx then open the stomata within minutes (Shimazaki et al., 2007). Third, leaf photosynthetic capacity: Hogewoning et al. (2010) grew cucumber under red light with 0 to 50 % blue; only the leaves grown with no blue at all showed dysfunctional photosynthesis, 7 % blue was enough to prevent it, and the maximum photosynthetic rate doubled between 0 and 7 % blue and kept rising up to 50 %, together with leaf mass per area, nitrogen and chlorophyll — the typical traits of sun leaves. Fourth, secondary metabolites: blue (and UV-A) increases anthocyanins and other phenolic compounds in many leafy crops (Li & Kubota, 2009).

Blue light is not free, however. A blue photon carries about 47 % more energy than a red one (4.41 versus 3.01 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; Lesson 4.1), and although blue InGaN LEDs are the most efficient LEDs in terms of watts, they deliver fewer photons per joule than red ones (3.5 versus 4.5 µmol J⁻¹ at package level; Kusuma et al., 2020). And more blue usually means less growth, because smaller leaves intercept fewer photons: Snowden et al. (2016) raised the blue fraction from 11 to 28 % in seven species; at 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ growth fell by 22, 26 and 14 % in tomato, cucumber and pepper, with no significant effect in radish, soybean, lettuce and wheat, while at 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ only tomato responded (−41 %). The effects ran through leaf area and radiation capture, not through photosynthesis per unit leaf area. Cope and Bugbee (2013) added a further subtlety: several responses depend on the absolute blue photon flux, not only on the blue fraction.

Common misconception

"Plants need lots of blue light to grow well." A small amount of blue — roughly 5–10 % of PPFD, or a few tens of µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — is needed for normal leaf development and photosynthetic function (Hogewoning et al., 2010). Beyond that, extra blue generally makes plants more compact and can reduce growth at high photon flux, because smaller leaves capture fewer photons (Snowden et al., 2016). More blue is a tool for compactness, colour and quality — not for yield.

Spectrum and photosynthesis: YPF, green light and the Emerson effect

Photosynthesis itself is only moderately wavelength-selective. McCree (1972) measured the photosynthesis of single leaves of 22 crop species per incident photon at narrow wavebands; relative to the best (red) photons, blue photons were about 70 % as effective and green 75–90 %. Sager et al. (1988) used McCree's average curve to define the yield photon flux (YPF): the photon flux weighted by relative quantum efficiency from 360 to 760 nm.

Yield photon flux and the YPF:PPF ratio(4.4.6)
\[ \mathrm{YPFD} = \int_{360}^{760} q(\lambda)\,\mathrm{RQE}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{YPFD}}{\mathrm{PPFD}} = f_B\,\overline{\mathrm{RQE}}_B + f_G\,\overline{\mathrm{RQE}}_G + f_R\,\overline{\mathrm{RQE}}_R + (\text{tails}) \]

the yield photon flux density is the photon spectrum weighted by McCree's relative quantum efficiency; its ratio to the PPFD is the photon-weighted average efficiency of the spectrum — approximately the waveband fractions times the average efficiency in each band.

Green light: the underrated waveband

Leaves look green because they absorb green photons less strongly than red and blue ones — typically still 70–90 % absorbed, not the few per cent the colour suggests. This "weakness" has advantages. Green photons penetrate deeper into the leaf and the canopy, reaching chloroplasts that red and blue photons, absorbed near the surface, never reach. Terashima et al. (2009) showed with sunflower leaves that under strong white light, additional green light drove leaf photosynthesis more efficiently than additional red light, because the red photons were absorbed by already light-saturated chloroplasts near the surface. Green light also acts as a signal: it can counteract blue-light-induced stomatal opening and contributes to shade perception (Smith et al., 2017).

What about growth? Early NASA work found that adding green to red–blue LEDs enhanced lettuce growth: adding 24 % green light (500–600 nm) produced more biomass than the red–blue control (Kim et al., 2004). Later experiments were less clear-cut. Snowden et al. (2016) found that raising green from 0 to 30 % had relatively small effects on growth in seven species. Kusuma et al. (2021) found that increasing the green fraction up to 50 % promoted the growth of tomato but not of lettuce or cucumber. A meta-analysis of 136 datasets from 17 species concluded that, on average, green light is similarly effective as red–blue light: partially replacing red–blue with green did not change dry mass, increased fresh mass by 4 %, reduced stomatal conductance by 15 % and increased intrinsic water-use efficiency by 15 %, with species-dependent effects — positive in lettuce and microgreens, negative in basil and tomato (Chen et al., 2024). Note the contradiction for lettuce and tomato between two careful studies: spectral effects depend on cultivar, photon flux and the rest of the spectrum.

Far-red and the Emerson effect

In the 1940s and 1950s Robert Emerson and his colleagues found that the quantum yield of photosynthesis in algae collapsed at wavelengths beyond about 680 nm — the red drop — but that far-red light became efficient again when shorter-wavelength light was added at the same time: the combined rate exceeded the sum of the two separate rates — the Emerson enhancement effect (Emerson et al., 1957). The explanation, which led to the discovery of the two photosystems, is that photosystem II (reaction centre P680) and photosystem I (P700) work in series, and far-red photons excite mainly photosystem I. Linear electron transport needs both photosystems to turn over at the same rate; a spectrum that excites one of them more than the other wastes photons.

A two-photosystem model of the Emerson effect(4.4.7)
\[ J = \min\!\Big(\sum_i f_{II,i}\,Q_i,\ \sum_i \big(1 - f_{II,i}\big)\,Q_i\Big), \qquad E = \frac{J(Q_1 + Q_2)}{J(Q_1) + J(Q_2)} \]

the linear electron transport rate is limited by the less-excited photosystem: it equals the smaller of the photons delivered to photosystem II and to photosystem I; the enhancement ratio compares the rate with both light sources together to the sum of the rates with each source alone.

\(Q_i\)absorbed photon flux of source \(i\)µmol m⁻² s⁻¹–
\(f_{II,i}\)fraction of source \(i\)'s absorbed photons delivered to PSII–≈ 0.6 (red), ≈ 0.15 (far-red 720 nm)
\(J\)linear electron transport rateµmol e⁻ m⁻² s⁻¹–
\(E\)Emerson enhancement ratio (> 1: synergy)––
Follow the derivation
  1. Each electron that travels from water to NADP⁺ must be excited twice — once in PSII and once in PSI (Figure 6). The chain therefore runs at the rate of the slower photosystem: \(J = \min(\text{PSII excitations}, \text{PSI excitations})\).
  2. Source \(i\) contributes \(f_{II,i}Q_i\) excitations to PSII and \((1-f_{II,i})Q_i\) to PSI; sum over the sources.
  3. Red alone (100 µmol, \(f_{II} = 0.6\)): \(J = \min(60, 40) = 40\) — PSI-limited. Far-red alone (50 µmol, \(f_{II} = 0.15\)): \(J = \min(7.5, 42.5) = 7.5\) — the red drop.
  4. Together: \(J = \min(60 + 7.5,\ 40 + 42.5) = 67.5\), so \(E = 67.5/(40 + 7.5) = 1.42\).
  5. Balance point: the photosystems are balanced when \(\sum f_{II}Q = \sum(1-f_{II})Q\); for this example \(60 + 0.15x = 40 + 0.85x\), so \(x = 29\) µmol of far-red per 100 µmol of red. Beyond that, extra far-red is PSII-limited and gives little.
Assumptions and validity

A didactic model: complete absorption, identical quantum efficiency of each photosystem, no cyclic electron flow and no state transitions (plants partly rebalance excitation by moving antenna complexes between photosystems within minutes). The distribution fractions are illustrative. The model nevertheless captures why far-red is inefficient alone but efficient in combination — the basis of extended PAR (Zhen & van Iersel, 2017; Zhen & Bugbee, 2020a).

µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of photons → µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of electrons (one electron per pair of excitations) ✓; \(E\) dimensionless ✓

These principles led Zhen and Bugbee (2020a) to measure canopy photosynthesis of 14 species under combinations of traditional (400–700 nm) and far-red (700–750 nm) photons. Far-red photons, when combined with shorter wavelengths, were as efficient as the traditional photosynthetic photons; they proposed extending PAR to 400–750 nm (ePAR) and, later, including far-red photons in the measurement of fixture efficacy (Zhen et al., 2021). Far-red therefore acts twice: photosynthetically, through the Emerson effect, and morphologically, through phytochrome, by expanding leaves that capture more light.

Frontier · 2025 — how much is a far-red photon worth?

The ePAR proposal is debated. Jin et al. (2025) added far-red or extra PAR photons to lettuce grown at 200 and 400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PAR: per incident photon, far-red increased leaf photosynthesis by only 39–64 % as much as extra PAR, because leaves absorb far-red poorly — yet per absorbed photon the efficiencies were similar, and partly substituting PAR with far-red increased the radiation-use efficiency of growth, through the morphological response. Whether sensors, fixture labels and DLI targets should count far-red photons (ePAR) is one of the live questions in horticultural lighting as of 2026.

UV-A, UV-B and the colour of red lettuce

Ultraviolet radiation is divided into UV-C (below 280 nm, absorbed by the ozone layer and used for disinfection), UV-B (280–315 nm) and UV-A (315–400 nm). UV-B photons can damage DNA and proteins, but at the levels found in daylight plants perceive them as information through UVR8 (Rizzini et al., 2011). In darkness UVR8 is a dimer; absorbing UV-B through specific tryptophan residues splits it into monomers, which bind COP1 and thereby stabilise the transcription factor HY5. HY5 switches on genes for flavonoid and anthocyanin synthesis, DNA repair and other protective traits (Jenkins, 2014). The result is a leaf that screens itself with UV-absorbing phenolics — and, in red cultivars, turns red.

This biology explains a familiar commercial problem. Red lettuce grown indoors or under UV-blocking greenhouse covers is often disappointingly green. Tsormpatsidis et al. (2008) grew the red cultivar 'Lollo Rosso' under polyethylene films with different UV transmission and found that anthocyanin production was almost entirely dependent on UV radiation below 370 nm — but that plants under a completely UV-blocking film produced up to 2.2 times more above-ground dry weight than plants under a UV-transparent film. Colour and growth pull in opposite directions. The practical compromise is end-of-production lighting: grow for yield, then give the crop a few days of a pigment-promoting spectrum before harvest. Owen and Lopez (2015) found that supplementing red-leaf lettuce with high-intensity red and blue LED light at the end of production increased red pigmentation of four varieties within about 5–7 days; UV-A, blue and UV-B treatments are used in the same way.

UV has costs and hazards. UV-A LEDs at 385 nm have external quantum efficiencies around 50 % (Amano et al., 2020) — far below blue and red LEDs — and UV-B LEDs are much less efficient still. UV-B and UV-A, and intense blue light, are photobiological hazards for the eyes and skin of workers; lamps are classified into risk groups under the international standard IEC 62471, and UV installations need shielding, interlocks or operation when no one is present. Doses are specified as energy per area per day.

Spectral recipes: evidence, electricity and people

A light recipe specifies the spectrum, photon flux, photoperiod and their timing for a crop and growth stage — for example "20 % blue during propagation, far-red added after transplanting, blue- and UV-rich end-of-production lighting for colour". Multi-channel LED fixtures make such recipes easy to implement, and suppliers market them enthusiastically. The evidence base deserves a critical engineering eye.

  • Photons first. Growth depends mainly on the number of photons intercepted by the canopy (the daily light integral of Lesson 4.2 and the leaf area that intercepts it). Bugbee (2016) argued that the most important spectral effects on growth work through radiation capture — leaf expansion — and that, at equal photon flux, spectrum usually changes yield by much less than a change in photon flux would.
  • Interactions. Spectral effects depend on photon flux (Snowden et al., 2016), on absolute rather than relative amounts (Cope & Bugbee, 2013), on species and cultivar (Chen et al., 2024) and on the rest of the spectrum. A "recipe" optimised for one cultivar at 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ may not transfer to another at 400.
  • Confounding. Many comparisons of fixtures do not hold photon flux equal, or measure it with a quantum sensor that under-reads narrow-band LEDs near the edges of PAR (Lesson 4.1). A difference in growth then reflects photons, not colour.
  • Design and statistics. Spectral treatments are usually applied per chamber or per shelf, so the chamber — not the plant — is the experimental unit; many published comparisons have one chamber per treatment, i.e. no true replication (Lesson 3.1).
  • Conflicting results are normal. Green light promoted tomato but not lettuce in one careful study (Kusuma et al., 2021) and lettuce but not tomato in a meta-analysis (Chen et al., 2024). Treat any single study as a hypothesis for your own conditions.

Spectral choices also have a price, because LED colours differ in photon efficacy (Lesson 4.3). The equation below turns a spectral decision into kilowatt-hours and into the yield increase it must deliver to pay for itself.

Working under magenta light

Red–blue LED light looks magenta ("blurple") to the human eye and renders colours very poorly. Park and Runkle (2018) compared white-based and blue + red spectra: the colour rendering index (CRI, maximum 100) of a spectrum with 15 % blue and 85 % red was negative, while a mint-white LED reached 64 and mixtures of mint white and red reached 72–77 — and dry-mass production per unit of electricity was similar. Under poor colour rendering, workers struggle to recognise nutrient deficiencies, pests, diseases and ripeness, and many find the light uncomfortable over long shifts. Designers respond in three ways: white-based spectra with a red boost, a "work mode" that switches fixtures to white or dims the red while people are in the room, and task lighting for inspection. Already in 2004, the NASA study that added green light to red–blue LEDs noted that green improves the visual environment for crews in long missions (Kim et al., 2004). Wherever intense blue or UV channels are used, check the photobiological risk group of the fixtures (IEC 62471) and provide eye protection where necessary.

Key idea

Design lighting in this order: (1) enough photons at the canopy, uniformly, for the target DLI (Lessons 4.2–4.3); (2) the most efficient photons you can buy; (3) spectral adjustments where there is evidence for a specific benefit — compactness, leaf expansion, colour, flowering control — and where that benefit outweighs its cost in photon efficacy; (4) a visual environment that lets people do their work.
