The EU Novel Food Regulation

In June 2016 a small Swedish company asked the Swedish Food Agency for permission to sell button mushrooms that had been briefly exposed to ultraviolet light. Nothing had been added to them, yet eighteen months, an initial assessment, objections from other Member States and a Commission decision passed before the mushrooms could be sold. Mealworm flour took more than three years; chia seeds more than six. This lesson explains why — and how the European Union decides whether a new food may reach your plate. You will learn to read Regulation (EU) 2015/2283 like a regulatory affairs specialist: its definition, its procedures, its clocks and its loopholes.

What you will be able to do

  • Apply the legal definition of a novel food — the 15 May 1997 cut-off and the ten categories of Article 3(2)(a) — to real products, including those from controlled-environment agriculture and alternative-protein production (LO6).
  • Choose the correct route to market: no authorisation needed, status consultation, full application (Articles 10–12) or traditional-food notification (Articles 14–20) (LO6).
  • Describe every step of the authorisation procedure — validity check, EFSA risk assessment, comitology in the PAFF Committee — with its legal time limit and the rules for clock stops (LO6).
  • Calculate realistic procedure durations from legal deadlines and empirical statistics, and the expiry of five-year data protection (LO6, LO3).
  • Analyse case studies (chia, UV-treated mushrooms, insects, CBD, precision-fermentation ingredients, cultivated meat) to explain how the regulation enables or hinders the adoption of new food technologies (LO6, LO1).

Why a new food needs permission

Most food in the European Union reaches the market without anyone asking permission. A baker who invents a new rye bread, a grower who switches her lettuce from soil to hydroponics, or a start-up that mixes pea protein, rapeseed oil and beetroot into a burger does not need a licence. They must follow the general principles of EU food law — above all Article 14 of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, which forbids placing unsafe food on the market — together with hygiene, labelling and contaminant rules. Responsibility for safety lies with the food business operator, and national authorities check compliance afterwards. This is a post-market control system.

For a small group of products the logic is reversed. If a food is novel, it may not be placed on the market until it has been assessed and authorised: the control is pre-market. The rationale is simple. For foods eaten for generations we have a history of use that serves as a large, uncontrolled but informative safety experiment. For a food nobody in Europe has eaten, that evidence is missing, and it is replaced by a scientific risk assessment. The Regulation also has an internal-market purpose (its legal basis is Article 114 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union): one centralised authorisation, valid in all 27 Member States, replaces a patchwork of national decisions.

Big idea

The EU novel food system is process- and history-based, not risk-based in the first step: whether a food needs pre-market authorisation depends on whether it was eaten in the EU before 15 May 1997 and how it is made, not on how risky it is. Risk enters in the second step, when EFSA assesses an application.

For this course the regulation matters in three ways. First, many products of future food production — insect flours, microalgae, mycoprotein, precision-fermentation proteins, cultivated meat, new plant extracts — fall under it. Second, it decides when an innovation can earn money, and time-to-market is often what kills a start-up (Lesson 14.4). Third, it does not apply to most products of controlled-environment agriculture: a lettuce grown in a vertical farm in Västerås is still a lettuce. Knowing where the boundary lies is a professional skill.

From Regulation (EC) No 258/97 to Regulation (EU) 2015/2283

The EU's first novel food law, Regulation (EC) No 258/97, entered into force on 15 May 1997. That date — not a scientific threshold but the birthday of the first regulation — is still the cut-off in today's definition, so that foods that were legally on the market under the old rules did not suddenly become "novel". Under Regulation 258/97 an applicant approached the competent authority of the Member State where the food would first be marketed. That authority wrote an initial assessment report, normally within three months; the Commission circulated it, and the other Member States had 60 days to raise reasoned objections. Without objections the food could be marketed; with objections, the file went to the Commission, often via the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), and ended in a Commission decision addressed to the individual applicant. A simplified notification procedure (Article 5) existed for foods judged "substantially equivalent" to an existing food.

Two documented cases show how this worked in practice. On 30 June 2003 a company asked the UK authorities to authorise chia seeds (Salvia hispanica) for use in bread. The UK initial assessment followed on 7 May 2004, other Member States objected, EFSA's first opinion (5 October 2005) found the data insufficient, and only its second opinion (13 March 2009) cleared the way for Commission Decision 2009/827/EC of 13 October 2009 — authorising chia at up to 5 % in bread products, more than six years after the request. By contrast, the Swedish company Ekoidé AB applied to the Swedish competent authority on 10 June 2016 for UV-treated mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus) with increased vitamin D₂. Sweden's initial assessment was ready on 27 February 2017; objections raised in the 60-day period were resolved with additional explanations; and Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2017/2355 of 14 December 2017 authorised the product — about 18 months in total.

Regulation 2015/2283 kept the concept but changed the machinery in four ways that you should be able to explain:

  1. Centralisation. Applications go directly to the European Commission, and the risk assessment is done by EFSA, not by a national authority.
  2. Generic authorisations. Authorisation is no longer a decision addressed to one company. The food is added to the Union list, and any operator may market it within the authorised specification and conditions of use — unless five-year data protection applies.
  3. Time limits. EFSA has nine months and the Commission seven months for their parts of the procedure (with extensions discussed below).
  4. A fast track for traditional foods from third countries with a 25-year history of safe use elsewhere in the world.

What counts as a novel food?

Article 3(2)(a) defines a novel food as "any food that was not used for human consumption to a significant degree within the Union before 15 May 1997, irrespective of the dates of accession of Member States to the Union, and that falls under at least one of the following categories". The definition therefore has two cumulative conditions: a historical test (no significant consumption in the EU before the cut-off date) and a categorical test (the food belongs to at least one of ten categories). Note the phrase about accession: a food eaten widely in Finland or Sweden in 1994 counts as "used in the Union" even though both countries joined only in 1995, and — importantly — so does a food eaten in, say, Poland before 1997, although Poland joined in 2004.

Several details matter in practice. "Significant degree" is not defined numerically; evidence of sale in shops across a Member State, in recipes, in trade statistics or in nutrition surveys counts, whereas sporadic use or use only as a medicine does not. Food supplements are a special case: a food used before 1997 exclusively in supplements may still be sold as a supplement, but that use does not count as consumption "to a significant degree" for other foods (recital 13, category (x)). Recipes are not novel: recital 17 states that food produced exclusively from ingredients outside the scope — by changing the ingredients or their amounts — is not a novel food. And the scope excludes genetically modified food covered by Regulation (EC) No 1829/2003, and foods used solely as enzymes, additives, flavourings or extraction solvents, which have their own authorisation regimes (Article 2(2)).

Common misconception

"Food from a vertical farm or aquaponic system is a novel food because the production method is new." Category (vii) only captures a new process if it causes significant changes in composition or structure affecting nutritional value, metabolism or undesirable substances. Hydroponic lettuce, LED-grown basil or aquaponic tilapia normally do not meet that test; they are ordinary foods under general food law (although specific rules, for example on organic labelling, still apply — Lesson 14.2).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Hydroponic lettuce. Lettuce was eaten to a significant degree in the EU before 1997. Novel, but already authorised: the Union list entry allows up to 10 µg vitamin D₂ per 100 g fresh weight, with the name "UV-treated mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus)". Sanity check: only (c) needs an EFSA assessment. That is typical — most future-food innovations in growing systems stay outside the regulation, whereas new sources of food usually fall inside.

When in doubt: the status consultation

Article 4 puts the first responsibility on the operator: "Food business operators shall verify whether or not the food which they intend to place on the market within the Union falls within the scope of this Regulation." If they are unsure, they must consult the Member State where they first intend to market the food. The procedure is set out in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2018/456:

  • The operator submits a consultation request (cover letter, technical dossier, supporting documents and an explanatory note) to one Member State only — in Sweden the Swedish Food Agency (Livsmedelsverket).
  • The Member State checks the validity of the request "without delay" and may ask for more information.
  • It must conclude on the status within 4 months of deciding that the request is valid. A request for additional information does not stop this clock; in duly justified cases the Member State may extend the period by at most 4 months (Article 6).
  • The conclusion is one of three: novel, not novel, or not novel only in food supplements. It is notified to all Member States and the Commission, and published on the Commission's website (Article 7).

The evidence that decides a consultation is historical: invoices, catalogues, recipes, trade and customs data, or affidavits showing that the food was sold to consumers in a Member State before 15 May 1997. Statements that a plant grows wild or is used in traditional medicine do not suffice. If a Member State cannot decide, the Commission can settle a question of status by implementing act (Article 5).

Practical tip

The status outcomes are collected by the Commission in its public list of consultation outcomes and in the (non-binding) EU Novel Food status Catalogue. Always check both before starting a dossier — many ingredients have already been classified.

The authorisation procedure step by step

If a food is novel and not yet on the Union list (or is on the list but with a different specification or use), the operator — the applicant — submits an application to the Commission (Article 10). Its content is fixed in Article 10(2) and in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2469: identity and composition, production process, specifications, history of use, proposed uses and intake, absorption–distribution–metabolism–excretion, nutritional information, toxicology and allergenicity, plus a proposal for labelling and a justification of any confidentiality or data-protection claim. The Commission publishes a summary of each valid application.

Step 1 — Validity check

The Commission verifies "without delay" that the application falls within the scope of the regulation and contains the required information (Article 6 of Implementing Regulation 2017/2469). It may consult EFSA, which then has 30 working days to give its view on completeness. In practice EFSA performs a "suitability check" of the scientific dossier before the risk assessment starts. Under the Transparency Regulation, EFSA also checks that all studies supporting the application were notified in advance in EFSA's database (Article 32b of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, as amended); an application that relies on un-notified studies is not considered valid. Applicants can request pre-submission advice from EFSA staff on the rules and the content of an application (Article 32a).

Step 2 — EFSA risk assessment

The Commission forwards the valid application to EFSA "without delay, and not later than one month after having verified its validity" (Article 11(1)). EFSA's Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens (NDA) must adopt its opinion within nine months from the date of receipt of a valid application. It considers whether the novel food is as safe as food from a comparable category, whether its composition and conditions of use pose a risk, and whether, if it replaces another food, it would be nutritionally disadvantageous (Article 11(2)). Since 1 February 2025 the data requirements are those of EFSA's 2024 guidance, which replaced the 2016 guidance and its 2021 revision. It asks for a stepwise assessment built around the production process, compositional data on several batches, specifications, proposed uses and intake estimates, toxicological studies (a tiered strategy in which the subchronic 90-day oral toxicity study, OECD TG 408, is a cornerstone for many novel foods), nutritional information and allergenicity; for foods from cell culture it also asks for the identity and genetic and phenotypic stability of the cells.

Step 3 — Risk management and comitology

Within seven months from the publication of EFSA's opinion, the Commission submits a draft implementing act to the Standing Committee on Plants, Animals, Food and Feed (PAFF), composed of Member State representatives (Article 12(1)). The Commission must take into account the conditions of Article 7 (safety, not misleading, not nutritionally disadvantageous), relevant Union law including the precautionary principle, EFSA's opinion and "any other legitimate factors". The committee votes under the examination procedure of Regulation (EU) No 182/2011, i.e., by qualified majority (55 % of Member States representing 65 % of the EU population). A positive vote leads to adoption of a Commission implementing regulation that amends the Union list; it is published in the Official Journal and enters into force, typically after 20 days. The Commission may also terminate the procedure at any stage if it considers an update "not justified" (Article 10(6)), and the applicant may withdraw at any time (Article 10(7)).

The deadlines are not what they seem

"Nine months" and "seven months" are net times. Article 11(4) lets EFSA extend its period when it requests additional information: EFSA sets, after consulting the applicant, a period for the answer; unless the Commission objects within eight working days, the nine months are "automatically extended by that additional period". This is the famous clock stop. Article 21 allows the Commission to extend its seven months in the same way for risk-management information, and Article 22 allows ad hoc extensions "in exceptional circumstances". Information sent by the applicant on its own initiative does not stop the clock (Article 11(6)).

Clocks, clock stops and real durations

To reason quantitatively about time-to-market, separate active assessment time from time spent waiting for the applicant. For EFSA's part, the legal rule can be written as an equation.

EFSA opinion date with clock stops(14.1.1)
\[ t_{\text{opinion}} = t_{\text{valid}} + T_{\text{act}} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta_i, \qquad T_{\text{act}} \le 9\ \text{months} \]

the date on which EFSA adopts its opinion equals the date it received the valid application, plus the active assessment time (which must not exceed nine months), plus the sum of all clock-stop periods during which EFSA waited for additional information from the applicant.

Where does this come from?
  1. Article 11(1): the opinion is due nine months after receipt of a valid application — this is the running clock.
  2. Article 11(4): each request for additional information extends the nine months "by that additional period", i.e., by the time given to the applicant. EFSA's practice is to "suspend" the assessment on the day of the request and "restart" it when the information arrives, so \(\Delta_i\) is measured from request to reply.
  3. Adding all suspensions to the running clock gives Eq. 14.1.1. Rearranged, it gives the active time actually used by EFSA: \(T_{\text{act}} = (t_{\text{opinion}}-t_{\text{valid}}) - \sum\Delta_i\).

The Union list, generic authorisation and data protection

Only novel foods included in the Union list may be placed on the market, and only "in accordance with the conditions of use and the labelling requirements specified therein" (Article 6(2)). The list was established by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2470, which carried over every food authorised or notified under Regulation 258/97, and it has been amended by every authorisation since. An entry (Article 9(3)) contains:

  • the specification — a description of the food and its production process with compositional, microbiological and contaminant limits (for UV-treated mushrooms: vitamin D₂ ≤ 10 µg per 100 g fresh weight after UV-B treatment);
  • the conditions of use — food categories and maximum levels, and any restrictions for population groups (chia seeds in bread up to 5 %; pre-packed chia seeds limited to 15 g per day);
  • additional specific labelling requirements — the designation on the label and warnings (rapeseed protein must carry a statement that it may cause allergic reactions in consumers allergic to mustard; insect products warn people allergic to crustaceans, molluscs and dust mites);
  • where appropriate, post-market monitoring requirements (Article 24).

Because the authorisation is generic, a competitor who produces a food complying with the specification may sell it without a new application. That is good for competition and consumers, but it weakens the incentive to pay for an expensive dossier. Article 26 therefore offers data protection: on request, newly developed scientific evidence or proprietary data may not be used for the benefit of a subsequent application for five years from the date of authorisation without the agreement of the initial applicant. Three conditions apply: the data were designated as proprietary when the application was made; the applicant had the exclusive right of reference to them; and the food could not have been assessed and authorised without them. During those five years the Union list entry names the applicant, and the food "is authorised for placing on the market within the Union only by the applicant" (Article 27(1)(d)) — unless a competitor obtains its own authorisation without referring to the protected data. Protection cannot be renewed (Article 27(2)), and it does not exist for traditional foods from third countries (Article 26(3)). When an applicant also seeks a health claim for the same food under Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, Article 28 allows the novel food procedure to be stayed so that the two protection periods run concurrently.

\[ t_{\text{end}} = t_{\text{auth}} + 5\ \text{a}, \qquad t_{\text{comp}} = t_{\text{auth}} + \min\!\big(5\ \text{a},\; s + W_2\big) \]

data protection ends five years after the date of authorisation. A competitor can enter either when protection expires or, earlier, if it submits its own full dossier \(s\) years after the first authorisation and that procedure takes \(W_2\) years — whichever comes first.

Worked example 3How long is a head start worth?

A precision-fermentation company obtains authorisation, with data protection, for a milk protein made by a yeast; the implementing regulation enters into force on 15 March 2027. A rival submitted its own complete dossier (with its own studies) on 1 October 2026. Using the average duration from Eq. 14.1.2 (3.3 years), estimate when the rival can sell, and when anyone else can sell a food that meets the first company's specification without doing any studies.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Protection end (Eq. 14.1.3): 15 March 2027 + 5 years = 15 March 2032. That date will be printed in the Union list entry.
  2. Rival with own data. Its procedure started 1 October 2026 — \(s = -0.45\) a relative to the first authorisation. Expected authorisation: 1 October 2026 + 3.3 a ≈ January 2030, i.e., \(s + W_2 = 2.85\) a after the first authorisation, earlier than 5 a. The rival gets its own entry (possibly also with data protection).
  3. Everyone else. After 15 March 2032 the entry becomes generic: any operator producing the food to specification may market it, relying on the authorisation.
  4. Interpretation. The first mover's effective exclusivity against a well-funded rival is about 2.8 years, not 5. Exclusivity against smaller followers who cannot afford a full dossier is the full five years.
Protection ends 15 March 2032; a rival with its own dossier could enter around January 2030

Traditional foods from third countries

Many foods that are new to Europe have been eaten safely for centuries elsewhere. For them, Articles 14–20 provide an alternative to the full procedure. The food must be a traditional food from a third country: a novel food derived from primary production (not a novel molecule, mineral, new process, nanomaterial, vitamin/mineral case or former supplement — categories (i), (iii), (vii), (viii), (ix) and (x) are excluded) with a history of safe food use in a third country. That history means that safety has been "confirmed with compositional data and from experience of continued use for at least 25 years in the customary diet of a significant number of people in at least one third country" (Article 3(2)(b)–(c)).

  1. The applicant sends a notification to the Commission with composition, country of origin, documented data on the history of safe use and proposed conditions of use and labelling (Article 14; Implementing Regulation 2017/2468).
  2. The Commission forwards the valid notification to the Member States and EFSA without delay and not later than one month after verifying its validity (Article 15(1)).
  3. Within four months, a Member State or EFSA may submit duly reasoned safety objections (Article 15(2)).
  4. No objections → the Commission authorises the food and updates the Union list "without delay"; the entry states that it is a traditional food from a third country (Article 15(4)). No comitology vote is needed.
  5. Objections → no authorisation; the applicant may submit an application addressing them (Article 16). EFSA then has six months (extendable with clock stops, Article 17(4)) and the Commission three months after publication of the opinion to submit a draft implementing act to the PAFF Committee (Article 18).
Probability-weighted duration of the traditional-food route(14.1.4)
\[ \mathbb{E}[T_{\text{TF}}] = T_{\text{v}} + 1 + 4 + p_{\text{obj}}\big(T_{\text{app}} + 6 + S + 3 + T_{\text{vote}}\big) \quad [\text{months}] \]

the expected time to authorisation is the validity check, plus at most one month for forwarding, plus the four-month objection window; with probability \(p_{\text{obj}}\) an objection sends the file into the application route, which adds the time to prepare the application, six months for EFSA plus clock stops, three months for the Commission and the time for the vote and adoption.

Follow the derivation
  1. Two mutually exclusive outcomes after the objection window: no objection (probability \(1-p_{\text{obj}}\)) with duration \(T_1 = T_{\text{v}}+1+4\); objection (probability \(p_{\text{obj}}\)) with duration \(T_2 = T_1 + T_{\text{app}} + 6 + S + 3 + T_{\text{vote}}\).
  2. Law of total expectation: \(\mathbb{E}[T] = (1-p)T_1 + pT_2 = T_1 + p(T_2 - T_1)\), which is Eq. 14.1.4.
  3. The equation uses legal maxima (1, 4, 6, 3 months); real durations can be shorter or longer.

Worked example 4Fast track or full application?

An importer wants to sell a dried fruit pulp eaten for over 50 years in West Africa. The notification's validity check takes 3 months. (a) What is the earliest authorisation date if the notification is valid on 1 February 2027 and no one objects? (b) If objections are raised, the importer needs 4 months to prepare an application, EFSA uses its 6 months plus 6 months of clock stops, and the vote and adoption take 2 months after the Commission's 3 months. What is the total? (c) Should a company with a 40 % risk of objection choose this route rather than an Article 10 application expected to take 3.3 years?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Forwarding ≤ 1 month → by 1 March 2027; objection window of 4 months → ends about 1 July 2027. Authorisation "without delay" afterwards: early July 2027, about 8 months after submission (3 + 1 + 4).
  2. (b) 3 + 1 + 4 + 4 + 6 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 29 months ≈ 2.4 years.
  3. (c) Eq. 14.1.4 with \(p=0.4\): \(8 + 0.4\times21 = 16.4\) months ≈ 1.4 years, versus about 40 months for Article 10. Even the objection branch (29 months) is faster than the average full procedure, because EFSA's clock is six rather than nine months and the Commission's three rather than seven. The catch: no data protection (Article 26(3)), and the dossier must genuinely document 25 years of safe use.
(a) ≈ July 2027 · (b) ≈ 29 months · (c) expected ≈ 16 months — the notification route is attractive if the evidence exists

Insects: a transitional puzzle

Under Regulation 258/97 it was disputed whether whole insects were covered at all (the old text referred to "food ingredients isolated from animals"), and several Member States, such as Belgium and the Netherlands, tolerated their sale. Regulation 2015/2283 settled the question — recital 8 says the categories "should cover whole insects and their parts" — and Article 35(2) let foods that were lawfully on the market before 2018, but outside the old regulation, stay on the market provided an application was submitted by a deadline (set at 1 January 2019 by Implementing Regulation 2017/2469). The first authorisation came in June 2021; each species and each form (dried, frozen, powder, defatted, UV-treated) requires its own entry. EFSA's opinions consistently concluded that insect proteins may cause allergic reactions in people allergic to crustaceans and dust mites, hence the labelling conditions (EFSA NDA Panel, 2021).

History — a myth that would not die

When Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/89 authorised UV-treated mealworm powder in January 2025, social media claimed that the EU was "secretly adding insects to bread". In fact every insect ingredient must be named in the list of ingredients with its scientific and common name, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 and the specific conditions in the Union list. Misinformation about novel foods is itself a factor in consumer acceptance (Module 13).

CBD: when the clock stops indefinitely

After the Court of Justice held in November 2020 (Case C-663/18, Kanavape) that CBD extracted from the whole hemp plant is not a narcotic drug, the Commission accepted that CBD can qualify as a food and received a large number of novel food applications for it. In June 2022 EFSA's NDA Panel concluded that the safety of CBD as a novel food could not be established because of data gaps on effects on the liver, gastrointestinal tract, endocrine, nervous and reproductive systems (EFSA NDA Panel, 2022), and the assessments were put on hold until applicants provided the missing data. In February 2026 EFSA updated its statement and set a provisional safe intake of 0.0275 mg per kg body weight per day for adults — about 2 mg per day for a 70 kg adult — applicable only to food supplements containing CBD of at least 98 % purity, and not to people under 25, pregnant or lactating women or people on medication (EFSA NDA Panel, 2026). The case shows the asymmetry of the system: a lack of evidence is not a reason to authorise.

Precision fermentation and cultivated meat

Proteins or oligosaccharides made by genetically modified micro-organisms are novel foods, not GM foods, when the final product contains no GM material (no viable cells or recombinant DNA). Human-milk oligosaccharides such as 2′-fucosyllactose produced by fermentation have been authorised since the late 2010s, each production strain with its own specification. Applications for animal-free dairy proteins — for example a β-lactoglobulin produced by the yeast Komagataella phaffii — appear among the Commission's 2023 application summaries. Cultivated meat is category (vi). The first EU application, for duck cells from cell culture intended for a foie-gras-like product, was submitted by Gourmey (Suprême SAS) in July 2024, and Mosa Meat applied for cultivated beef fat in January 2025. Mosa Meat has explained its choice of fat as a first product by the EU's ingredient-by-ingredient assessment. As of 2026, no cultivated-meat product has been authorised in the EU. Regulation 2015/2283 applies in full; in addition, rules on the names such products may carry changed in 2026 (Lesson 14.2), and some Member States have tried to ban them (Lesson 14.3).

Frontier 2025–2026

EFSA has prepared for cell-culture-derived foods with a scientific colloquium and dedicated pre-submission advice, and its 2024 guidance includes requirements for foods from cell cultures (identity and stability of cell lines, culture media components and their residues, microbiological safety). Because EFSA assesses each ingredient, companies often start with simple products (fat, cells for a blended product) before whole-cut meat. Watch for the first EFSA opinion on a cultivated animal product: it will define the evidence expected from every later applicant.

Key terms

Novel food
Food not used for human consumption to a significant degree in the EU before 15 May 1997 that falls into at least one of the ten categories of Article 3(2)(a) of Regulation (EU) 2015/2283.
Union list
The list of authorised novel foods, established by Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2470, specifying each food, its conditions of use and labelling.
Generic authorisation
An authorisation that allows any operator to market a food complying with the Union list entry, as opposed to an authorisation addressed to one applicant.
Status consultation
Procedure under Article 4 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2018/456 in which a Member State determines, within 4 (+4) months, whether a food is novel.
Validity check
The Commission's verification that an application is within scope and complete, with an optional EFSA view within 30 working days (Implementing Regulation (EU) 2017/2469, Article 6).
Clock stop
Suspension of a legal time limit while the applicant provides information requested by EFSA (Article 11(4)) or the Commission (Article 21).
NDA Panel
EFSA's Panel on Nutrition, Novel Foods and Food Allergens, which adopts novel food opinions.
PAFF Committee
Standing Committee on Plants, Animals, Food and Feed, where Member States vote by qualified majority on draft implementing acts.
Data protection (novel foods)
Five-year protection of proprietary scientific data from use for a subsequent application (Article 26), during which only the applicant may market the food unless a rival obtains its own authorisation.
Traditional food from a third country
A novel food from primary production with at least 25 years of safe use in the customary diet of a significant number of people in a third country, eligible for notification (Articles 14–20).
History of safe food use
Evidence from compositional data and experience of continued use in the customary diet that a food is safe (Article 3(2)(b)).
Post-market monitoring
Obligations imposed in an authorisation to monitor actual use and intake after the food is marketed (Article 24).
Transparency Regulation
Regulation (EU) 2019/1381, which introduced pre-submission advice, mandatory notification of studies and proactive publication of application data.
