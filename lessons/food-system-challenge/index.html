Feeding ten billion: the food-system challenge

The world already grows enough calories to feed everyone — and yet about one person in twelve goes hungry, one adult in six lives with obesity, and farming is the largest human pressure on land, water, nitrogen and biodiversity. By the 2080s there will be roughly two billion more of us. This lesson turns that paradox into numbers: how fast demand grows and why, how much more the land can yield, how much food is lost on the way to the plate, and why a new machine or a new molecule will not, on its own, solve the problem.

What you will be able to do

  • Model population growth with exponential and logistic equations, compute doubling times, and interpret the UN World Population Prospects 2024 (LO7).
  • Decompose the environmental impact of the food system into population, consumption per person and impact per unit of food, and calculate the efficiency gains needed to stay within a target (LO1, LO7).
  • Explain how undernourishment is estimated from the distribution of food intake, and quantify hunger, food insecurity and obesity with the latest UN figures (LO7).
  • Define potential, water-limited and actual yield, calculate yield gaps, and estimate how much production could rise by closing them (LO1, LO3).
  • Trace crop calories through feed, losses and waste to the plate, and evaluate land sparing, land sharing and rebound effects when judging whether a technology will really help (LO1, LO7).

The arithmetic of feeding humanity

Every day roughly eight billion people need, on average, a little over 2,000 kilocalories of food energy each, together with protein, fats, vitamins and minerals in the right proportions. The global food system — every farm, fishing boat, silo, mill, lorry, supermarket and kitchen — exists to meet that need. It is the largest economic sector by employment, the largest user of land and fresh water, and one of the largest sources of greenhouse gases. Understanding it quantitatively is the first step towards judging any "future food" technology, from vertical farms to cultivated meat.

Four numbers frame the challenge. First, the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024 estimate that the world population reached about 8.2 billion in 2024 and will grow to about 9.7 billion in 2050, peaking at around 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s before declining slightly to about 10.2 billion by 2100 (UN DESA, 2024). Second, the average global dietary energy supply — the food energy available for human consumption according to FAO food balance sheets — was about 2,985 kcal per person per day in 2022 and passed 3,000 kcal in 2023 (FAO, 2025): more than enough, on average, for everyone. Third, despite that surplus, the UN's State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2026 estimates that about 645 million people (7.8 % of the world population) faced chronic hunger in 2025 and that 2.69 billion people could not afford a healthy diet (FAO et al., 2026). Fourth, food production already dominates the human pressure on the planet: it occupies about 43 % of the world's ice- and desert-free land and generates about a quarter to a third of all anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions (Poore & Nemecek, 2018; Crippa et al., 2021).

The tension between these numbers is the subject of this module. We need more food (or at least better-distributed and healthier food) for more people, with less environmental damage. Engineers tend to see this as a supply problem to be solved by higher yields and new production systems. That is part of the answer, but the numbers above already hint that access, distribution, diets and waste matter at least as much as production.

Key idea

The food challenge has two sides. The supply side asks how much we can produce, where and at what environmental cost. The demand side asks how much and what we eat, how much is lost and wasted, and who can afford it. Every credible analysis — from the World Resources Institute to the EAT–Lancet Commission — concludes that both sides must move together.

Population: exponential and logistic growth

Population is the first multiplier of food demand, so we begin with the two simplest models of how a population grows. They are worth learning carefully, because the same mathematics describes the growth of bacteria in a bioreactor (Lesson 12.3), the adoption of a new technology (Lesson 1.4) and the growth of a lettuce plant (Lesson 2.4).

Exponential growth

If every person, on average, contributes the same net growth per year — births minus deaths plus net migration — then the number of people added per year is proportional to the number of people already there. This is the defining property of exponential growth.

Exponential population growth(1.1.1)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = r\,N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N(t) = N_0\,e^{\,r\,(t-t_0)} \]

the rate of change of population equals the growth rate times the population; its solution says the population at time t equals the starting population multiplied by e raised to the power of the growth rate times the elapsed time.

Follow the derivation
  1. Write the assumption as a differential equation: \(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = rN\).
  2. Separate the variables: \(\mathrm{d}N/N = r\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. Integrate both sides from \(t_0\) to \(t\): \(\ln N(t) - \ln N_0 = r(t - t_0)\).
  4. Exponentiate: \(N(t) = N_0 e^{r(t-t_0)}\).
  5. Relation to an annual percentage: if the population grows by \(g\) (e.g., 0.9 %) per year compounded annually, \(N_{t+1} = (1+g)N_t\), so \(r = \ln(1+g)\). For small \(g\), \(r \approx g\) (0.009 vs 0.00896).
Assumptions and validity

The per-capita rate \(r\) must be constant. For human populations this is never true for long: fertility falls as incomes, education (especially of women) and urbanisation rise — the demographic transition. Exponential growth is therefore a good description over one or two decades and a poor one over a century. It remains an excellent model for early growth of micro-organisms and for compound interest.

Exponent: yr⁻¹ × yr = dimensionless ✓ (an exponent must always be dimensionless); \(N\) has the unit of \(N_0\) ✓

A useful property of exponential growth is that it has a constant doubling time: however large the population, it takes the same number of years to double.

Doubling time (the "rule of 70")(1.1.2)
\[ t_{d} = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \approx \frac{70}{g_{\%}} \]

the doubling time equals the natural logarithm of two divided by the growth rate; approximately, seventy divided by the growth rate expressed in per cent per year.

In the 1960s the world population was growing by more than 2 % per year — a doubling time of about 35 years. Today's rate of just under 1 % per year corresponds to a doubling time of more than 70 years, and the rate itself is falling. That is why the UN projects a peak rather than a doubling.

Logistic growth: growth that slows down

In 1838 the Belgian mathematician Pierre-François Verhulst proposed that the per-capita growth rate should decline linearly as a population approaches a ceiling, the carrying capacity \(K\) (Verhulst, 1838). For humans the ceiling is not a fixed food limit in the Malthusian sense but the outcome of the demographic transition; the logistic curve is nevertheless a useful first description of a population whose growth decelerates.

Logistic (Verhulst) growth(1.1.3)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = r_{\max}\,N\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N(t)=\frac{K}{1+\dfrac{K-N_0}{N_0}\,e^{-r_{\max}(t-t_0)}} \]

the growth rate equals the intrinsic rate times the population times the fraction of the carrying capacity that is still "unused"; the solution is an S-shaped curve that starts exponentially and levels off at K.

\(K\)carrying capacity (asymptotic population)persons≈ 10.3–10.5 × 10⁹

\(N_0\)population at \(t_0\)persons8.2 × 10⁹ (2024)
Follow the derivation
  1. Separate variables: \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}N}{N(1-N/K)} = r_{\max}\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  2. Use partial fractions: \(\dfrac{1}{N(1-N/K)} = \dfrac{1}{N} + \dfrac{1/K}{1-N/K}\).
  3. Integrate: \(\ln N - \ln(1-N/K) = r_{\max} t + C\), i.e. \(\ln\dfrac{N}{K-N} = r_{\max}t + C'\).
  4. Use \(N(t_0)=N_0\) to find \(C'\) and solve for \(N\): \(N(t) = K/\left[1 + \frac{K-N_0}{N_0}e^{-r_{\max}(t-t_0)}\right]\).
  5. The observed per-capita growth rate is \(r_{\text{obs}} = r_{\max}(1-N/K)\). If we know today's observed rate and assume \(K\), we can back out \(r_{\max} = r_{\text{obs}}/(1-N_0/K)\) — the trick used in Figure 2.
  6. The inflection point (fastest absolute growth) is at \(N=K/2\).
Assumptions and validity

The linear decline of per-capita growth with \(N\) is an assumption of convenience, not a law. The real demographic transition depends on income, education, health care and policy, and differs strongly between regions: sub-Saharan Africa's population is still growing rapidly while Europe's and East Asia's are stable or shrinking. The UN therefore projects each country separately with probabilistic fertility, mortality and migration models (UN DESA, 2024) — the logistic curve is a teaching tool, not a forecasting method.

Demand: incomes, diets and the nutrition transition

Population is only one multiplier. As households get richer, they do not simply eat more of the same food; they change what they eat. Two empirical regularities describe this.

  • Engel's law (Ernst Engel, 1857): as income rises, the share of income spent on food falls, even though absolute spending rises. A Swedish household spends a much smaller share of its budget on food than a household in a low-income country.
  • Bennett's law (Bennett, 1941): as income rises, the share of dietary energy obtained from starchy staples — cereals, roots and tubers — falls, and the share from animal-source foods, vegetable oils, sugar, fruit and vegetables rises.

Together with urbanisation, these regularities drive the nutrition transition: a shift from diets based on staples and pulses to diets richer in meat, dairy, refined carbohydrates, sugar and fat, and increasingly in ultra-processed foods (Popkin et al., 2012; Pingali, 2007). The transition has health benefits at the start — more protein and micronutrients for previously under-nourished people — but continues into over-consumption and diet-related non-communicable disease. Bodirsky et al. (2020) projected that if current trends continue, the global prevalence of overweight and obesity will keep rising while food waste and the environmental footprint of diets grow faster than population.

Because animal-source foods require several kilograms of feed per kilogram of product (Section 1.1.6), the nutrition transition multiplies the demand for crop calories far more than it multiplies the demand for eaten calories. This is why projections of crop demand to 2050 are much higher than population growth alone would suggest. Tilman et al. (2011) projected a 100–110 % increase in global crop demand between 2005 and 2050. The World Resources Institute's Creating a Sustainable Food Future estimated a "food gap" of 7,400 trillion kcal — 56 % more crop calories in 2050 than in 2010 — together with a land gap of 593 million hectares (nearly twice the area of India) and a greenhouse-gas mitigation gap of 11 Gt CO₂e per year, the difference between the 15 Gt expected from agriculture in 2050 and a 4 Gt target consistent with holding warming well below 2 °C (Searchinger et al., 2019).

Decomposing impact: I = P × A × T for food

In 1971 Paul Ehrlich and John Holdren argued that environmental impact is the product of population, affluence (consumption per person) and technology (impact per unit consumed) — the IPAT identity (Ehrlich & Holdren, 1971). The energy-sector version is the Kaya identity used by the IPCC. For food it is natural to write:

IPAT decomposition of food-system impact(1.1.4)
\[ I = P \times \frac{F}{P} \times \frac{I}{F} = P \cdot A \cdot T \]

the total impact equals the number of people, times the food consumed (or supplied) per person, times the impact caused per unit of food.

SymbolMeaningUnit (example)
\(I\)total impact of the food systemGt CO₂e yr⁻¹, Mha of cropland, Tg N yr⁻¹ …
\(P\)populationpersons
\(F\)total food (supplied, or crop calories demanded)kcal yr⁻¹
\(A = F/P\)"affluence": food per personkcal person⁻¹ yr⁻¹
\(T = I/F\)"technology": impact intensity per unit of foodkg CO₂e kcal⁻¹, m² kcal⁻¹ …
Why it is an identity, and why that matters

The \(P\) and \(F\) terms cancel algebraically: \(P\cdot(F/P)\cdot(I/F) = I\). The equation is therefore always true — it is an accounting identity, not a causal model. Its value lies in forcing us to ask which factor we intend to change, and by how much. Its danger lies in treating the factors as independent: in reality a cheaper, more efficient technology (lower \(T\)) can raise consumption (higher \(A\)) — the rebound effect of Section 1.1.7. Choosing which unit of food to use for \(F\) (kcal, kg, g of protein) is a functional-unit decision, discussed in Lesson 1.3.

persons × (kcal person⁻¹ yr⁻¹) × (kg CO₂e kcal⁻¹) = kg CO₂e yr⁻¹ ✓

Because the identity is a product, relative changes add up in logarithms. This is the most useful form for scenario analysis:

Growth-rate (logarithmic) decomposition(1.1.5)
\[ \ln\frac{I_1}{I_0} = \ln\frac{P_1}{P_0} + \ln\frac{A_1}{A_0} + \ln\frac{T_1}{T_0}, \qquad \frac{T_1}{T_0}\bigg|_{\text{target}} = \frac{I_{\text{target}}/I_0}{(P_1/P_0)(A_1/A_0)} \]

the logarithm of the change in impact is the sum of the logarithms of the changes in population, food per person and intensity; to hit a target, the intensity must change by the target ratio divided by the product of the population and consumption ratios.

Hunger, food insecurity and obesity: the triple burden

If average food supply exceeds requirements, why are hundreds of millions of people hungry? The answer is distribution: between countries, within countries and within households. Before looking at the latest figures, it is worth understanding how the UN actually counts the hungry — because it is a beautiful example of engineering statistics applied to a humanitarian question.

Measuring undernourishment

FAO's headline indicator, the prevalence of undernourishment (PoU), is the probability that a randomly selected person habitually consumes less dietary energy than the minimum needed for a healthy, active life. It is not counted person by person. Instead, FAO models the distribution of habitual energy intake in each country with a parametric probability distribution characterised by its mean (the dietary energy consumption, from food balance sheets and household surveys) and its spread (coefficient of variation, from surveys), and integrates the part of the distribution below the minimum dietary energy requirement (MDER), which depends on the country's age and sex structure and body heights (FAO et al., 2026, methodological annex). The classical formulation uses a lognormal distribution; recent revisions use more flexible skewed distributions, but the logic is identical.

Prevalence of undernourishment (lognormal model)(1.1.6)
\[ \text{PoU} = \int_{0}^{\text{MDER}} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln \text{MDER} - \mu}{\sigma}\right),\qquad \sigma^{2} = \ln\!\left(1+\text{CV}^{2}\right),\quad \mu = \ln \text{DEC} - \frac{\sigma^{2}}{2} \]

the prevalence of undernourishment is the area under the intake distribution below the minimum requirement; for a lognormal distribution it equals the standard normal cumulative probability of the log of the requirement, measured in standard deviations from the log-mean.

Follow the derivation
  1. Assume \(\ln x\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\): \(x\) is then lognormal, which is positive and right-skewed, like real intakes.
  2. For a lognormal variable the arithmetic mean is \(E[x] = e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\) and \(\text{CV}^2 = e^{\sigma^2}-1\).
  3. Invert: \(\sigma^2 = \ln(1+\text{CV}^2)\) and \(\mu = \ln E[x] - \sigma^2/2\), with \(E[x] = \text{DEC}\).
  4. \(P(x < \text{MDER}) = P(\ln x < \ln\text{MDER}) = \Phi\big((\ln \text{MDER}-\mu)/\sigma\big)\).
  5. Number undernourished = PoU × population.
Assumptions and validity

PoU measures chronic energy deficiency only — not micronutrient deficiencies ("hidden hunger") or acute crises. It is sensitive to the CV, which is estimated from infrequent household surveys, and to revisions in food balance sheets. Each SOFI report therefore revises the whole historical series: SOFI 2025 put the 2024 prevalence at 8.2 %, whereas SOFI 2026 revised the same year to 8.1 %. Always compare numbers from the same edition. A complementary, experience-based indicator is the Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), which asks people directly about difficulties accessing food.

\(\ln\text{MDER}-\mu\) is a difference of logarithms of intakes → \(\ln(\text{MDER}/e^{\mu})\), dimensionless; divided by dimensionless \(\sigma\) → a z-score ✓
Worked example 3A country with "enough" food

A country of 50 million people has a mean dietary energy consumption of 2,400 kcal person⁻¹ d⁻¹, a coefficient of variation of 0.30 and an MDER of 1,800 kcal person⁻¹ d⁻¹. (a) Estimate the PoU and the number of undernourished people. (b) What PoU results if inequality is reduced to CV = 0.20 at the same mean?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Spread: \(\sigma^2 = \ln(1+0.30^2) = \ln 1.09 = 0.08618\); \(\sigma = 0.2936\).
  2. Log-mean: \(\mu = \ln 2400 - 0.08618/2 = 7.7832 - 0.0431 = 7.7401\).
  3. z-score: \(z = (\ln 1800 - 7.7401)/0.2936 = (7.4955 - 7.7401)/0.2936 = -0.833\).
  4. Probability: \(\Phi(-0.833) = 0.202\): PoU ≈ 20 %, i.e. about 10 million people — although the average supply exceeds the requirement by a third.
  5. Lower inequality: \(\sigma^2 = \ln 1.04 = 0.03922\), \(\sigma = 0.1980\), \(\mu = 7.7832 - 0.0196 = 7.7636\), \(z = (7.4955-7.7636)/0.1980 = -1.354\), \(\Phi = 0.088\): PoU ≈ 9 %.

Common misconception

"People are hungry because the world does not produce enough food." Global production and supply exceed requirements; hunger today is mainly caused by poverty, conflict, price shocks and unequal access. Amartya Sen's entitlement approach showed that major famines have occurred without any decline in food availability — people starved because they lost the means (income, employment, exchange) to command food (Sen, 1981). A technology that raises supply but not incomes or access may leave hunger almost unchanged.

Supply: yields and yield gaps

If demand must be met without clearing more forest, production has to rise on existing farmland. The key concept is the yield gap: the difference between what a crop could yield in a given place and what farmers actually harvest. Crop scientists distinguish three yield levels (van Ittersum et al., 2013), shown in Figure 5.

  • Potential yield \(Y_p\): the yield of an adapted cultivar grown with no limitation of water or nutrients and with pests, diseases and weeds fully controlled. It is set only by solar radiation, temperature, CO₂ concentration and the crop's genetics — the defining factors. It is the relevant benchmark for irrigated crops.
  • Water-limited yield \(Y_w\): as \(Y_p\), but limited by the water supply from rainfall and soil storage. It is the benchmark for rain-fed crops, including most Swedish cereals.
  • Actual yield \(Y_a\): the average yield farmers actually achieve, lowered further by nutrient shortages (limiting factors) and by weeds, pests, diseases and pollutants (reducing factors).
Yield gap and production gain(1.1.7)
\[ Y_g = Y_w - Y_a, \qquad \Delta Q = A\,\big(f\,Y_w - Y_a\big), \qquad \frac{\Delta Q}{Q} = \frac{f\,Y_w}{Y_a} - 1 \]

the yield gap is the water-limited (or potential) yield minus the actual yield; closing the gap up to a fraction f of the water-limited yield on an area A adds A times (f Y_w minus Y_a) of production, a relative increase of f Y_w over Y_a minus one.

Assumptions and validity

\(Y_p\) and \(Y_w\) cannot be measured directly; they are estimated with crop simulation models calibrated on field experiments, or from the best farmers' yields in similar climates. The Global Yield Gap Atlas (yieldgap.org) provides such estimates with a documented, bottom-up protocol. Closing yield gaps usually needs more inputs (fertiliser, irrigation, crop protection), which can raise emissions and nutrient losses unless efficiency improves at the same time — the theme of Lesson 11.1.

How big are the global gaps? Mueller et al. (2012) combined yield statistics with climate, irrigation and fertiliser data for 17 major crops and found that closing yield gaps to 100 % of attainable yields could increase production by 45–70 % for most crops — and that eliminating nutrient overuse in places like parts of China, India and the USA would still allow about a 30 % increase in major cereal production. The largest relative gaps are in sub-Saharan Africa and Eastern Europe, where fertiliser use and irrigation are low. But yields are not rising fast enough to close them: Ray et al. (2013) found that yields of maize, rice, wheat and soybean were increasing at 1.6, 1.0, 0.9 and 1.3 % per year (non-compounding), well below the 2.4 % per year needed to double production by 2050, and Grassini et al. (2013) showed that yields have plateaued in several of the world's most productive regions.

Worked example 4Closing the gap in a rain-fed wheat region

A rain-fed winter-wheat region of 120,000 ha has a simulated water-limited yield of 9.0 t ha⁻¹ and an actual average yield of 6.5 t ha⁻¹ (illustrative values). (a) Calculate the yield gap and the relative yield \(Y_a/Y_w\). (b) How much extra wheat could be produced if farmers reached 80 % of \(Y_w\)? (c) How much land would that "save" if the extra wheat were otherwise grown elsewhere at 6.5 t ha⁻¹?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Gap: \(Y_g = 9.0 - 6.5 = 2.5\) t ha⁻¹; relative yield \(6.5/9.0 = 0.72\) (72 %).
  2. Exploitable level: \(0.8\times9.0 = 7.2\) t ha⁻¹, so the exploitable gap is \(7.2 - 6.5 = 0.7\) t ha⁻¹ — only 28 % of the full gap.
  3. Extra production (Eq. 1.1.7): \(\Delta Q = 120\,000\ \text{ha}\times0.7\ \text{t ha}^{-1} = 84\,000\) t yr⁻¹, a relative increase of \(7.2/6.5-1 = 10.8\,\%\).
  4. Equivalent land: \(84\,000/6.5 = 12\,900\) ha of wheat land elsewhere.
  5. Sanity check: a 10 % gain is modest compared with the 45–70 % global potential, as expected for a region already at 72 % of \(Y_w\): most of the remaining potential is in low-yield regions.
Yg = 2.5 t ha⁻¹; +84,000 t yr⁻¹ (+10.8 %); ≈ 12,900 ha "saved"

From field to fork: feed, losses and waste

Not every calorie harvested is eaten by a person. Three large "leaks" separate the field from the fork.

1. Feed. Cassidy et al. (2013) traced the calories of the world's crops and found that only 55 % directly feed people; 36 % are used as animal feed and 9 % for biofuels and other industrial uses. Of the feed calories, only about 12 % return to the human diet as meat, milk and eggs. Reallocating crops away from feed and biofuels could, in principle, raise available food calories by up to 70 % — enough for about 4 billion more people. An important nuance: most livestock feed by mass is not human-edible. Mottet et al. (2017) estimated that 86 % of global livestock feed intake (dry matter) consists of grass, leaves, crop residues and by-products that people cannot eat. The feed–food competition is therefore concentrated in grain- and soy-fed systems (pigs, poultry, feedlot cattle), while grazing ruminants convert inedible biomass — at a high land and methane cost.

2. Food loss. FAO defines food loss as the decrease in food quantity or quality from harvest up to, but not including, retail. The State of Food and Agriculture 2019 estimated that about 14 % of food produced is lost in this way (FAO, 2019); the latest SDG indicator puts it at 13.2 % in 2021. Losses are largest where cold chains, storage and processing are weak — a fundamentally engineering problem (drying, cooling, packaging, logistics).

3. Food waste. Food waste occurs at retail, food service and household level. The UNEP Food Waste Index Report 2024 estimated 1.05 billion tonnes of food waste in 2022 (including inedible parts such as bones and peels) — 132 kg per person per year, almost one fifth (19 %) of the food available to consumers. Households generated 60 % of it, about 79 kg per person (UNEP, 2024). In the EU, Eurostat estimates about 130 kg of food waste per inhabitant in 2023, 53 % of it from households; in Sweden, Naturvårdsverket reported about 1.2 million tonnes of food waste in 2022, 117 kg per person, of which households accounted for 56 kg (48 %) (Naturvårdsverket, 2024).

Calorie delivery efficiency of the food chain(1.1.8)
\[ \eta_{\text{chain}} = \frac{E_{\text{eaten}}}{E_{\text{crop}}} = \big(s_{\text{food}} + s_{\text{feed}}\,\eta_{\text{feed}}\big)\,(1-L)\,(1-W) \]

the fraction of harvested crop calories that is eaten equals the share used directly as food plus the share used as feed times the feed-to-food conversion efficiency, multiplied by the fraction not lost before retail and the fraction not wasted after it.

Assumptions and validity

This is a deliberately simple, multiplicative chain. The shares come from different studies, years and definitions (Cassidy's feed shares refer to crop calories around 2000; the loss and waste fractions to 2021–2022, partly measured in mass and including inedible parts), so the result is an order-of-magnitude estimate, not an official statistic. The remaining 9 % of crop calories used for biofuels and industry are treated as not eaten.

Worked example 5Halve waste or change the menu?

Using Eq. 1.1.8 with the global defaults (\(s_{\text{food}}=0.55\), \(s_{\text{feed}}=0.36\), \(\eta_{\text{feed}}=0.12\), \(L=0.132\), \(W=0.19\)): (a) compute \(\eta_{\text{chain}}\). (b) Compute the relative increase in calories eaten from halving consumer-level waste (SDG target 12.3). (c) Compare with shifting one third of feed calories to direct food use.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Baseline: \(0.55 + 0.36\times0.12 = 0.5932\); \(\times(1-0.132) = 0.5149\); \(\times(1-0.19) = 0.4171\). About 42 % of crop calories are eaten.
  2. Halve waste (\(W = 0.095\)): \(0.5149\times0.905 = 0.4660\); increase \(0.4660/0.4171 - 1 = +11.7\,\%\).
  3. Shift one third of feed (\(s_{\text{feed}}=0.24\), \(s_{\text{food}}=0.67\)): \(0.67 + 0.24\times0.12 = 0.6988\); \(\times0.868\times0.81 = 0.4913\); increase \(+17.8\,\%\).
  4. Interpretation: both are large compared with a decade of yield growth (≈1 % per year). Neither requires new technology — but both require behaviour, markets and policy to change. Note also that the "freed" feed must be human-edible (grain, soy) for the shift to be real.

EU and Sweden

The EU has made food-waste reduction legally binding. Directive (EU) 2025/1892, amending the Waste Framework Directive, was adopted in September 2025 and entered into force on 16 October 2025. It requires each Member State to reduce, by 31 December 2030, food waste in processing and manufacturing by 10 % and food waste per capita in retail, restaurants, food services and households by 30 %, both relative to the 2021–2023 average. For Sweden, with about 117 kg of food waste per person in 2022, this is a concrete engineering and behavioural target.

Land sparing, land sharing and the rebound problem

Where should the extra food come from? Agriculture already covers about 43 % of the world's ice- and desert-free land (Poore & Nemecek, 2018), and most remaining land that could be converted is forest, savannah or peatland of high value for biodiversity and carbon. The land needed for a given food demand is simply demand divided by yield — the land version of the IPAT identity:

Land requirement and the rebound effect(1.1.9)
\[ A = \frac{Q}{Y}, \qquad \frac{A_1}{A_0} = \frac{Q_1/Q_0}{Y_1/Y_0}, \qquad R = 1 - \frac{\Delta A_{\text{actual}}}{\Delta A_{\text{potential}}} \]

the land area needed equals production divided by yield; the relative change in area is the relative change in production divided by the relative change in yield; the rebound fraction is one minus the actual land saving divided by the saving that would have occurred had demand stayed constant.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(A\)land area in productionha
\(Q\)production (= demand, in a closed system)t yr⁻¹ or kcal yr⁻¹
\(Y\)yieldt ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹
\(\Delta A_{\text{potential}}\)land saving at constant \(Q\): \(A_0(1 - Y_0/Y_1)\)ha
\(\Delta A_{\text{actual}}\)land saving that actually occurs: \(A_0 - A_1\)ha
\(R\)rebound fraction (0 = none; 1 = saving fully eaten up; > 1 = "backfire")–
Follow the derivation
  1. By definition \(Y = Q/A\), so \(A = Q/Y\) at both times; divide to get the ratio.
  2. If demand were fixed (\(Q_1 = Q_0\)), a yield increase from \(Y_0\) to \(Y_1\) would save \(A_0 - A_0Y_0/Y_1 = A_0(1-Y_0/Y_1)\).
  3. If higher yields lower prices and demand responds (or the region captures export markets), \(Q_1 > Q_0\) and the actual saving is smaller: \(A_0 - A_0(Q_1/Q_0)(Y_0/Y_1)\).
  4. The rebound fraction compares the two savings. When \(Q_1/Q_0 \ge Y_1/Y_0\), \(R \ge 1\): land use does not fall at all (the Jevons paradox).
t yr⁻¹ ÷ t ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹ = ha ✓; \(R\) is ha ÷ ha = dimensionless ✓

The evidence that rebound is real is strong. Rudel et al. (2009) found little evidence that the yield increases of 1970–2005 spared land at a national scale; cultivated area usually stayed the same or grew. Hertel et al. (2014) showed that when a region with good market access raises its yields, it can capture export markets, so a future African Green Revolution could increase global cropland and CO₂ emissions. Pellegrini & Fernández (2018) concluded that the green revolution fits the Jevons paradox: efficiency gains in land and energy use did not by themselves lead to resource savings — realising them "depends on bold political decisions". Lambin & Meyfroidt (2011) describe how globalisation displaces land use across borders, so that a country can appear to "spare" land while importing the footprint.

Sparing or sharing?

Green et al. (2005) framed a now classic question for conservation: to produce a given amount of food, is it better for wild species to farm a small area intensively and spare the rest as natural habitat (land sparing), or to farm a large area in a wildlife-friendly, lower-yielding way (land sharing)? Their answer depends on how the population density of each species responds to farming intensity — the density–yield function. If a species' density collapses as soon as land is farmed even lightly, sparing wins; if it tolerates moderate farming, sharing may win. Phalan et al. (2011), measuring birds and trees in Ghana and India, found that most species did better under land sparing, because many forest species cannot survive even on low-intensity farmland. Figure 7 lets you explore the logic.

Worked example 6When a yield boost does not save land

A new cultivar raises yields in an exporting region by 25 %. Because the region's grain becomes cheaper, it gains market share and its production rises by 15 %. The region farms 2.0 million ha. (a) How much land would have been saved at constant production? (b) How much is actually saved? (c) What is the rebound fraction?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Potential saving: \(A_0(1 - 1/1.25) = 2.0\times0.20 = 0.40\) million ha.
  2. Actual area (Eq. 1.1.9): \(A_1 = 2.0\times1.15/1.25 = 1.84\) million ha, so the actual saving is 0.16 million ha.
  3. Rebound: \(R = 1 - 0.16/0.40 = 0.60\): 60 % of the potential land saving is "eaten" by extra production.
  4. Global view: if the extra 15 % displaces production elsewhere, land may be spared there instead — or, if lower prices raise total consumption, not at all. Land sparing requires policy (zoning, protected areas, deforestation-free supply chains) coupled with yield growth; it does not happen automatically.
0.40 Mha potential; 0.16 Mha actual; R = 60 %

The toolbox — and why technology alone is not enough

How can the gaps be closed? Table 2 sorts the main levers into demand-side and supply-side options, following the WRI "menu" (Searchinger et al., 2019) and the modelling of Springmann et al. (2018), which found that no single measure keeps the food system within environmental limits, but a combination of dietary change, technological and management improvements and halving food loss and waste can. Gerten et al. (2020) reached a similar conclusion for four terrestrial planetary boundaries: if the boundaries were strictly respected, today's food system could provide a balanced diet for only about 3.4 billion people, but a transformation of both production (redistributed cropland, better water and nutrient management) and consumption (less waste, dietary change) could feed 10.2 billion.

Why is technology alone insufficient? Five reasons recur throughout this course:

  1. Rebound. Efficiency lowers costs, and lower costs raise consumption or shift production elsewhere (Section 1.1.7).
  2. Distribution and access. Hunger is driven mainly by poverty, conflict and prices, not by global scarcity (Section 1.1.4). A technology that is profitable only for affluent urban consumers — such as many indoor-farmed salads — does little for the 2.69 billion who cannot afford a healthy diet.
  3. Scale and time. A promising technology must grow from pilot to a substantial share of the market within one or two decades to matter for 2050; diffusion takes time (Lesson 1.4, Lesson 14.4).
  4. Burden shifting. A system that saves land may use more energy (vertical farms), more water or rare materials; only a life-cycle view reveals this (Lesson 1.3).
  5. Acceptance and governance. Consumers must want the product and regulators must allow it (Lesson 13.2, Lesson 14.1).

The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2026 (released 21 July 2026) reports that global hunger declined for the third consecutive year, to about 645 million people in 2025, while the cost of a healthy diet rose to 4.28 PPP dollars per person per day and the report's theme was understanding and addressing the high cost of a healthy diet (FAO et al., 2026). Watch both indicators during your career: they measure whether the food system is moving in the right direction.

History

In 1798 Thomas Malthus argued that population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, so that famine would inevitably check population. Two centuries of yield growth — mechanisation, synthetic nitrogen fertiliser (the Haber–Bosch process), breeding and the green revolution — proved the arithmetic wrong: world food supply per person has risen while population grew more than fivefold. The modern concern is different: not that we cannot produce enough, but that the way we produce it is pushing the Earth system beyond safe limits — the subject of the next lesson.

Key terms

Food system
All actors and activities involved in producing, processing, distributing, consuming and disposing of food, together with their drivers and their environmental, health and social outcomes.
Dietary energy supply
Food energy available for human consumption per person per day, from FAO food balance sheets (kcal person⁻¹ d⁻¹); it includes food later wasted.
Prevalence of undernourishment (PoU)
Estimated share of a population whose habitual energy intake is below the minimum dietary energy requirement; the UN's headline hunger indicator.
Triple burden of malnutrition
The coexistence of undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies and overweight/obesity in a population.
Nutrition transition
The shift, with rising income and urbanisation, from staple-based diets to diets richer in animal products, sugar, fats and processed foods.
Bennett's law
The empirical regularity that the share of dietary energy from starchy staples declines as income rises.
IPAT identity
Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology; an accounting identity used to decompose changes in environmental impact.
Potential yield (Yp)
Yield of an adapted cultivar without water, nutrient or biotic limitations, determined by radiation, temperature, CO₂ and genotype.
Water-limited yield (Yw)
Potential yield further limited by water supply; the benchmark for rain-fed crops.
Yield gap
Difference between potential (or water-limited) yield and actual farm yield; the exploitable gap extends to ~75–85 % of Yp/Yw.
Food loss / food waste
Loss: decrease in food quantity or quality from harvest up to (not including) retail. Waste: food discarded at retail, food service and households.
Feed–food competition
Use of human-edible crops (grain, soy) as livestock feed, reducing the calories available for direct human consumption.
Land sparing / land sharing
Sparing: intensive farming on less land with natural habitat protected elsewhere. Sharing: lower-intensity, wildlife-friendly farming over a larger area.
Rebound effect (Jevons paradox)
The part of an expected resource saving from an efficiency gain that is offset by increased consumption or production; a rebound above 100 % is called backfire.
