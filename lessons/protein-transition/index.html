The protein transition: nutrition and footprints

A gram of protein from a steak, a pea, a fungus or a mealworm is not the same gram. It differs in which amino acids it carries, how much of it your gut can absorb, which vitamins and minerals come with it — and how much land, feed and greenhouse gas it cost to produce. This lesson gives you the quantitative tools to compare protein sources fairly: requirements, amino-acid scores, PDCAAS and DIAAS, feed-conversion efficiencies and footprints per 100 g of protein. With them you can judge the claims made for every alternative protein in the rest of this module.

What you will be able to do

  • Calculate individual and population protein requirements from body mass using the WHO/FAO/UNU (2007) safe level, and explain the difference between an average requirement and a safe level (LO3).
  • Compute amino-acid scores, PDCAAS and DIAAS from composition and digestibility data, including for protein blends, and explain why DIAAS replaced PDCAAS (LO3).
  • Quantify feed-conversion ratios and protein-conversion efficiencies of livestock and relate them to land use and emissions (LO1, LO3).
  • Compare greenhouse-gas and land footprints per 100 g of protein, and discuss how the choice of functional unit changes the ranking (LO1, LO7).
  • Identify the nutrients that change when protein sources are substituted (vitamin B12, iron, zinc, iodine, omega-3 fatty acids) and evaluate policy instruments and the ultra-processing debate (LO6, LO7).

Why protein sits at the centre of the food transition

Of all the nutrients in our diet, protein is the one most tightly coupled to the environmental footprint of the food system. The reason is simple: in high-income countries most dietary protein comes from animals, and animals are converters, not producers. They eat plant protein and return a fraction of it as meat, milk and eggs. Poore and Nemecek (2018), in the largest meta-analysis of food life-cycle assessments to date (about 38 700 farms in 119 countries), found that meat, aquaculture, eggs and dairy occupy about 83 % of the world's farmland and contribute 56–58 % of food's emissions, while providing only 37 % of our protein and 18 % of our calories. At the same time, roughly a third of all crop calories grown globally (36 % according to Cassidy et al., 2013) are fed to animals rather than eaten by people.

This imbalance is why "the protein transition" — shifting the balance between animal and plant (or other alternative) protein — has become a policy objective in several European countries. High-income diets currently obtain about two-thirds of their protein from animal sources: Drewnowski and Hooker (2025) put the typical animal-to-plant (A:P) protein ratio at around 65:35, and many transition plans aim at 50:50 or lower. Yet the same authors show that, globally, the opposite transition is under way: as incomes rise in low- and middle-income countries, diets include more animal protein. Any serious engineering of the protein supply must therefore be quantitative about both sides of the equation — what people need and what each production route costs.

Big idea

To compare protein sources fairly you need three numbers per source: how much protein it supplies, how good that protein is (its amino-acid profile and digestibility), and what it costs the planet per unit of protein. None of the three is sufficient on its own.

How much protein do we need?

Proteins in the body are continuously broken down and rebuilt: an adult turns over several hundred grams of body protein per day, far more than is eaten. Most of the released amino acids are re-used, but some are oxidised and their nitrogen excreted as urea. Dietary protein must replace these obligatory losses and supply amino acids for growth, pregnancy and lactation. The classical method for measuring the requirement is the nitrogen balance: feed volunteers graded protein intakes and find the intake at which nitrogen intake equals nitrogen losses (urine, faeces, skin and other routes).

The joint WHO/FAO/UNU expert consultation re-analysed the nitrogen-balance studies and concluded that for healthy adults the median (average) requirement is 0.66 g of good-quality protein per kilogram body mass per day, and that a safe level of intake — one that meets the needs of nearly all (97.5 %) healthy adults — is 0.83 g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹ (WHO/FAO/UNU, 2007). "Good-quality protein" here means protein with the digestibility and amino-acid balance of milk or egg protein; for poorer proteins the amount must be corrected, as you will see in Section 12.1.4.

Daily protein requirement from body mass(12.1.1)
\[ P_{\text{req}} = r\,m, \qquad r_{\text{safe}} = r_{50}\,e^{1.96\,\sigma_{\ln}} \approx 0.66\times e^{1.96\times0.12} \approx 0.83\ \text{g kg}^{-1}\,\text{d}^{-1} \]

the protein a person needs per day equals the requirement per kilogram multiplied by body mass. The safe level lies at the 97.5th percentile of the distribution of individual requirements: the median multiplied by e to the power 1.96 times the spread of the (log-normal) distribution.

Follow the derivation
  1. Individuals differ in their requirement; the nitrogen-balance data are approximately log-normally distributed, i.e. \(\ln r\) is normally distributed with mean \(\ln r_{50}\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_{\ln}\).
  2. For a normal distribution, 97.5 % of values lie below the mean + 1.96 standard deviations. So 97.5 % of individuals have \(\ln r \le \ln r_{50} + 1.96\,\sigma_{\ln}\).
  3. Taking the exponential of both sides: \(r_{97.5} = r_{50}\,e^{1.96\sigma_{\ln}}\). With \(\sigma_{\ln}\approx0.12\): \(e^{0.235}=1.265\), and \(0.66\times1.265 = 0.835 \approx 0.83\) g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹.
  4. The ratio 0.83/0.66 ≈ 1.26 is therefore the "safety margin" that covers individual variation. It is not a margin for poor protein quality or for illness.
  5. Multiply by body mass to get grams per day: a 70 kg adult needs \(0.83\times70 = 58\) g d⁻¹ at the safe level.
Assumptions and validity

Valid for healthy adults in energy balance, eating protein of high digestibility and amino-acid quality. Use a healthy body mass (not actual mass in obesity). Requirements per kilogram are higher for infants and children (growth), in pregnancy and lactation, and many expert bodies — including the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations — advise older adults to eat more protein to help preserve muscle mass. The safe level is a population reference, not an optimum for athletes.

Nutrition scientists also express protein as a share of dietary energy. One gram of protein yields about 17 kJ (4 kcal) of metabolisable energy, so a daily intake can be converted to an energy percentage (E%). This is how the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations (NNR 2023) express their recommended range of 10–20 E% for adults (Blomhoff et al., 2023). Actual intakes are well within this range: adult protein intake in the Nordic and Baltic countries is about 15–19 E% (Geirsdóttir & Pajari, 2023) — the typical Nordic adult eats considerably more protein than the safe level.

\[ \text{E\%}_{\text{prot}} = 100\times\frac{e_{\text{p}}\,P}{E_{\text{d}}} \]

the percentage of dietary energy from protein equals 100 times the energy per gram of protein times the grams of protein eaten, divided by the total daily energy intake.

Indispensable amino acids and the amino-acid score

Proteins are chains of 20 amino acids. Nine of them cannot be synthesised by the human body at a rate that meets demand and must come from the diet: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. They are called indispensable (or essential) amino acids (IAA). Two dispensable amino acids are made from indispensable ones — cysteine from methionine and tyrosine from phenylalanine — so requirements are stated for the sums "sulfur amino acids" (SAA = Met + Cys) and "aromatic amino acids" (AAA = Phe + Tyr).

Because the body cannot store surplus amino acids, protein synthesis proceeds only as far as the scarcest indispensable amino acid allows, relative to need. This is exactly Liebig's law of the minimum, familiar from plant nutrition (Lesson 2.3): the shortest stave of the barrel sets the water level (Figure 2). The other amino acids in excess are oxidised for energy and their nitrogen is excreted.

Amino-acid score(12.1.3)
\[ \text{AAS} = \min_{i\,\in\,\text{IAA}} \left(\frac{c_i}{r_i}\right) \]

the amino-acid score is the smallest of the ratios between the content of each indispensable amino acid in the test protein and its content in the reference pattern. The amino acid that gives the smallest ratio is the first-limiting amino acid.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(c_i\)content of indispensable amino acid \(i\) in the test proteinmg g⁻¹ protein

AASamino-acid score (often ×100 and expressed in %)–
Follow the reasoning
  1. Suppose a person eats exactly the reference amount of protein, \(P\) grams. The intake of amino acid \(i\) is \(c_i P\); the need is \(r_i P\).
  2. The fraction of the need met for amino acid \(i\) is \(c_iP/(r_iP) = c_i/r_i\).
  3. Protein synthesis requires all IAA simultaneously in fixed proportions, so the fraction of the protein that can be used for synthesis is limited by the worst-supplied amino acid: \(\min_i (c_i/r_i)\).
  4. Corollary: to meet all IAA needs with a protein of score AAS < 1, one must eat \(1/\text{AAS}\) times as much of it (if it were fully digestible).
(mg g⁻¹)/(mg g⁻¹) = dimensionless ✓
Common misconception

"Plant proteins are incomplete." Every plant protein contains all nine indispensable amino acids. What differs is their proportions (cereals are low in lysine, legumes in sulfur amino acids) and their digestibility. Gorissen et al. (2018) measured commercial protein isolates from ten plant sources: their indispensable-amino-acid content was lower (e.g., 21 % of protein for oat and lupin, 22 % for wheat) than in whey (43 %), milk (39 %), casein (34 %) or egg (32 %) protein — lower, but not absent. Blending sources fixes most of the gap.

Protein quality: from PDCAAS to DIAAS

The amino-acid score describes the composition of a protein, but part of any protein escapes digestion. Two correction methods have dominated the last three decades.

PDCAAS — the protein digestibility-corrected amino-acid score — was recommended by an FAO/WHO expert consultation (FAO/WHO, 1991). It multiplies the amino-acid score by the true faecal digestibility of crude protein (measured in rats), and truncates the result at 1.0 (100 %), on the argument that a protein cannot supply more than is needed.

Protein digestibility-corrected amino-acid score(12.1.4)
\[ \text{PDCAAS} = \min\!\left(1,\ \text{AAS}\times \text{TD}_{\text{f}}\right) \]

PDCAAS equals the amino-acid score multiplied by the true faecal digestibility of the protein, but never more than one.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(\text{TD}_{\text{f}}\)true faecal digestibility of crude protein (N), corrected for endogenous N–0.85–0.97
AASamino-acid score (Eq. 12.1.3); in the 1991 method based on the preschool-child pattern–0.4–1.6
Why the method was criticised
  1. Faecal, not ileal. Amino acids are absorbed in the small intestine. Nitrogen that reaches the large intestine is metabolised by bacteria; bacterial nitrogen that disappears there does not supply amino acids to the host. Faecal digestibility therefore overestimates how much of the protein is actually absorbed as amino acids.
  2. One digestibility for all amino acids. Individual amino acids differ in digestibility; lysine in heat-treated foods can be damaged (Maillard reactions) and become unavailable.
  3. Truncation. Capping at 1.0 hides the surplus of high-quality proteins that can compensate for poorer proteins in a mixed diet.
dimensionless × dimensionless = dimensionless ✓

An FAO expert consultation therefore recommended a new method, the digestible indispensable amino-acid score (DIAAS) (FAO, 2013). DIAAS uses the true ileal digestibility of each indispensable amino acid — measured at the end of the small intestine, preferably in humans, otherwise in growing pigs or rats — and is not truncated when calculated for a single food.

Digestible indispensable amino-acid score(12.1.5)
\[ \text{DIAAS}\ (\%) = 100\times\min_{i\,\in\,\text{IAA}}\left(\frac{d_i\,c_i}{r_i}\right) \]

DIAAS is one hundred times the smallest ratio between the digestible content of each indispensable amino acid (content times its own ileal digestibility) and the reference pattern.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(d_i\)true ileal digestibility of amino acid \(i\)–0.75–0.98
\(c_i\)content of amino acid \(i\)mg g⁻¹ proteinTable 1 scale
\(r_i\)reference pattern (FAO 2013, age-specific)mg g⁻¹ proteinTable 1
Follow the derivation
  1. Of \(c_i\) mg of amino acid \(i\) per gram of protein eaten, only the fraction \(d_i\) is absorbed before the large intestine. The digestible content is \(d_ic_i\).
  2. Compare with need exactly as in Eq. 12.1.3: the fraction of the need for \(i\) that is met is \(d_ic_i/r_i\).
  3. The protein is only as good as its worst-supplied amino acid: take the minimum over the nine IAA (with SAA and AAA as sums).
  4. Multiply by 100 to obtain a percentage. FAO (2013) proposed that foods with DIAAS < 75 carry no protein claim, 75–99 qualify as "good" (source) quality and ≥ 100 as "excellent/high" quality.
Assumptions and validity

True ileal digestibility is difficult to measure in humans; most published DIAAS values come from growing pigs (e.g., Mathai et al., 2017) or rats (Rutherfurd et al., 2015), and they depend on processing (cooking, extrusion, heat damage). DIAAS depends on the reference pattern chosen: the same protein scores lower against the more demanding 6 mo–3 y pattern. In a mixed diet, the relevant score is that of the whole meal or diet, not of each food.

(– × mg g⁻¹)/(mg g⁻¹) = dimensionless; × 100 → % ✓

The differences between the methods are not academic. In pig studies, PDCAAS-type values for skimmed milk powder, pea protein concentrate, soy protein isolate, soy flour and wheat were significantly greater than DIAAS values — PDCAAS overestimates the quality of several plant proteins in particular (Mathai et al., 2017). In rats, DIAAS values ranged from 0.01 for a corn-based breakfast cereal to 1.18 for a milk protein concentrate (Rutherfurd et al., 2015). Complementation: why blends beat single sources

Cereals are limited by lysine but rich in sulfur amino acids; legumes are the reverse. In a blend, each partner fills the other's gap. Because both scores and digestibilities are defined per gram of protein, a blend must be computed on a protein basis:

DIAAS of a protein blend(12.1.6)
\[ \text{DIAAS}_{\text{mix}} = 100\times\min_i\ \frac{\sum_k w_k\,d_{k,i}\,c_{k,i}}{r_i},\qquad \sum_k w_k = 1 \]

the score of a mixture is found by first adding the digestible amino-acid contents of the ingredients, each weighted by its share of the total protein, and only then dividing by the reference and taking the minimum.

\(w_k\)share of total protein supplied by ingredient \(k\) (not share of mass!)–

Follow the derivation
  1. Take 1 g of blended protein. Ingredient \(k\) contributes \(w_k\) g of it, carrying \(w_kd_{k,i}c_{k,i}\) mg of digestible amino acid \(i\).
  2. Sum over ingredients: digestible amino acid \(i\) per gram of blend protein is \(\sum_k w_kd_{k,i}c_{k,i}\).
  3. Now apply Eq. 12.1.5 to the blend. Note that the minimum must be taken after summing: \(\min(\sum)\ge\sum(\min)\), which is exactly why complementation works — a blend can score higher than either ingredient.
  4. To convert a mass ratio to a protein ratio: \(w_A = m_Ax_A/(m_Ax_A+m_Bx_B)\), where \(x\) is protein content (g g⁻¹).

From feed to food: conversion efficiencies

An animal is a protein converter with an efficiency far below one. Two indicators describe its performance. The feed-conversion ratio (FCR) is the mass of feed eaten per unit of live-weight gain; it is the number farmers use, because feed is their largest cost. The protein-conversion efficiency (PCE) is the fraction of feed protein that ends up in edible product; it is the number the food-system analyst needs.

Feed-conversion ratio and protein-conversion efficiency(12.1.7)
\[ \text{FCR} = \frac{m_{\text{feed}}}{\Delta m_{\text{LW}}},\qquad \text{PCE} = \frac{m_{\text{edible}}\,x_{\text{e}}}{m_{\text{feed}}\,x_{\text{f}}} = \frac{y_{\text{e}}\,x_{\text{e}}}{\text{FCR}\;x_{\text{f}}} \]

the feed-conversion ratio is feed eaten divided by live-weight gained. The protein-conversion efficiency is the protein in the edible product divided by the protein in the feed; it equals the edible yield times the protein content of the edible product, divided by the FCR times the protein content of the feed.

  2. Divide: \(\text{PCE} = y_{\text{e}}\Delta m_{\text{LW}}x_{\text{e}}/(m_{\text{feed}}x_{\text{f}})\).
  3. Substitute \(m_{\text{feed}} = \text{FCR}\,\Delta m_{\text{LW}}\): \(\Delta m_{\text{LW}}\) cancels, giving \(y_{\text{e}}x_{\text{e}}/(\text{FCR}\,x_{\text{f}})\).

Figure 4 seems to settle the matter: beef wastes 95 % of its feed protein. But this ignores what the animals eat. Mottet et al. (2017) estimated that the world's livestock consume about 6 billion tonnes of feed dry matter per year, of which 86 % consists of materials that humans cannot or do not eat — grass, crop residues, by-products such as oilseed cakes and bran. Measured in human-edible feed, producing 1 kg of boneless meat required on average 2.8 kg of human-edible feed in ruminant systems and 3.2 kg in monogastric (pig and poultry) systems. A cow grazing land that cannot be cropped does not compete with people for food; a feedlot steer eating maize does. The useful indicator is therefore the human-edible protein-conversion ratio: edible protein out per unit of human-edible protein in. Values above one mean the animal adds to the human food supply.

Careful with FCR comparisons

Feed-conversion ratios are quoted on very different bases: as-fed feed or dry matter; per kg live weight, carcass weight or edible meat; for the growing animal only or for the whole herd including breeding stock. A broiler FCR of "1.6" and an insect FCR of "1.7" are only comparable when both are expressed on the same basis — ideally as edible protein per unit of feed protein. You will meet the same pitfall in Lesson 12.5.

  3. (b) Human-edible protein in: \(0.5\times0.34 = 0.17\) kg. Ratio \(= 0.1155/0.17 = 0.68\). The chicken returns 0.68 kg of edible protein per kg of human-edible protein it eats: it is a net consumer of human-edible protein, although much less so than the raw PCE suggests.

Footprints per 100 g of protein

Life-cycle assessment (Lesson 1.3) expresses impacts per functional unit. For protein-rich foods, "per kilogram of product" is misleading because a kilogram of dry peas contains about ten times as much protein as a kilogram of milk. Poore and Nemecek (2018) therefore also reported impacts per 100 g of protein — the functional unit used in Figure 5.

Converting a footprint to the protein functional unit(12.1.8)
\[ F_{100\,\text{g prot}} = F_{\text{kg}}\times\frac{0.1\ \text{kg protein}}{x_{\text{p}}} \]

the footprint per hundred grams of protein equals the footprint per kilogram of product multiplied by one tenth of a kilogram of protein, divided by the protein mass fraction of the product.

Follow the derivation
  1. 1 kg of product carries \(x_{\text{p}}\) kg of protein, so the product mass that carries 1 kg of protein is \(1/x_{\text{p}}\) kg.
  2. The mass carrying 0.1 kg (100 g) of protein is \(0.1/x_{\text{p}}\) kg.
  3. Its footprint is \(F_{\text{kg}}\times0.1/x_{\text{p}}\).
Assumptions and validity

The protein unit ignores all other nutrients: milk is penalised (it also supplies calcium, iodine, B12 and energy), and nuts look excellent partly because Poore and Nemecek allocate a carbon credit to orchards that replace cropland. Always state the functional unit and consider a second one (energy, a nutrient-density index, or a whole diet).

(kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ product) × (kg protein)/(kg protein kg⁻¹ product) = kg CO₂e per 0.1 kg protein ✓

Three features of Figure 5 deserve attention. First, the range spans more than two orders of magnitude: 100 g of protein from beef-herd beef is associated with about 50 kg CO₂e and 164 m² of land for a year, the same protein from peas with 0.44 kg CO₂e and 3.4 m². Second, the ranking differs between indicators — farmed prawns have high emissions (from mangrove conversion and energy use) but little land, whereas lamb uses the most land of all. Third, beef from the dairy herd has about a third of the emissions of beef-herd beef, because the cow's emissions are shared (allocated) between milk and meat.

Poore and Nemecek also showed that the variation between producers of the same food is huge, yet even low-impact animal products typically exceeded the impacts of vegetable substitutes. This is the quantitative basis of the claim that what we eat matters more than how it is produced — for protein at least.

Quality-adjusted protein footprint(12.1.9)
\[ F^{*} = \frac{F_{100\,\text{g prot}}}{\min\!\left(1,\ \text{DIAAS}/100\right)} \]

the footprint per hundred grams of "reference-quality" protein equals the footprint per hundred grams of protein divided by the DIAAS expressed as a fraction, capped at one.

Why cap at 1, and what are the limits?

From the reasoning behind Eq. 12.1.3, a protein with score \(S<1\) must be eaten in \(1/S\) times the amount to meet all IAA needs, so its footprint per unit of useful protein rises by \(1/S\). A score above 1 does not make the protein "worth more" to someone whose needs are met, hence the cap. The adjustment is conservative: in a real diet, limiting amino acids of one food are compensated by others (Eq. 12.1.6), so single-food adjustments overstate the penalty for plant proteins.

Reveal the reasoning

Iodine illustrates the risk well. Bath et al. (2017) analysed 47 milk-alternative drinks sold in the UK: the unfortified drinks contained only about 2 % of the iodine concentration of cows' milk. A consumer who replaces milk with an unfortified oat drink may therefore lose a major iodine source without noticing — relevant for pregnant women in particular, since iodine is critical for foetal brain development.

The zinc problem can be quantified. Phytic acid (myo-inositol hexakisphosphate, molar mass 660 g mol⁻¹) forms insoluble complexes with zinc in the gut. WHO/FAO (2004) use the molar ratio of phytate to zinc in the diet to classify zinc bioavailability: below about 5 high, 5–15 moderate, above 15 low.

  1. Binding in the gut is a chemical interaction between molecules, so it is the number of molecules (moles), not their mass, that matters.
  2. Moles = mass / molar mass. The factor mg → g cancels because it appears in numerator and denominator.

Diet modelling makes the trade-offs concrete. Zhu et al. (2026) modelled Dutch adult diets in which meat and/or dairy were replaced by plant-based alternatives. All scenarios reduced greenhouse-gas emissions (by 11–39 %) and land use (8–18 %), but increased the water footprint (4–60 %). A "half meat" scenario met the Dutch target of 50 % plant protein while largely preserving nutrient adequacy, whereas "no meat" and "no red meat" scenarios increased the risk of inadequate vitamin B12 and B6 intake. The lesson is not that substitution is bad, but that substitution must be designed — through fortification, blending and diet planning — rather than assumed.

The risk of substitution

A product can match meat for protein grams and still worsen a diet if it lacks the micronutrients the meat supplied, has a low DIAAS, or adds salt and saturated fat. Engineers designing alternative proteins should specify fortification (B12, iron, zinc, iodine, calcium) as part of the product, not as an afterthought.

Policies, dietary guidelines and the ultra-processing debate

Governments have started to treat the protein transition as policy. Three examples from our region:

  • Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023. For the first time, the Nordic recommendations explicitly considered environmental sustainability alongside health (Blomhoff et al., 2023). Their food-based advice includes eating more legumes, vegetables, fruit and whole grains, limiting red meat to at most about 350 g (cooked weight) per week, keeping processed meat to a minimum and eating fish two to three times (300–450 g) per week.
  • Denmark. In October 2023 the Danish government published a national Action Plan for Plant-based Foods, described as the first of its kind, covering research, farming, public procurement, education and export promotion, backed by a dedicated fund for plant-based foods (Danish Ministry of Food, Agriculture and Fisheries, 2023).
  • The Netherlands. Dutch policy aims for half of dietary protein to come from plants (a 50:50 ratio), from a starting point of roughly 60:40 animal:plant (Zhu et al., 2026).

Are meat analogues "ultra-processed" — and does it matter?

The NOVA system classifies foods by the extent and purpose of processing. Group 4, ultra-processed foods (UPF), are "formulations of ingredients, mostly of exclusive industrial use", made by a series of industrial processes and typically containing little or no intact whole food, with additives such as flavours, colours and emulsifiers (Monteiro et al., 2019). Many plant-based meat and dairy analogues fall in this group almost by definition: they are made from isolated proteins, oils and additives, often by extrusion.

The debate matters because UPF consumption is associated with poorer health. In a tightly controlled inpatient trial, 20 adults ate about 500 kcal more per day and gained weight when offered ultra-processed rather than minimally processed diets matched for presented calories, sugar, fat, fibre and macronutrients (Hall et al., 2019). In the UK Biobank cohort, Rauber et al. (2024) separated plant-sourced foods by processing: every 10 percentage-point increase in the share of energy from plant-sourced non-ultra-processed foods was associated with a 7 % lower risk of cardiovascular disease, whereas plant-sourced ultra-processed foods were associated with a 5 % higher risk.

Critics answer that NOVA lumps together very different products, that processing is not intrinsically harmful (fortification, fermentation and cooking are processing too), and that the comparison relevant for the protein transition is not "meat analogue versus lentil stew" but "meat analogue versus the processed meat it replaces". The engineering response is to design analogues with better nutritional profiles — lower salt and saturated fat, higher fibre, adequate fortification, minimal additives — and, where possible, to use less refined ingredients (Lesson 12.2).

Frontier 2024–2026

Researchers are proposing "processing-aware" nutrient profiles that score a product on its nutrient density, protein quality (DIAAS) and footprint at the same time. Such multi-criteria indices (see Lesson 15.4) may replace single-number claims such as "high in protein" or "ultra-processed".

Key terms

Safe level of protein intake
The intake that meets the requirement of 97.5 % of healthy individuals; 0.83 g kg⁻¹ d⁻¹ of good-quality protein for adults (WHO/FAO/UNU, 2007).
Indispensable amino acid
An amino acid that the body cannot synthesise fast enough and must obtain from the diet: His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val.
Limiting amino acid
The indispensable amino acid present in the smallest amount relative to the reference pattern; it limits protein synthesis.
Amino-acid score (AAS)
The lowest ratio of indispensable-amino-acid content in a protein to that in a reference pattern.
PDCAAS
Protein digestibility-corrected amino-acid score: AAS × true faecal crude-protein digestibility, truncated at 1.0 (FAO/WHO, 1991).
DIAAS
Digestible indispensable amino-acid score: lowest ratio of digestible (true ileal) IAA content to the reference pattern, × 100, not truncated (FAO, 2013).
True ileal digestibility
Fraction of an amino acid absorbed by the end of the small intestine, corrected for endogenous losses.
Complementation
Combining proteins with different limiting amino acids so that the blend scores higher than its components.
Feed-conversion ratio (FCR)
Mass of feed eaten per unit mass of live-weight gain; its basis (as-fed or dry matter, live or edible weight) must always be stated.
Protein-conversion efficiency (PCE)
Fraction of feed protein recovered as edible protein.
Human-edible feed
The part of animal feed that could be eaten by people (e.g., cereal grain, soybean), as opposed to grass, residues and by-products.
Functional unit
The reference quantity to which LCA impacts are expressed, e.g. 1 kg of product or 100 g of protein.
Ultra-processed food (NOVA group 4)
Industrial formulations of food-derived substances and additives with little intact food, as defined by the NOVA classification.
Phytate:zinc molar ratio
Moles of phytate per mole of zinc in a diet; values above about 15 indicate low zinc bioavailability.
