Digital twins, data governance and responsible AI

Imagine every lettuce in a greenhouse accompanied by a virtual copy that grows in step with it, updated each night from camera images, that can tell the grower when the crop will be ready and what would happen if the lights were dimmed during tomorrow's price peak. That is the promise of the digital twin — and it pulls together everything in this module: models (Lesson 10.1), learning (10.2), vision (10.3) and control (10.4). This lesson explains what a twin really is, how it stays synchronised with reality through data assimilation, and where twins are used today. It then asks the questions that decide whether data-driven farming is a good thing: who owns and may use the data, whether the models are fair and explainable, how much energy AI consumes, what the EU AI Act requires — and who benefits.

  • Estimate the energy and carbon footprint of AI workloads and classify agricultural AI systems under the EU AI Act risk categories and timeline (LO5, LO6, LO7).

What a digital twin is — and is not

The idea came from manufacturing. In 2002, at the University of Michigan, Michael Grieves presented a concept for product lifecycle management with three parts: a physical product in real space, a virtual product in virtual space, and the flows of data and information that link them; the name “digital twin” was later coined by John Vickers of NASA (Grieves & Vickers, 2017). The essential word is link: a twin is not a model sitting on a hard disk but a model that is continuously fed with data from its physical counterpart and that, in its most developed form, acts back on it. For agriculture, Verdouw et al. (2021) define a digital twin as “a digital equivalent to a real-life object of which it mirrors its behaviour and states over its lifetime in a virtual space”.

Because the term has become fashionable, it is worth being precise. Kritzinger et al. (2018) distinguish three levels of integration by the direction and automation of data flows (Figure 1). A digital model exchanges no data automatically: a crop-growth model run with manually typed weather data, however sophisticated, is a model. A digital shadow is updated automatically from the physical object, but changes in the digital object do not flow back: a dashboard of live sensor data with a crop model that re-estimates leaf area every night is a shadow. Only when data flow automatically in both directions — when the virtual object's conclusions change set-points, irrigation or lighting in the real greenhouse — do we have a digital twin in the strict sense.

Verdouw et al. (2021) add a second dimension: the purpose of the twin along the lifecycle of the object (Table 1). The typology is useful when you read claims about “digital twins in agriculture”: most published examples are monitoring twins; predictive and prescriptive twins are emerging; autonomous twins are still rare.

Common misconception

“A 3D visualisation of the greenhouse is a digital twin.” A beautiful 3D scene without live data is a digital model; with live data but no feedback it is a digital shadow. Conversely, a twin needs no graphics at all: a Kalman filter that keeps a two-number crop state synchronised with daily images and adjusts the irrigation is a genuine (if modest) digital twin.

Architecture of an agricultural digital twin

Whatever its purpose, an agricultural twin is built from the same layers (Figure 2; Verdouw et al., 2021; Pylianidis et al., 2021). At the bottom is the physical system: crop, greenhouse, animals, field. The sensing and actuation layer measures it and acts on it — the sensors and IoT networks of Module 9, cameras (Lesson 10.3), and the actuators of Lesson 10.4. A data layer stores time series, images and metadata, checks their quality and makes them findable and reusable (the FAIR principles; Wilkinson et al., 2016). The model layer contains mechanistic models — such as Van Henten's lettuce model (Van Henten, 1994) or the GreenLight greenhouse model (Katzin et al., 2020) — and data-driven models, often in hybrid form (Lesson 10.1). Data assimilation keeps the model state synchronised with reality (Section 10.5.3). On top, services deliver what the user needs: monitoring, forecasting, what-if analysis and optimisation, and — through the control layer — autonomous action.

A twin is only as good as its data. A drifting pH electrode or a frozen temperature sensor does not only give a wrong number on a dashboard; assimilated into a twin, it silently corrupts the model state and every forecast and decision that follows. Formally, every twin rests on a state-space model: a set of state variables that summarise the system (plant dry mass, leaf area, air temperature, substrate water content), an equation that moves them forward in time, and an equation that says what the sensors should read for a given state.

State-space model of a digital twin(10.5.1)
\[ \mathbf x_{k+1} = f(\mathbf x_k, \mathbf u_k, \boldsymbol\theta) + \mathbf w_k,\qquad \mathbf z_k = h(\mathbf x_k) + \mathbf v_k,\qquad \mathbf w_k\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf Q),\quad \mathbf v_k\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf R) \]

the state at the next time step is a function of the current state, the controls and the weather, and the model parameters, plus a random process error; the measurements are a function of the state plus a random measurement error. The process errors, with covariance \(\mathbf Q\), express how much we distrust the model; the measurement errors, with covariance \(\mathbf R\), how much we distrust the sensors.

Why log leaf area makes the lettuce twin linear
  1. Early growth is close to exponential: \(A_{k+1} = A_k e^{\rho\Delta t}\), with relative growth rate \(\rho\) (Eq. 10.1.4).
  2. Taking logarithms, \(\ell = \ln A\) grows linearly: \(\ell_{k+1} = \ell_k + \rho\,\Delta t\). If we also let the growth rate drift slowly, \(\rho_{k+1} = \rho_k + w_\rho\), the state \(\mathbf x = (\ell, \rho)\) evolves with the linear matrix \(\mathbf F = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \Delta t\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Image errors are roughly proportional to plant size (a few per cent), which is an additive error on the log scale: \(z = \ell + v\). So both equations are linear with Gaussian errors — the ideal case for the Kalman filter (Figure 3).
  4. A logistic slow-down then appears as a gradually decreasing \(\rho\), which the filter tracks from the data without being told the growth curve.
Each term of the state equation has the unit of the corresponding state variable (e.g., \(\ln\)(cm²) is dimensionless, \(\rho\) in d⁻¹, \(\rho\,\Delta t\) dimensionless) ✓.

Keeping the twin in sync: data assimilation

Every model drifts away from reality: parameters are uncertain, some processes are missing, the weather forecast was wrong. Every sensor is noisy and some days are missing. Data assimilation combines the two in a statistically optimal way. The idea goes back to Rudolf Kálmán, who in 1960 published a recursive algorithm for estimating the state of a linear system from noisy measurements (Kalman, 1960). Within a few years a version of his filter was navigating the Apollo spacecraft to the Moon (Grewal & Andrews, 2010); today it runs in every phone's GPS, in weather forecasting and in crop models updated with satellite data (Jin et al., 2018). The filter alternates two steps: predict with the model, then update with the measurement.

Kalman filter, step 1: predict(10.5.2)
\[ \hat{\mathbf x}^{-}_{k} = \mathbf F\,\hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1} + \mathbf B\,\mathbf u_{k-1},\qquad \mathbf P^{-}_{k} = \mathbf F\,\mathbf P_{k-1}\mathbf F^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf Q \qquad\bigl(\text{scalar: } P^{-} = a^2P + q\bigr) \]

push the best current estimate through the model to obtain a prior estimate for the next time step, and propagate its uncertainty: the covariance is transformed by the model and grows by the process noise. In the scalar case the variance is multiplied by the square of the model coefficient and increased by the process variance.

\(\hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1}\), \(\mathbf P_{k-1}\)estimate and its covariance after the previous updatestate units; squared

Follow the derivation
  1. Mean: \(\mathbb E[\mathbf x_k] = \mathbf F\,\mathbb E[\mathbf x_{k-1}] + \mathbf B\mathbf u_{k-1} + \mathbb E[\mathbf w_{k-1}]\), and the noise has zero mean.
  2. Covariance: the prediction error is \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x_{k-1}-\hat{\mathbf x}_{k-1}) + \mathbf w_{k-1}\). Because the estimation error and the new process noise are independent, their covariances add: \(\mathbf F\mathbf P\mathbf F^{\mathsf T} + \mathbf Q\) (for a scalar, \(\operatorname{Var}(aX + W) = a^2\operatorname{Var}X + \operatorname{Var}W\)).
  3. Without measurements the uncertainty only grows: a forecast further into the future is less certain. That is what the widening band in the forecast part of Figure 3 shows.
\(\mathbf F\mathbf P\mathbf F^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\mathbf Q\) are both covariances of the state (squared state units) ✓.
Kalman filter, step 2: update with a measurement(10.5.3)
\[ \mathbf K_k = \mathbf P^{-}_{k}\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}\bigl(\mathbf H\mathbf P^{-}_{k}\mathbf H^{\mathsf T}+\mathbf R\bigr)^{-1},\qquad \hat{\mathbf x}_k = \hat{\mathbf x}^{-}_{k} + \mathbf K_k\bigl(\mathbf z_k - \mathbf H\hat{\mathbf x}^{-}_{k}\bigr),\qquad \mathbf P_k = (\mathbf I-\mathbf K_k\mathbf H)\,\mathbf P^{-}_{k} \] \[ \text{scalar: } K = \frac{P^{-}}{P^{-}+R},\qquad \hat x = \hat x^{-} + K\,(z-\hat x^{-}),\qquad P = (1-K)\,P^{-} \]

the Kalman gain weighs the model against the measurement: it is the prior variance divided by the total of prior and measurement variance. The new estimate is the prediction plus the gain times the surprise — the difference between what was measured and what was expected. The uncertainty shrinks by the factor \(1-K\).

\(\mathbf R\) (\(R\))measurement-noise covariance (variance)\(0.10^2\) for a 10 % image error

Follow the derivation of the optimal gain (scalar case)
  1. We have two independent, unbiased estimates of the same quantity: the prediction \(\hat x^-\) with error variance \(P^-\), and the measurement \(z\) with error variance \(R\). Combine them linearly: \(\hat x = (1-K)\hat x^- + Kz = \hat x^- + K(z-\hat x^-)\).
  2. The error of the combination is \((1-K)e^- + Kv\) with independent parts, so its variance is \(P(K) = (1-K)^2P^- + K^2R\).
  3. Minimise over \(K\): \(\mathrm dP/\mathrm dK = -2(1-K)P^- + 2KR = 0\;\Rightarrow\; K = P^-/(P^-+R)\).
  4. Insert back: \(P = (1-K)^2P^- + K^2R = (1-K)P^-\) (use \(KR = (1-K)P^-\)). Equivalently \(1/P = 1/P^- + 1/R\): information (inverse variance) adds.
  5. For Gaussian errors this is exactly Bayes' theorem: the posterior of a Gaussian prior times a Gaussian likelihood is Gaussian with this mean and variance. The matrix form follows the same logic for several states and measurements (Kalman, 1960).
Assumptions and validity

The Kalman filter is optimal for linear models with Gaussian, independent errors whose covariances are known. For non-linear crop models, the extended Kalman filter linearises \(f\) and \(h\) at each step, and the ensemble Kalman filter (Evensen, 1994) propagates an ensemble of model runs instead of a covariance matrix — the standard in weather forecasting and common in crop models. A badly chosen \(\mathbf Q\) or \(\mathbf R\) makes the filter either sluggish (trusting the model too much) or nervous (chasing every noisy measurement); try both in Figure 3.

Worked example 1Two days of a lettuce twin by hand

A twin tracks \(\ell = \ln A\) of one lettuce, assuming a known relative growth rate of 0.20 d⁻¹. Its current estimate is \(A = 50\) cm² (\(\ell = 3.912\)) with standard deviation 0.05 on the log scale. Process noise is 0.02 per day, image measurement noise 0.10 (≈ 10 %), both as standard deviations on the log scale. The next two daily images give 57 cm² and 72 cm². Run two predict–update cycles.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Variances. \(P_0 = 0.05^2 = 0.0025\); \(q = 0.02^2 = 0.0004\); \(R = 0.10^2 = 0.01\).
  2. Day 1, predict. \(\hat\ell^- = 3.912 + 0.20 = 4.112\) (61.1 cm²); \(P^- = 0.0025 + 0.0004 = 0.0029\).
  3. Day 1, update. \(z = \ln 57 = 4.043\); innovation \(= -0.069\); \(K = 0.0029/(0.0029+0.01) = 0.225\). \(\hat\ell = 4.112 - 0.225\times0.069 = 4.097\) → \(A = 60.1\) cm²; \(P = 0.775\times0.0029 = 0.00225\).
  4. Day 2. \(\hat\ell^- = 4.297\) (73.4 cm²), \(P^- = 0.00265\); \(z = \ln 72 = 4.277\); \(K = 0.00265/0.01265 = 0.209\); \(\hat\ell = 4.297 - 0.209\times0.020 = 4.292\) → 73.1 cm²; \(P = 0.00209\) (SD 0.046).
  5. Interpretation. The image said 57 cm², the model 61 cm²; the twin settled at 60 cm² because it trusts its prediction (variance 0.0029) more than a single image (0.01). With every update the gain falls a little, towards a steady value of about 0.18 — the balance between how fast the model drifts (\(q\)) and how noisy the images are (\(R\)).
Day 1: K = 0.22, A ≈ 60 cm²; day 2: K = 0.21, A ≈ 73 cm² (SD ≈ 4.6 %)

The Kalman filter needs linear models and Gaussian errors. Many agricultural problems are neither: a crop model with thresholds, a disease that is either present or absent, a sensor that sometimes returns wild outliers. Particle filters replace the Gaussian description of uncertainty by a cloud of samples — "particles" — each a complete guess of the state, which is simulated forward with the model, weighted by how well it explains the latest measurement, and occasionally resampled so that improbable guesses are replaced by copies of good ones (Gordon et al., 1993; Arulampalam et al., 2002).

Particle filter: weights, estimate and effective sample size(10.5.4)
\[ w^{(i)}_k \propto w^{(i)}_{k-1}\;p\bigl(z_k \mid x^{(i)}_k\bigr),\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} w^{(i)}_k = 1,\qquad \hat x_k = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w^{(i)}_k\,x^{(i)}_k,\qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\sum_i \bigl(w^{(i)}_k\bigr)^2} \]

each particle's weight is multiplied by the likelihood of the new measurement given that particle's state, then all weights are rescaled to sum to one. The estimate is the weighted average of the particles. The effective sample size counts how many particles really carry weight; when it falls below about half of \(N\), the particles are resampled in proportion to their weights.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(x^{(i)}_k\)state of particle \(i\) at step \(k\) (simulated with the model plus random process noise)state units
\(w^{(i)}_k\)normalised weight of particle \(i\)–
\(p(z_k\mid x^{(i)}_k)\)likelihood: e.g. \(\exp[-(z_k-h(x^{(i)}))^2/2R]\) for Gaussian noise–
\(N\), \(N_{\text{eff}}\)number of particles; effective sample size–
Follow the reasoning
  1. Bayes' theorem: posterior ∝ prior × likelihood. If the particles are samples from the prior, weighting each by the likelihood turns them into weighted samples from the posterior — whatever the shapes of the distributions.
  2. After a few updates most weights become tiny (degeneracy). \(N_{\text{eff}}\) measures this: equal weights give \(N_{\text{eff}} = N\), one dominant particle gives 1. Weights (0.5, 0.3, 0.1, 0.1) give \(1/0.36 = 2.8\) effective particles out of four.
  3. Resampling draws \(N\) new particles from the old ones with probabilities \(w^{(i)}\) and resets the weights to \(1/N\): good hypotheses multiply, poor ones die out — a small-scale natural selection.
  4. The price is computing: thousands of model runs per step instead of one. For low-dimensional crop states this is cheap; for spatial field models it is the reason ensemble Kalman filters are preferred.
Weights are dimensionless probabilities; \(\hat x\) inherits the state units ✓.

Use cases in controlled environments and fields

How far has agriculture come? Pylianidis et al. (2021) reviewed the literature from 2017 to 2020, identified 28 agricultural use cases and compared them with digital twins in other industries: most agricultural examples were at an early stage of technology readiness and focused on monitoring. Ariesen-Verschuur et al. (2022) reviewed greenhouse horticulture specifically and found only 8 articles that explicitly addressed digital twins but 115 studies that applied the concept implicitly in IoT-based systems; the dominant focus was climate control, energy management and lighting at greenhouse level, only about 9 % of the articles virtualised the plants themselves, and none considered the company level. Predictive and prescriptive capabilities were still in an early stage, although predictive twins were gaining prominence. Purcell and Neubauer (2023) reach a similar conclusion for agriculture as a whole.

Key idea

The value of a twin lies in its decisions, not in its graphics. Ask of any proposed twin: which decisions will it change, how much better will they be, and what does it cost — in sensors, data management, modelling effort and energy? A calibrated growth curve updated with weekly photos may deliver most of the value of a full 3D twin for a fraction of the cost.

Who controls farm data? Sharing, ownership and the law

A digital twin is fed by data that someone produces and that someone else often stores: the grower's climate computer reports to the supplier's cloud, the tractor sends its logs to the manufacturer, the advisor's app collects field photos. Data differ from physical goods in two ways that make "ownership" slippery. They are non-rival — a copy costs nothing and deprives nobody — and their value grows when they are aggregated: one farm's yield map says little, a million farms' maps reveal regional harvests before the market does. In EU law there is no general property right in raw data; control is shaped by contracts, trade secrets, database rights, the GDPR and, since 2025, the Data Act.

The voluntary code of conduct (2018)

In 2018 nine EU organisations of the agri-food chain — Copa-Cogeca (farmers and cooperatives), CEMA (machinery), Fertilizers Europe, CEETTAR (contractors), CEJA (young farmers), ECPA (crop protection), EFFAB (animal breeders), FEFAC (feed) and ESA (seeds) — published the EU code of conduct on agricultural data sharing by contractual agreement. It avoids the word "owner" and speaks of the data originator: the party that created or commissioned the data, which has the right to decide who may access and use them. For data produced on the farm or during farming operations this is, in principle, the farmer. The code sets out principles for contracts — transparency about the purpose of data use, consent before sharing with third parties, portability, security, and liability — but, as it states itself, compliance is voluntary (Copa-Cogeca et al., 2018).

The GDPR: when farm data are personal data

The General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679), applied since 25 May 2018, protects personal data: any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Many farms are family businesses, so data linked to a farmer's name, a land register entry or the coordinates of a field can be personal data — the code of conduct makes the same point. Data about workers — wearables, performance logs, greenhouse cameras that capture people (Lesson 10.3) — are personal data too. Processing them needs a lawful basis, a specified purpose and data minimisation, and gives the persons rights of access, rectification, erasure and portability. In Sweden the Authority for Privacy Protection (IMY) supervises compliance.

The Data Act: a right to your machines' data

The Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) entered into force on 11 January 2024 and has applied since 12 September 2025. It targets exactly the situation of the connected farm. Its central idea is that the user of a connected product — a tractor, a milking robot, a greenhouse climate computer, a weather station — and of a related digital service has a right to the data that the use generates:

  • Access. The data holder must make readily available product and service data accessible to the user without undue delay, easily, securely and free of charge (Art. 4(1)).
  • Sharing. At the user's request, the data holder must make the data available to a third party of the user's choice — an independent advisor, a farm-management app, a cooperative (Art. 5(1)). Companies designated as “gatekeepers” under the Digital Markets Act are not eligible recipients (Art. 5(3)).
  • Protection of the farmer's position. The data holder may not use such data to derive insights about the economic situation, assets and production methods of the user in a way that could undermine the user's commercial position (Art. 4(13)). In return the user may not use the data to develop a product that competes with the one that produced them (Art. 4(10)).
  • Access by design. Connected products and related services placed on the market after 12 September 2026 must be designed so that the data are directly accessible to the user (Art. 3(1); Art. 50(3)) — a deadline that has just passed as this course runs.
  • Fair contracts and switching. Unfair contractual terms on data imposed unilaterally on a business are not binding (Chapter IV, for contracts concluded after 12 September 2025, and from 12 September 2027 also for older long-term contracts), and charges for switching between cloud and data-processing services are to disappear entirely from 12 January 2027 (European Commission, 2025).

Your project data

Research data from your own experiment should follow the FAIR principles — Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable (Wilkinson et al., 2016): a tidy table with units (Lesson 10.1), a short metadata description of methods and sensors, a persistent place where it is stored, and a licence that says how others may reuse it. FAIR does not mean "open to everyone": personal data stay protected, but they are described well enough that authorised people can find and use them.

Bias, fairness and explainability

A model learns the world that its data describe. When the data describe some farms, crops, regions or conditions better than others, the model works better for them — algorithmic bias. Mehrabi et al. (2022) catalogue its sources; in agriculture the most important are representation bias (training images from laboratories, from large commercial greenhouses, from temperate regions or from a few popular cultivars), measurement bias (sensors calibrated under conditions that differ from those of use), label bias (experts disagree on disease severity or quality grades), aggregation bias (one model for very different farm types) and feedback loops (recommendations change the data the next model is trained on). The PlantVillage story of Lesson 10.3 — 99 % in the laboratory, 31 % on field images, and even 49 % from background pixels alone (Noyan, 2022) — is a textbook case. Bias becomes a fairness problem when errors fall systematically on some groups of users.

Group fairness: the equal-opportunity gap(10.5.5)
\[ \text{TPR}_g = \frac{TP_g}{TP_g+FN_g},\qquad \Delta_{\text{EO}} = \text{TPR}_A - \text{TPR}_B,\qquad \Delta_{\text{PPV}} = \frac{TP_A}{TP_A+FP_A}-\frac{TP_B}{TP_B+FP_B} \]

compute the true-positive rate (recall, Lesson 10.2) separately for each group of users; the equal-opportunity gap is their difference — how much more likely a real problem is to be caught for group A than for group B. The same can be done for precision (positive predictive value): how much more trustworthy an alarm is for one group than for the other.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(g\in\{A,B\}\)groups of users or conditions (e.g., large commercial greenhouses vs small growers)–
\(TP_g, FN_g, FP_g\)confusion-matrix counts within group \(g\)counts
\(\text{TPR}_g\)true-positive rate (recall) in group \(g\)–

Why several fairness metrics — and why they conflict
  1. Equal opportunity asks that real cases (diseased plants, faulty sensors) are detected equally often in every group: \(\text{TPR}_A = \text{TPR}_B\).
  2. Predictive parity asks that an alarm is equally reliable: equal precision. Because precision depends on prevalence (Lesson 10.2, Eq. 10.2.10), two groups with different disease prevalence cannot in general have both equal recall and equal precision with the same classifier.
  3. Fairness therefore requires a choice of which errors matter most for which users — a value judgement, not a purely technical one — and it must be measured per group; an overall accuracy hides it.
All terms are ratios of counts — dimensionless ✓.
Worked example 2A disease alarm that serves some growers better

A camera-based downy-mildew alarm was trained mostly on images from large commercial greenhouses. An evaluation on 100 infected plants in each of two groups gives: large greenhouses (A) TP = 90, FN = 10, FP = 20; small growers with other cultivars and cameras (B) TP = 60, FN = 40, FP = 30. Compute recall and precision per group and the gaps, and discuss the consequences.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Recall. A: 90/100 = 0.90; B: 60/100 = 0.60. \(\Delta_{\text{EO}} = 0.30\): an infection is missed four times as often for small growers (40 % vs 10 %).
  2. Precision. A: 90/110 = 0.82; B: 60/90 = 0.67. \(\Delta_{\text{PPV}} = 0.15\): small growers also get more false alarms.
  3. Overall accuracy on the pooled data would look acceptable and hide both gaps.
  4. Consequences and remedies. The group that can least afford crop losses benefits least. Remedies: collect and label images from group B, report metrics per group, fine-tune or use separate thresholds, and tell users where the tool has been validated. Under the AI Act, such a system is usually minimal-risk, so these steps are good practice rather than legal obligations (Section 10.5.8).
Recall 0.90 vs 0.60 (Δ_EO = 0.30); precision 0.82 vs 0.67 — the alarm serves large greenhouses markedly better

Explainability is the second pillar of trustworthy AI. A grower who is told to dim the lamps, an advisor who must justify a fungicide recommendation, an inspector who audits a high-risk system: all need to know why a model decided as it did. Explanations are global (which features matter on average) or local (why this prediction for this greenhouse today). The most principled local method borrows from cooperative game theory: treat the features as players who cooperate to produce the prediction, and share the "payout" — the difference between the prediction and the average prediction — fairly among them (Shapley, 1953; Lundberg & Lee, 2017).

Shapley value of a feature(10.5.6)
\[ \phi_j = \sum_{S\subseteq F\setminus\{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!}{|F|!}\,\bigl[v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\bigr],\qquad v(S) = \mathbb E\bigl[f(\mathbf x_S, \mathbf X_{\bar S})\bigr],\qquad \sum_{j}\phi_j = f(\mathbf x) - \mathbb E[f] \]

the contribution of feature \(j\) is its average marginal effect: for every subset \(S\) of the other features, compare the model's expected output when the features in \(S\) are known with and without also knowing feature \(j\), and average these differences with weights that treat every order of adding features as equally likely. The contributions of all features add up exactly to the difference between this prediction and the average prediction.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(F\), \(|F|\)set of features, number of features–
\(S\)a subset (coalition) of features not containing \(j\)–
\(v(S)\)expected model output when the features in \(S\) are fixed to this instance's values and the others vary as in background dataunit of the prediction
\(\phi_j\)Shapley value (SHAP value) of feature \(j\)unit of the prediction
Follow the reasoning
  1. Imagine the features "arriving" one at a time in random order. When feature \(j\) arrives after the coalition \(S\), it changes the expected prediction by \(v(S\cup\{j\})-v(S)\).
  2. There are \(|F|!\) orders; the coalition \(S\) precedes \(j\) in \(|S|!\,(|F|-|S|-1)!\) of them. Averaging over all orders gives the formula.
  3. Shapley proved that this is the only attribution with four fairness properties: efficiency (contributions sum to the total), symmetry (identical features get identical credit), dummy (a feature that never changes the output gets zero) and additivity. Lundberg and Lee (2017) showed that several earlier explanation methods are approximations of Shapley values and named the approach SHAP.
  4. With 3 features there are only \(2^3 = 8\) coalitions and the exact values are cheap (Figure 6); with hundreds of features they are estimated by sampling or with model-specific shortcuts.
Assumptions and validity

The result depends on the chosen background (what "average" means) and, when features are correlated — light and temperature in a greenhouse — on how the missing features are filled in. Shapley values explain the model, not the plant: a large contribution of CO₂ says the model relies on CO₂, not that CO₂ causes growth. For high-stakes decisions, Rudin (2019) argues for inherently interpretable models instead of explaining black boxes afterwards.

Every \(\phi_j\) has the unit of the prediction (e.g., g d⁻¹), and so does their sum ✓; the weights are dimensionless and sum to 1 ✓.
Worked example 3Shapley values by hand

A yield model uses two standardised features, \(x_1\) (light) and \(x_2\) (CO₂): \(f = 2 + 3x_1 + x_2 + 2x_1x_2\) (g d⁻¹). The background average is \(x_1 = x_2 = 0\), i.e. \(\mathbb E[f] = 2\). Explain the prediction for a day with \(x_1 = 1\), \(x_2 = 2\).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Coalition values. \(v(\varnothing) = f(0,0) = 2\); \(v(\{1\}) = f(1,0) = 5\); \(v(\{2\}) = f(0,2) = 4\); \(v(\{1,2\}) = f(1,2) = 2+3+2+4 = 11\).
  2. Feature 1. Two orders: 1 first → \(5-2 = 3\); 1 after 2 → \(11-4 = 7\). \(\phi_1 = (3+7)/2 = 5\).
  3. Feature 2. 2 first → \(4-2 = 2\); 2 after 1 → \(11-5 = 6\). \(\phi_2 = (2+6)/2 = 4\).
  4. Check efficiency. \(5+4 = 9 = 11-2\) ✓. The interaction term \(2x_1x_2 = 4\) was split equally (2 each): \(\phi_1 = 3\cdot1 + 2\), \(\phi_2 = 1\cdot2 + 2\) — the general rule \(\phi_1 = b_1x_1 + \tfrac12 b_{12}x_1x_2\) used in the mini-calculator.
φ₁ = 5 g d⁻¹ (light), φ₂ = 4 g d⁻¹ (CO₂); together they explain the 9 g d⁻¹ above average

A second popular approach, LIME (local interpretable model-agnostic explanations), fits a simple model — usually a weighted linear regression — to the black-box predictions in the neighbourhood of the instance to be explained (Ribeiro et al., 2016).

LIME: a local surrogate model(10.5.7)
\[ \hat g = \arg\min_{g\in G}\ \sum_{m=1}^{M} \pi_{\mathbf x}(\mathbf z_m)\,\bigl[f(\mathbf z_m)-g(\mathbf z_m)\bigr]^2 + \Omega(g),\qquad \pi_{\mathbf x}(\mathbf z) = \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\lVert\mathbf z-\mathbf x\rVert^2}{\sigma^2}\Bigr) \]

sample many perturbed inputs around the instance, ask the black box for its predictions, and fit an interpretable model — here a straight line or plane — by least squares, giving more weight to samples closer to the instance and penalising complexity. The slopes of the local model are the explanation.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\mathbf z_m\)perturbed samples around the instance \(\mathbf x\)feature units
\(g\), \(G\)surrogate model from a simple family (linear models)–
\(\pi_{\mathbf x}\)proximity weight with kernel width \(\sigma\)–
\(\Omega(g)\)complexity penalty (e.g., number of non-zero coefficients)–
Assumptions and validity

LIME is weighted least squares (Lesson 10.1) on synthetic data. Its explanation depends on the sampling distribution and the kernel width, and it can change between runs; it describes the local slope, which differs from the Shapley contribution whenever the model is curved or has interactions — Figure 6 lets you compare the two. Ryo (2022) reviews the use of such methods in agricultural data analysis.

The loss is in (unit of prediction)², weights are dimensionless; the fitted slopes have units of prediction per unit feature ✓.

The energy and carbon footprint of AI

AI runs on electricity. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024 — around 1.5 % of the world's electricity — with the United States accounting for 45 %, China 25 % and Europe 15%, and projected consumption to more than double to around 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the most important driver (IEA, 2025). Early studies drew attention to the cost of training large language models (Strubell et al., 2019); Patterson et al. (2021) showed that the choice of model architecture, data centre and processor can change the carbon footprint of training by a factor of roughly 100–1000, and the location alone by 5–10 because grids differ so much. The life-cycle footprint of the BLOOM language model's final training was estimated at about 24.7 t CO₂-equivalent from electricity use alone and 50.5 t when manufacturing of the equipment and idle consumption were included (Luccioni et al., 2022). For using models, Luccioni et al. (2024) found that multi-purpose generative models consume orders of magnitude more energy than task-specific models for the same task, and Google reported that the median text prompt to its Gemini assistant used 0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL of water in 2025, after a 33-fold reduction in energy per prompt within one year (Elsworth et al., 2025). The numbers change quickly; the method for estimating them does not.

Energy and carbon of a computation(10.5.8)
\[ E = \frac{\bar P\,t\cdot\text{PUE}}{1000},\qquad E_{\text{use}} = \frac{n\,e\cdot\text{PUE}}{1000},\qquad m_{\text{CO}_2} = E\cdot \text{CI} \]

the electricity of a computation, in kilowatt-hours, is the average power of the hardware times the running time times the power usage effectiveness of the data centre, divided by a thousand; for use of a model it is the number of requests times the energy per request. The carbon footprint is the energy times the carbon intensity of the electricity.

\(\bar P\)average power of processors, memory and cooling fans of the hardwareW10–25 W (edge device); 300–700 W (data-centre GPU)
\(t\)running timeh
PUEpower usage effectiveness: total facility energy ÷ IT energy (cooling, power conversion)–≥ 1; ≈ 1.7 as a default average (Lannelongue et al., 2021)

Follow the reasoning
  1. Energy is power × time: W × h = Wh; dividing by 1000 gives kWh.
  2. The IT hardware is not the only consumer: cooling and power distribution add a fraction that the PUE captures; on-farm edge devices without special cooling have PUE ≈ 1.
  3. Emissions follow from the electricity's carbon intensity — which, as Lesson 15.1 showed, varies by a factor of about 20 across Europe. The same training run emits six times less in Sweden than on the EU-average grid.
  4. This is the calculation behind tools such as Green Algorithms (Lannelongue et al., 2021), which add the separate power of processor cores and memory.
Assumptions and validity

The formula covers operational electricity only. Embodied emissions of hardware manufacturing, water use for cooling, and systemic effects (rebound: cheaper computation invites more of it) are extra. Published per-query or per-training figures differ widely because system boundaries differ; always check what was included (Luccioni et al., 2024; de Vries, 2023).

W × h × (–)/1000 = kWh ✓; kWh × kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ = kg CO₂e ✓.
Worked example 4The AI energy budget of a one-hectare greenhouse

A 1 ha tomato greenhouse runs a crop twin with four camera trolleys, each with an edge computer drawing 15 W around the clock. Every month a disease model is retrained in the cloud: 10 h on a 300 W GPU in a data centre with PUE 1.5. Staff ask an LLM assistant 50 questions a day at 0.24 Wh each. Estimate the annual electricity and emissions on the Swedish production mix (35 g kWh⁻¹) and the EU mix (211 g kWh⁻¹), and compare with the heating demand of the Västerås glasshouse of Lesson 8.1 (≈ 650 kWh m⁻² yr⁻¹).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Edge computers. \(4\times15\ \text{W}\times8760\ \text{h} = 525.6\) kWh (PUE ≈ 1 on the farm).
  2. Retraining. \(12\times10\ \text{h}\times300\ \text{W}\times1.5/1000 = 54\) kWh.
  3. LLM questions. \(50\times365\times0.24/1000 = 4.4\) kWh (data-centre overhead is included in the reported figure).
  4. Total. ≈ 584 kWh yr⁻¹ → 20.4 kg CO₂e on the Swedish mix, 123 kg on the EU mix.
  5. Comparison. Heating: \(650\times10\,000 = 6.5\) GWh yr⁻¹. The AI system uses about 0.009 % of that. For a farm, its own AI is negligible next to heating and lighting — the relevant question is whether it helps save a few per cent of the heat.

≈ 580 kWh yr⁻¹ (≈ 20 kg CO₂e in Sweden, ≈ 120 kg on the EU mix) — about 0.01 % of the greenhouse's heat demand
Key idea

For a single farm, AI's direct energy use is small compared with heating, lighting and cooling; its indirect effects — energy saved by better control, or extra consumption induced by cheaper production — are usually far larger. At the scale of society, however, AI is becoming a significant electricity consumer, and the choice between a small task-specific model and a large general-purpose one is also an environmental choice.

The EU AI Act and responsible AI

The Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the world's first comprehensive horizontal law on AI. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and regulates AI systems according to the risk they pose to health, safety and fundamental rights (European Commission, 2026).

  • Unacceptable risk — prohibited (Art. 5), since 2 February 2025: for example social scoring, manipulative techniques that cause significant harm, exploitation of vulnerabilities, untargeted scraping of facial images, emotion recognition in workplaces and education (with medical and safety exceptions) and certain biometric uses. A prohibition of AI systems generating non-consensual intimate content and child sexual abuse material was added in 2026 and applies from December 2026.
  • High risk (Art. 6): AI used as a safety component of regulated products (Annex I) and AI in the areas listed in Annex III — biometrics, critical infrastructure (e.g., water, gas, heating and electricity supply), education, employment and management of workers, access to essential services such as credit, law enforcement, migration, and justice. Providers must operate a risk-management system, ensure data governance (including examination of training data for bias), keep technical documentation and logs, provide transparency to deployers, enable human oversight, and achieve adequate accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, before a conformity assessment and registration.
  • Transparency risk (Art. 50): people must be told when they interact with an AI system such as a chatbot, and AI-generated or manipulated content (deepfakes) must be disclosed and marked.
  • Minimal risk: everything else — the great majority of AI in agriculture, from climate control to yield forecasting — with no specific obligations beyond voluntary codes.
  • General-purpose AI models such as large language models have their own obligations since 2 August 2025 (documentation, copyright policy, a summary of training content), with extra duties for models with systemic risk.

Penalties reach €35 million or 7 % of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices and €15 million or 3 % for most other infringements (Art. 99).

Law sets minimum requirements; responsible AI is broader. The European Commission's High-Level Expert Group on AI (2019) formulated seven requirements for trustworthy AI: human agency and oversight; technical robustness and safety; privacy and data governance; transparency; diversity, non-discrimination and fairness; societal and environmental well-being; and accountability. For a digital twin in a greenhouse they translate into concrete engineering practice: a grower who can override the twin, sensor-fault detection and safe fallback settings (Lesson 10.4), clear rules on who may use the data, explanations of recommendations, validation for all the kinds of farms that will use it, an energy budget, and a named person responsible when things go wrong.

Who benefits? Farmers, platforms and society

Digital agriculture is often presented as a win for everyone: higher yields, fewer inputs, less waste. Social scientists ask a sharper question: who captures the value? Data flow from many farms to few platforms, where aggregation creates knowledge — about yields, input use, land values — that individual farmers do not have (Wolfert et al., 2017; Rotz et al., 2019). Proprietary formats and closed ecosystems create lock-in: the climate computer, the sensors and the advisory service must come from the same supplier. Studies of farmers' attitudes find that trust depends on transparency about what is done with the data and on a fair share of the benefits (Jakku et al., 2019), and that a patchwork of contracts and laws that farmers find hard to navigate explains much of their reluctance to share (Wiseman et al., 2019). Klerkx et al. (2019) set out a research agenda on how digitalisation reshapes farm work, knowledge and power, and Ryan (2023) maps the ethical issues raised by agricultural AI, from data control to impacts on employment. Robotics and autonomy change the nature of farm work — removing heavy and repetitive tasks but also jobs, and concentrating skills in fewer hands (Sparrow & Howard, 2021). In low-income countries, AI advisory services could reach farmers who have never had an extension officer, but only if they work in local languages and conditions and are accountable for their advice (Tzachor et al., 2023; Lesson 10.2).

Frontier 2024–2026

Three developments are shaping digital twins in food production: the application of the Data Act to connected farm equipment from 2025 and its access-by-design requirement from September 2026; the staged application of the AI Act, amended in 2026; and the arrival of foundation models that let twins be queried in natural language (Lesson 10.2) — which makes explanation, validation and data governance more, not less, important. For your own project, the lesson is simple: document your data, validate your models on data they have not seen, say what they cannot do, and keep a human — you — responsible for the decisions.

Key terms

Digital twin
A digital equivalent of a real-life object that mirrors its states and behaviour over its lifetime, with automatic data flows in both directions.
Digital shadow
A digital object updated automatically from its physical counterpart but without automatic feedback to it.
Digital model
A digital representation without automatic data exchange with the physical object.
State-space model
Model with a state vector, a process equation that moves it in time and an observation equation that predicts measurements.
Data assimilation
Statistically optimal combination of model predictions and measurements to estimate the state of a system.
Kalman gain
Weight given to the measurement surprise in the Kalman update; \(P^-/(P^-+R)\) in the scalar case.
Particle filter
Sequential Monte Carlo method that represents uncertainty by weighted samples, re-weighted with each measurement and resampled.
Data originator
In the EU code of conduct, the party that created or commissioned data and decides on access and use — on the farm, usually the farmer.
Connected product
In the Data Act, a product that obtains or generates data about its use or environment and can communicate them; its user has access and sharing rights.
Personal data
Information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, protected by the GDPR.
Algorithmic bias
Systematic differences in model performance or outcomes between groups, caused by data, labels, design or use.
Shapley value (SHAP)
Attribution of a prediction to features as their average marginal contribution over all coalitions; contributions sum to prediction minus average.
LIME
Local explanation method that fits a weighted, simple surrogate model to a black box's predictions near one instance.
Power usage effectiveness (PUE)
Ratio of a data centre's total energy use to the energy used by its IT equipment.
High-risk AI system
Under the AI Act, AI used in regulated products or listed areas such as employment, credit or critical infrastructure, subject to strict obligations.
