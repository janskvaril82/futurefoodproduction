Accessibility, equity and just transitions

A lettuce grown under LEDs on the eighth floor of a Stockholm warehouse can be spotless, pesticide-free and harvested this morning. Who will eat it? In 2025, 2.69 billion people — a third of humanity — could not afford the cheapest healthy diet available where they live, and the cost of that diet had risen to 4.28 international dollars per person and day. A technology can make food greener and still leave these people out; it can create jobs in cities while removing them from the countryside; it can concentrate land, patents and data in fewer hands. This lesson gives you quantitative tools — affordability, the levelised cost of food derived from discounting, learning curves, Lorenz curves and Gini coefficients, rebound and distributional effects — and conceptual ones — justice, food sovereignty, just transitions — to ask of every new food technology: accessible to whom, at what cost, and who carries the burden of change?

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the six dimensions of food security and the distributive, procedural and recognition dimensions of justice, and apply them to a food technology (LO7).
  • Calculate the affordability of a healthy diet and estimate the share of a population that cannot afford it from an income distribution (LO3, LO7).
  • Derive the capital recovery factor from discounting and compute and decompose the levelised cost of food of a plant factory (LO3).
  • Use net present value, learning curves and economies of scale to judge when, and for whom, a new technology becomes affordable (LO1, LO3).
  • Measure inequality with the Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient, and analyse the effects of new technologies on smallholders, workers, women, livestock farmers and rural regions, including intellectual property and data (LO1, LO7).
  • Quantify rebound and distributional effects of food-price changes, and compare high-tech and agroecological pathways fairly (LO1, LO7).

Accessible to whom? A justice lens

The earlier lessons of this module asked whether new food systems are greener (Lesson 15.1) and more resilient (Lesson 15.2). Both questions can be answered with averages: kilograms of CO₂e per kilogram, days of stock per person. This lesson asks a question that averages hide — for whom? Food security has long been described by four dimensions: availability (is there enough food?), access (can people obtain it, physically and economically?), utilisation (can their bodies use it — safe water, health, nutrition knowledge?) and stability (over time and through shocks). The UN's High Level Panel of Experts on Food Security and Nutrition added two more: agency, the capacity of individuals and groups to decide what they eat and produce and to take part in shaping food-system policies, and sustainability, the long-term ability of food systems to provide food security without compromising the economic, social and environmental bases for future generations (HLPE, 2020).

Justice theory offers a complementary vocabulary (Schlosberg, 2007). Distributive justice asks who receives the benefits and who bears the costs — cheaper protein, cleaner water, lost jobs, higher prices. Procedural justice asks who decides — whether farmers, workers and consumers take part in choosing technologies and policies. Recognition justice asks whose identities, knowledge and cultures are respected — whether traditional food practices, indigenous knowledge or small-scale farming are treated as valuable or as obstacles. When a society deliberately shifts a sector onto a new track — away from coal, or from intensive livestock — these questions become the agenda of a just transition, a concept born in the labour movement and now used by the International Labour Organization (ILO, 2015) and in food-system research (Tribaldos & Kortetmäki, 2022).

Key idea

A technology can raise average availability while worsening access for some groups — for example if it makes a staple cheaper in cities but undercuts the income of the smallholders who grew it. Equity assessment means looking at distributions (income groups, regions, genders, farm sizes, workers versus owners) and at processes (who decided), not only at totals.

Affordability: who can buy a healthy diet?

Since 2020 the UN agencies behind The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI) have tracked two indicators that put numbers on economic access. The cost of a healthy diet (CoHD) is the cost of the least expensive combination of locally available foods that meets energy needs and national food-based dietary guidelines, in purchasing-power-parity (PPP) dollars per person per day. The prevalence of unaffordability is the share of the population whose income is too low to buy it. The 2026 edition, dedicated to the high cost of a healthy diet, reports (FAO et al., 2026):

  • The global cost rose to 4.28 PPP dollars per person per day in 2025, from 3.44 in 2021 and 2.94 in 2017; it was highest in Latin America and the Caribbean (4.91).
  • The number of people unable to afford it fell from 2.97 billion (37.4 %) in 2021 to 2.69 billion (32.7 %) in 2025 — incomes rose faster than food prices on average — but in Africa unaffordability rose to 66.6 % of the population.
  • Animal-source foods, fruits and vegetables account for a large share of the cost; starchy staples contribute relatively little. Post-farmgate activities account for 70–75 % of what consumers pay, with up to 40 % of value-chain costs accumulating in midstream processing, logistics and wholesale.

The last point matters for technology assessment. Most food-technology innovation targets primary production — yields, feed conversion, photons per kilogram — yet most of the consumer price is made after the farm gate. SOFI 2026 found that current policies show "an excessive focus on primary production" and that lower diet costs are associated with higher productivity, public research and development, efficient logistics, mobile connectivity, trade integration and lower electricity and labour costs.

Affordability ratio and unaffordability threshold(15.3.1)
\[ a = \frac{c_{\text{diet}}\,N}{y\,N} = \frac{c_{\text{diet}}}{y}, \qquad \text{unaffordable if}\quad y < r = c_{\text{diet}} + n \]

the affordability ratio is the cost of a healthy diet for all members of a household divided by the household's income — with both expressed per person, the household size cancels; a person cannot afford the diet if their income is below a threshold equal to the diet cost plus the cost of basic non-food needs.

Follow the derivation
  1. A household of \(N\) people needs \(c_{\text{diet}}N\) per day for a healthy diet and has income \(yN\) if \(y\) is its per-person income. The ratio \(a\) is independent of \(N\).
  2. Early SOFI editions judged a diet unaffordable when it cost more than 52 % of income, the average food share of spending in low-income countries in the World Bank's 2017 International Comparison Programme — i.e. when \(a > 0.52\) (FAO et al., 2022).
  3. Rearranged: \(y < r = c_{\text{diet}}+n\). The non-food cost \(n\) differs between the World Bank's income groups of countries (FAO et al., 2025).

Assumptions and validity

The CoHD is a least-cost benchmark: it prices the cheapest foods that meet the guidelines, not what people actually buy, and ignores time for cooking and cultural preferences. Affordable does not mean eaten. The method changes between SOFI editions and every edition revises the whole historical series — compare numbers only within one edition.

Why is the share of income so decisive? Because of Engel's law: the poorer a household, the larger the share of its budget that goes to food (Chai & Moneta, 2010). In the International Comparison Programme data behind SOFI, food took on average 14 % of total expenditure in high-income countries, 27 % in upper-middle-income, 38 % in lower-middle-income and 52 % in low-income countries (FAO et al., 2022). Swedish households spent about 13 % of their expenditure on food and drink in 2023 (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025).

Worked example 1One household, two rules

A household of five in a lower-middle-income country has an income of 7.50 PPP$ per person per day. The local cost of a healthy diet is 4.28 PPP$ per person per day. (a) What share of income would the diet take? (b) Is it affordable under the old "52 % rule"? (c) Under the current rule, if basic non-food needs cost 3.00 PPP$ per person per day? (d) What if the household also buys 100 g of plant-factory lettuce per person per day at US$7.54 per kg (Section 15.3.3)?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(a = 4.28/7.50 = 0.571\): 57 % of income (household size cancels: \(5\times4.28/(5\times7.50)\)).
  2. (b) 57 % > 52 %: unaffordable under the old rule.
  3. (c) \(r = 4.28+3.00 = 7.28 < 7.50\): just affordable under the current rule, with 0.22 PPP$ per person per day to spare. The verdict depends on the method — a reason to read methods sections.
  4. (d) \(0.1\ \text{kg}\times7.54 = 0.75\) $ per person per day (treating US$ and PPP$ as roughly comparable for a rough check): 18 % of the entire cost of a healthy diet for one vegetable portion. At that price, plant-factory lettuce is a food for the affluent, not a route to affordable nutrition.
(a) 57 %; (b) unaffordable; (c) affordable by a margin of 0.22 PPP$ d⁻¹; (d) +0.75 $ d⁻¹, 18 % of the diet cost

To count how many people cannot afford the diet, you need the whole income distribution. Incomes are strongly right-skewed and are often approximated by a lognormal distribution, whose logarithm is normally distributed. With that approximation, the prevalence of unaffordability has a closed form that links it directly to the median income and to inequality.

Prevalence of unaffordability with lognormal incomes(15.3.2)
\[ \text{PUA} = \Pr(y < r) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln r - \ln y_{\text{med}}}{\sigma}\right), \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{2}\;\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1+G}{2}\right) \]

the prevalence of unaffordability is the probability that a randomly chosen person's income is below the threshold r: the standard normal cumulative distribution evaluated at the log of the threshold minus the log of the median income, divided by the log-standard deviation σ; σ itself follows from the Gini coefficient G of the income distribution.

Follow the derivation
  1. Lognormal model: \(\ln y \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). The median of \(y\) is \(e^{\mu}\), so \(\mu=\ln y_{\text{med}}\) (the mean is larger, \(e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}\), because of the long right tail).
  2. \(\Pr(y<r)=\Pr(\ln y<\ln r)=\Pr\!\big(Z<\frac{\ln r-\mu}{\sigma}\big)=\Phi\!\big(\frac{\ln r-\ln y_{\text{med}}}{\sigma}\big)\) with \(Z\) standard normal.
  3. The Gini coefficient of a lognormal distribution depends only on \(\sigma\): \(G = 2\Phi(\sigma/\sqrt2)-1\) (derived with Eq. 15.3.7). Inverting: \(\sigma=\sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}\big(\frac{1+G}{2}\big)\). A Gini of 0.40 gives \(\sigma = \sqrt2\times0.5244 = 0.742\).
  4. Sensitivity: \(\partial\,\text{PUA}/\partial\ln r = \phi(z)/\sigma\), where \(\phi\) is the normal density. When the threshold lies near the median (\(z\approx0\)), a 10 % fall in the diet cost moves several per cent of the population across the line — as Worked example 2 shows.
Assumptions and validity

Real income distributions have heavier upper tails than a lognormal and are measured with error, especially for subsistence farmers whose "income" is partly their own produce. SOFI uses country-specific survey distributions from the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform instead of a formula (FAO et al., 2025). The lognormal version is for understanding and for scenario analysis.

logarithms of ratios of PPP$ amounts are dimensionless; \(\Phi\) returns a probability ✓
Worked example 2How many people does a cheaper diet reach?

A country has a median income of 8.0 PPP$ per person per day and an income Gini of 0.40. The cost of a healthy diet is 4.28 and basic non-food needs cost 3.00 PPP$ per person per day. (a) Estimate the prevalence of unaffordability with Eq. 15.3.2. (b) Post-farmgate innovation (cold chains, shorter logistics) cuts the diet cost by 10 %. How many percentage points of the population now become able to afford it?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Spread. \(\sigma=\sqrt2\,\Phi^{-1}(0.70)=1.4142\times0.5244=0.742\).
  2. (a) \(r = 7.28\); \(z=(\ln7.28-\ln8.0)/0.742=(1.985-2.079)/0.742=-0.127\); \(\text{PUA}=\Phi(-0.127)=0.449\) — 45 % of the population.
  3. (b) \(c = 0.9\times4.28 = 3.85\), \(r = 6.85\); \(z=(1.924-2.079)/0.742=-0.209\); \(\text{PUA} = 0.417\).
  4. Change: 44.9 − 41.7 = 3.2 percentage points — in a country of 50 million, 1.6 million people. Because the threshold lies near the median, where the distribution is densest, a modest cost reduction has a large effect.

The levelised cost of food: discounting from first principles

Which system can deliver a kilogram of vegetables most cheaply? Operating costs are easy to compare, but controlled-environment farms are capital-intensive: most of their cost is paid up front, for a building, racks, LEDs and air handling that then produce for 15 years. To compare a farm that spends a lot now with one that spends a little every year, economists use discounting: money available earlier is worth more, because it could have been invested, or because the investor demands compensation for waiting and for risk. A payment \(F\) received \(t\) years from now is worth \(F/(1+r)^t\) today, where \(r\) is the discount rate — the investor's required return (in real terms, i.e. net of inflation, when costs are in constant prices).

Capital recovery factor(15.3.3)
\[ \text{CRF}(r,n) = \frac{r\,(1+r)^{n}}{(1+r)^{n}-1}, \qquad A = I\cdot\text{CRF} \]

the capital recovery factor equals the discount rate times one plus the rate to the power of the lifetime, divided by one plus the rate to the power of the lifetime minus one; multiplied by the investment it gives the constant annual amount that repays the investment with interest over the lifetime.

\(r\)real discount rate (required return)yr⁻¹ (fraction)0.05

Follow the derivation
  1. Present value of a payment \(A\) at year \(t\): \(A/(1+r)^t\). Of \(n\) equal payments at years 1…\(n\): \(\text{PV}=A\sum_{t=1}^{n}(1+r)^{-t}\).
  2. This is a geometric series with first term \(q=(1+r)^{-1}\) and ratio \(q\): \(\sum_{t=1}^{n}q^{t}=q\,\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}\). Since \(q/(1-q)=1/r\): \(\text{PV}=A\,\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r}\). The fraction is the annuity factor AF.
  3. Require the payments to repay the investment: \(\text{PV}=I\), so \(A = I/\text{AF} = I\,\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}\). Multiply numerator and denominator by \((1+r)^n\): \(\text{CRF}=\frac{r(1+r)^n}{(1+r)^n-1}\).
  4. Limits: as \(r\to0\), CRF \(\to1/n\) (straight-line repayment, no interest); as \(n\to\infty\), CRF \(\to r\) (a perpetuity pays only interest). Example: \(r = 0.05\), \(n=15\): \(1.05^{15}=2.0789\), CRF \(=0.05\times2.0789/1.0789=0.0963\) — 44 % more per year than \(1/15=0.0667\).
Assumptions and validity

Constant real discount rate and equal annual amounts at year end. The discount rate is not a law of nature: public investors may use 3–4 %, a venture-capital-funded start-up may face an effective cost of capital several times higher. Choosing \(r\) is therefore a choice about who invests — a distributive question.

\(\dfrac{\text{yr}^{-1}\cdot(-)}{(-)}=\text{yr}^{-1}\); \(\$\times\text{yr}^{-1}=\$\,\text{yr}^{-1}\) ✓

The levelised cost of food (LCOF) borrows the logic of the levelised cost of electricity used to compare power plants (Short et al., 1995): it is the constant price per kilogram at which the project exactly recovers all its costs, including the required return on capital.

Levelised cost of food(15.3.4)
\[ \text{LCOF} = \frac{\sum_{t=0}^{n} C_t\,(1+r)^{-t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} Q_t\,(1+r)^{-t}} \;=\; \frac{I\,(\text{CRF}+m) + F}{Y} \]

the levelised cost of food is the discounted sum of all costs divided by the discounted sum of all output; with a single investment at the start and constant annual costs and output, it equals the annualised investment (capital recovery plus maintenance) plus the annual operating costs, divided by the annual yield.

Follow the derivation
  1. Define LCOF as the constant selling price \(P\) at which the project's net present value is zero: \(\sum_t (P\,Q_t - C_t)(1+r)^{-t}=0\).
  2. Solve for \(P\): \(P=\sum_tC_t(1+r)^{-t}/\sum_tQ_t(1+r)^{-t}\). Output is "discounted" only because the revenue it earns arrives later — kilograms do not lose value.
  3. Special case: investment \(I\) at \(t=0\), then constant annual costs \(mI+F\) and output \(Y\) for \(t=1..n\). Numerator: \(I+(mI+F)\,\text{AF}\); denominator: \(Y\,\text{AF}\).
  4. Divide both by AF and use \(1/\text{AF}=\text{CRF}\) (Eq. 15.3.3): \(\text{LCOF}=\big(I\,\text{CRF}+mI+F\big)/Y\).
  5. Decomposition: capital \(I\,\text{CRF}/Y\), maintenance \(mI/Y\) and each operating item \(F_k/Y\) — the "contribution analysis" of cost, analogous to Lesson 15.1.
Assumptions and validity

Constant real prices and yields; no residual value, taxes, subsidies or financing structure; all output sold. Real farms ramp up over the first years, lose crops, and sell part of their output at discount — each of which raises the true cost. LCOF answers "what must a kilogram sell for?", not "what will it sell for?".

Worked example 3The levelised cost of plant-factory lettuce

Zhuang et al. (2022) compiled construction costs of 26 plant factories with artificial lighting in Japan, the USA, the EU, Canada and China and estimated their operating costs. For lettuce they report a yield of 115 kg per m² of cultivation area per year, a price of US$11 per kg and annual current costs per m² of $279 labour, $166 electricity, $86 seeds and nutrients, $157 packaging and logistics and $12 water and other. Construction cost per m² of cultivation area fell from $4,167 for a 12 m² facility to $1,159 for a 100,000 m² facility. Using their assumptions (15 years, 5 % interest, 1.5 % maintenance), compute the LCOF for a mid-sized farm at $1,500 m⁻², decompose it, and compare the smallest and largest facilities.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. CRF (Eq. 15.3.3): 0.0963 yr⁻¹. Annualised capital: \(1500\times0.0963 = 144.5\ \$\,\text{m}^{-2}\,\text{yr}^{-1}\); maintenance \(0.015\times1500 = 22.5\).
  2. Operating costs: \(F = 279+166+86+157+12 = 700\ \$\,\text{m}^{-2}\,\text{yr}^{-1}\).
  3. LCOF (Eq. 15.3.4): \((144.5+22.5+700)/115 = 867/115 = 7.54\ \$\,\text{kg}^{-1}\).
  4. Decomposition per kg: labour 2.43 (32 %), electricity 1.44 (19 %), packaging and logistics 1.37 (18 %), capital 1.26 (17 %), seeds and nutrients 0.75 (10 %), maintenance 0.20 (3 %), water and other 0.10 (1 %). The labour share is consistent with the 25–30 % reported by van Delden et al. (2021).

Investors, learning curves and economies of scale

The LCOF tells a buyer what a kilogram must cost. An investor asks a different question: given a price, does the investment pay? The standard answer is the net present value.

Net present value of an investment(15.3.5)
\[ \text{NPV} = -I + \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^{t}} \;=\; -I + C\,\frac{1-(1+r)^{-n}}{r} \quad(\text{constant } C) \]

the net present value is minus the investment plus the discounted sum of the net cash flows it produces; for a constant annual cash flow the sum is the cash flow times the annuity factor.

Follow the derivation

  2. For constant \(C\), the sum is the annuity factor of Eq. 15.3.3: \(\text{AF}=[1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r=1/\text{CRF}\).
  3. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which NPV = 0. An investment is attractive to an investor whose cost of capital is below its IRR.
  4. The simple payback time \(I/C\) ignores discounting and lifetime; it is popular because it is easy, and misleading because it is easy.

Worked example 4An LED retrofit — worth it for whom?

The plant factory of Worked example 3 spends $166 m⁻² yr⁻¹ on electricity, of which about 60 % is for lighting (Lesson 15.1). Replacing LEDs with an efficacy of 2.5 µmol J⁻¹ by fixtures delivering 3.5 µmol J⁻¹ costs $150 m⁻² and lasts 8 years. (a) Estimate the annual saving. (b) Compute the NPV for an investor with a 5 % cost of capital and for one who must pay 12 %. (c) Estimate the IRR.

Reveal the reasoning

Will new food technologies become cheaper over time? Many manufactured technologies follow learning curves: each doubling of cumulative production cuts unit cost by a roughly constant fraction, the learning rate. The observation goes back to Wright (1936), who found it for aircraft assembly; it has since described solar photovoltaics, wind turbines and batteries so well that probabilistic forecasts based on it outperformed most energy-economy models (Way et al., 2022).

Learning curve (Wright's law)(15.3.6)
\[ C(x) = C_0\left(\frac{x}{x_0}\right)^{-b}, \qquad \text{LR} = 1-2^{-b}, \qquad N_{\text{d}} = \frac{\ln(C/C_0)}{\ln(1-\text{LR})} \]

unit cost falls as a power of cumulative production; the learning rate is the fractional cost reduction per doubling of cumulative production; and the number of doublings needed to reach a target cost is the logarithm of the cost ratio divided by the logarithm of one minus the learning rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. Empirical law: \(C\propto x^{-b}\). Doubling \(x\) multiplies cost by \(2^{-b}\), so the learning rate is \(\text{LR}=1-2^{-b}\) and \(b=-\log_2(1-\text{LR})\). LR = 20 % gives \(b=0.322\).
  2. After \(N_{\text{d}}\) doublings, \(x/x_0=2^{N_{\text{d}}}\) and \(C/C_0=(1-\text{LR})^{N_{\text{d}}}\). Taking logarithms: \(N_{\text{d}}=\ln(C/C_0)/\ln(1-\text{LR})\).
  3. Halving cost at LR = 20 %: \(N_{\text{d}}=\ln0.5/\ln0.8=3.11\) doublings, i.e. \(2^{3.11}=8.6\) times more cumulative production.
  4. A close cousin describes economies of scale in plant size: construction cost per m² \(\propto S^{\beta}\). Zhuang et al. (2022) estimated \(\beta\approx-0.17\) for plant factories: a farm 10 times larger costs about 32 % less per m² (\(10^{-0.17}=0.68\)).
Assumptions and validity

Learning curves are empirical regularities, not laws of physics. They work for modular, mass-produced hardware (LEDs, solar panels); they may fail where costs are set by biology, thermodynamics or contamination risk. A techno-economic analysis of cultured meat concluded that the scale-up economics of animal cell culture "would likely preclude the affordability of their products as food", and that better metabolic efficiency and cheap plant-hydrolysate media were necessary but insufficient conditions (Humbird, 2021). Extrapolating a learning curve beyond physical limits is wishful thinking.

(c) What does a 20 % learning rate achieve after three doublings?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(N_{\text{d}}=\ln(2.50/7.54)/\ln(0.85)=\ln(0.3316)/(-0.1625)=(-1.104)/(-0.1625)=6.79\) doublings.
  2. (b) \(2^{6.79}=111\): cumulative production must grow more than a hundredfold.
  3. (c) \(7.54\times0.8^{3}=3.86\ \$\,\text{kg}^{-1}\).
  4. Interpretation. Not every cost component learns: labour, packaging and logistics make up about half of the LCOF in Worked example 3 and are similar for any lettuce. Learning in LEDs and automation attacks electricity and labour; the structural price gap to field production is unlikely to vanish on learning alone.

Who benefits? Farms, firms and the Global South

Capital intensity, economies of scale and access to finance all favour large, well-financed operators. Is that a problem? It depends on the structure of the food system the technology enters. Lowder et al. (2021) compiled agricultural censuses and found more than 608 million farms in the world. Small farms under 2 ha make up 84 % of all farms but operate only about 12 % of agricultural land and produce roughly 35 % of the world's food; the largest 1 % of farms — those over 50 ha — operate more than 70 % of the world's farmland. Inequality of this kind is measured with the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient.

Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient(15.3.7)
\[ G = 1 - 2\int_0^1 L(p)\,\mathrm{d}p \;\approx\; 1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(X_k-X_{k-1}\big)\big(Y_k+Y_{k-1}\big), \qquad G_{\text{lognormal}} = 2\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt2}\right)-1 \]

the Gini coefficient is one minus twice the area under the Lorenz curve; for grouped data it is one minus the sum over groups of the width of each group on the population axis times the sum of the cumulative shares at its two ends; for a lognormal distribution it depends only on the log-standard deviation σ.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(L(p)\)Lorenz curve: share of the total (income, land, benefits) held by the poorest fraction \(p\) of units–
\(X_k\), \(Y_k\)cumulative population share and cumulative share of the total at the upper end of group \(k\) (\(X_0=Y_0=0\))–
\(G\)Gini coefficient: 0 = perfect equality, 1 = one unit holds everything–
\(\sigma\)standard deviation of the logarithm (lognormal case)–
Follow the derivation
  1. Sort units from smallest to largest and plot the cumulative share of units \(p\) against the cumulative share of the total \(L(p)\) (Lorenz, 1905). Perfect equality is the diagonal \(L=p\).
  2. The Gini coefficient is the area between the diagonal and the Lorenz curve, divided by the area under the diagonal (1/2): \(G=\frac{1/2-\int L\,\mathrm{d}p}{1/2}=1-2\int_0^1L\,\mathrm{d}p\).
  3. For grouped data, approximate the area under \(L\) with trapezoids: each group contributes \((X_k-X_{k-1})(Y_k+Y_{k-1})/2\). Substituting gives the sum form. Because it ignores inequality within groups, it is a lower bound.
  4. For a lognormal distribution, \(L(p)=\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(p)-\sigma\big)\); integrating gives \(G=2\Phi(\sigma/\sqrt2)-1\) — the relation used in Eq. 15.3.2.
Assumptions and validity

The Gini coefficient summarises a whole curve in one number: very different distributions can share the same \(G\). Report the shares of the bottom 50 % and the top 10 % alongside it. For farm-size data, the result depends on how "farm" is defined in each census.

shares are dimensionless ✓
Worked example 6How unequal is the world's farmland?

Use the three points of Lowder et al. (2021) — the smallest 84 % of farms hold about 12 % of farmland, and the largest 1 % hold just over 70 % — to estimate a lower bound for the Gini coefficient of farmland.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Lorenz points: \((0,0)\), \((0.84, 0.12)\), \((0.99, 0.30)\) (the bottom 99 % hold at most 30 %), \((1,1)\).
  2. Trapezoids: \(0.84\times(0+0.12)=0.1008\); \(0.15\times(0.12+0.30)=0.0630\); \(0.01\times(0.30+1.00)=0.0130\). Sum = 0.1768.
  3. Gini (Eq. 15.3.7): \(G \ge 1-0.1768 = 0.82\).
  4. Comparison. Income Gini coefficients of countries typically lie between about 0.25 and 0.65: farmland is more concentrated than income almost everywhere. A technology whose benefits scale with land or capital will, by default, flow to the top of this distribution.
G ≥ 0.82

For the Global South, three further mechanisms decide who benefits:

  • Fit with infrastructure and prices. A plant factory needs reliable, affordable electricity, skilled technicians and buyers who pay premium prices. SOFI 2026 found that lower diet costs go together with agricultural productivity, public R&D, logistics, mobile connectivity and lower electricity and labour costs, and that long-term productivity gains are greatest when agricultural research targets fruits, vegetables and legumes — the expensive components of healthy diets (FAO et al., 2026). Technologies that fit smallholder conditions — drip irrigation, improved seed, cold storage, mobile market information — often do more for access than high-tech farms.
  • Intellectual property and seeds. Plant breeders' rights under the UPOV Convention (1991 Act) protect new varieties, and make saving protected seed an optional exception for national law; the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture (2001) recognises farmers' rights to save, use, exchange and sell farm-saved seed subject to national law, and to share in the benefits of genetic resources (FAO, 2001). Between 2016 and 2019 a wave of mergers — Dow and DuPont (forming Corteva), ChemChina and Syngenta, Bayer and Monsanto, with divestments to BASF — concentrated the global seed and agrochemical industry, raising concerns about prices and innovation (OECD, 2018; Deconinck, 2021). The EU's new genomic techniques regulation allows patents on gene-edited plants, with a code of conduct on licensing and a Commission review of access problems (Lesson 14.2). "Open-source" seed initiatives license varieties so that they and their derivatives stay freely usable (Kloppenburg, 2010).
  • Data and platforms. Digital agriculture raises questions of data ownership and control, of who produces technologies and data, and of data security (Rotz et al., 2019). Klerkx and Rose (2020) warn that "Agriculture 4.0" technologies — robotics, AI, gene editing, cellular agriculture, vertical farming — have strong inclusion and exclusion effects, and call for responsible innovation that anticipates them.

Vertical farming illustrates the pattern. Van Delden et al. (2021) note that its economics, "partly driven by venture capital", allow firms to raise capital "but seldom to run profitable operations", that it may create urban jobs but have displacement effects on rural areas, and that short local chains driven by start-ups may be absorbed by agri-food conglomerates.

Labour, gender and the digital divide

Labour is the largest single cost of a plant factory — 25–30 % of total production costs according to van Delden et al. (2021), 32 % in Worked example 3 — which is why automation is central to its business case. Automation replaces repetitive work (seeding, transplanting, harvesting, packing) with fewer, more skilled jobs (maintenance, data analysis, crop management). Whether that is progress depends on who loses the old jobs and who can get the new ones. Outside controlled environments the picture is reversed: fruits, vegetables and legumes are inherently more labour-intensive to produce than cereals, and mechanisation offers limited solutions for most perishable crops (FAO et al., 2026). Much of Europe's fruit and vegetable harvest depends on international migrant workers, frequently seasonal and in precarious conditions (Rye & Scott, 2018); the EU's Seasonal Workers Directive (2014/36/EU) sets minimum rights for seasonal workers from outside the EU. During the COVID-19 pandemic, closed borders exposed both the dependence and the vulnerability of this workforce.

Gender cuts across all of this. FAO (2023) reports that agrifood systems employed 36 % of working women and 38 % of working men worldwide in 2019 — and 66 % of women's employment in sub-Saharan Africa. The gap in land productivity between female- and male-managed farms of the same size is 24 %, and women in agricultural wage employment earn 18.4 % less than men — 82 cents for every dollar. In the first year of the pandemic 22 % of women lost their jobs in off-farm agrifood work, compared with 2 % of men. Closing the productivity and wage gaps would, FAO estimates, raise global GDP by about 1 % (nearly US$1 trillion) and reduce the number of food-insecure people by about 45 million.

Digital technologies can widen or narrow these gaps. The gender gap in mobile internet access in low- and middle-income countries narrowed from 25 % to 16 % between 2017 and 2021 (FAO, 2023) — progress, but it still means that farm-management apps, digital payments and precision-agriculture advice reach women less often. A technology assessment should ask who can use a tool, not only what the tool can do.

Just transitions for livestock farmers and rural regions

The protein transition of Module 12 implies, in rich countries, fewer animals. For consumers that may be a change of menu; for a livestock farmer it can mean the end of a business, of a skill passed down generations, and of the economic base of a village. The concept of a just transition — originally from trade unions facing the closure of coal mines — insists that the burdens of climate and environmental policy should not fall disproportionately on the workers and regions whose livelihoods depend on the old system (ILO, 2015). For food systems, Tribaldos and Kortetmäki (2022) propose principles and criteria for judging whether a transition is just: who bears the costs and who enjoys the benefits, and whether the transition can be inclusive, leaving no one behind.

Two neighbours of Sweden show how differently the question can be handled.

  • The Netherlands. In 2019 the Council of State struck down the national permitting scheme for nitrogen deposition in protected nature areas, freezing construction and farm projects across the country (Stokstad, 2019). The government's response — deep cuts in ammonia emissions, including buying out livestock farms near vulnerable nature — triggered years of large farmer protests and reshaped national politics. The lesson most often drawn is procedural: measures perceived as imposed from above, without farmers' participation or a credible future for rural areas, lose legitimacy.
  • Denmark. In the 2024 "green tripartite" negotiations, the government, farmers' organisations, industry and nature organisations agreed on the Agreement on a Green Denmark: large areas of farmland are to become forest, meadows and lakes, 140,000 ha of carbon-rich lowland soils are to be taken out of production and rewetted by 2030, and a CO₂e tax on greenhouse-gas emissions from livestock will be introduced from 2030 — with its revenue returned to agriculture as investments in climate technology, green measures and changes of production, targeted at the farmers who find the transition hardest (Ministeriet for Natur og Dyrevelfærd, 2026). A ministry was created solely to implement it. Whatever its eventual effects, the design embeds procedural justice (negotiation) and distributive justice (revenue recycling) from the start.

Instruments for just food-system transitions include voluntary buy-outs at fair prices, retraining and diversification grants, payments for public goods such as grazing of species-rich pastures and carbon storage in soils, support for new value chains (legumes, plant-based processing, local processing), social protection, and regional development funds — the EU's Just Transition Fund, created for regions leaving fossil fuels (Regulation (EU) 2021/1056), is often cited as a model (McCauley & Heffron, 2018). Controlled-environment agriculture enters this picture too: if urban farms displace rural vegetable production, the just-transition question applies to growers as well as to livestock farmers.

Common misconception

"Just transition means slowing down the transition." It means designing it so that it can last. Policies that ignore who pays tend to provoke backlash that stops them altogether; policies that share costs and include the affected tend to survive changes of government.

Rebound and distributional effects

New technologies change prices, and prices change behaviour. If a vertical farm makes fresh greens cheaper, people may buy more of them — good for nutrition, but the environmental saving per kilogram is partly eaten up by the extra kilograms. This rebound effect is familiar from energy efficiency (Greening et al., 2000), and Lesson 15.1 promised its quantitative treatment here.

Price elasticity and the direct rebound effect(15.3.8)
\[ \frac{\Delta Q}{Q} = \varepsilon\,\frac{\Delta P}{P}, \qquad \frac{E_1}{E_0} = (1-s)\left(1+\varepsilon\,\frac{\Delta P}{P}\right), \qquad R = \frac{s-\left(1-E_1/E_0\right)}{s} \]

the relative change in the quantity consumed equals the own-price elasticity times the relative change in price; total environmental impact after the change, relative to before, is one minus the saving per kilogram times one plus the consumption response; and the rebound is the fraction of the engineering saving that the extra consumption takes back.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical
\(\varepsilon\)own-price elasticity of demand (negative)–−0.27 to −0.81 for food groups
\(\Delta P/P\), \(\Delta Q/Q\)relative changes in price and quantity––
\(s\)engineering saving: fractional reduction of impact per kilogram–0.3
\(E_1/E_0\)total impact after/before––
\(R\)direct rebound (share of the saving lost)–0–1 (can exceed 1: "backfire")
Follow the derivation
  1. Definition of elasticity: \(\varepsilon = \frac{\mathrm{d}Q/Q}{\mathrm{d}P/P}\); for small changes \(\Delta Q/Q\approx\varepsilon\,\Delta P/P\). Andreyeva et al. (2010) reviewed 160 studies: elasticities of food categories range from 0.27 to 0.81 in absolute value, with food away from home, soft drinks, juice and meats most responsive (0.7–0.8).
  2. Total impact is impact per kg times kg: \(E=e\,Q\). After the change \(e_1=(1-s)e_0\) and \(Q_1=(1+\varepsilon\Delta P/P)Q_0\); multiply.
  3. The engineering estimate of the saving is \(s\); the realised saving is \(1-E_1/E_0\); the rebound is the fraction lost.
  4. Example: \(s=0.30\), \(\Delta P/P=-0.20\), \(\varepsilon=-0.6\): \(\Delta Q/Q=+12\ \%\); \(E_1/E_0=0.7\times1.12=0.784\), a 21.6 % saving instead of 30 %; \(R=(0.30-0.216)/0.30=28\ \%\).
Assumptions and validity

Linear, own-price, partial-equilibrium effect. Cheaper greens may displace other foods (a cross-price effect) — beneficial if they displace meat, harmful for the climate if the money saved is spent on flights (the indirect rebound). Market-wide effects, such as lower world prices increasing consumption elsewhere, add further rebound.

all quantities are dimensionless ratios ✓

Price changes also redistribute income, because different households spend different shares of their budgets on food. The first-order effect of a price change on a household's real income follows from a simple budget argument.

Distributional effect of a food-price change(15.3.9)
\[ \frac{\Delta W}{y} \approx \big(s_{\text{p}} - s_{\text{c}}\big)\,\frac{\Delta P}{P} \]

the change in a household's welfare, as a fraction of its income, is approximately the difference between the share of its income that comes from selling the food and the share it spends on buying the food, times the relative price change.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\Delta W\)money-metric change in welfare (compensating variation)$ per person per day
\(y\)income or total expenditure$ per person per day
\(s_{\text{c}}\)share of expenditure on the food (consumption share)–
\(s_{\text{p}}\)share of income from producing and selling it (production share)–
\(s_{\text{p}}-s_{\text{c}}\)net benefit ratio: positive for net sellers, negative for net buyers–
Follow the derivation
  1. A household consuming \(q_c\) of the food must spend \(q_c\,\Delta P\) more to keep eating the same after a price rise: a loss of \(q_c\Delta P=s_c\,y\,\Delta P/P\), since \(s_c=Pq_c/y\).
  2. If it also sells \(q_p\), it gains \(q_p\Delta P=s_p\,y\,\Delta P/P\).
  3. Net effect divided by income: \((s_p-s_c)\,\Delta P/P\). Ivanic and Martin (2008) used this net-buyer–net-seller logic with household data from nine low-income countries and found that higher staple prices raised poverty far more often than they reduced it: many of the rural poor are net buyers of food.
  4. For a pure consumer (\(s_p=0\)) the burden is \(s_c\,\Delta P/P\). With Engel's law, \(s_c\) falls with income, so food-price increases — including food taxes — are regressive: they take a larger share of poor households' incomes.
Assumptions and validity

First-order: ignores substitution (which reduces the loss) and wage or employment responses (which, for farm workers, can offset it over time). For large shocks, use the full demand system.

shares × relative change = fraction of income ✓

The numbers are sobering. From 2.3 % in December 2020, median global food-price inflation rose to 13.6 % in January 2023 — 5.1 percentage points above headline inflation — and in low-income countries it peaked at 30 % in May 2023. SOFI 2025 estimated that a 10 % increase in food prices is associated with a 3.5 % rise in moderate or severe food insecurity (FAO et al., 2025). With the average food shares of the International Comparison Programme, a 10 % food-price rise costs households 1.4 % of spending in high-income countries but 5.2 % in low-income countries.

Worked example 7Cheaper greens, greener plates?

A new greenhouse technology cuts the climate footprint of tomatoes by 30 % per kg and, thanks to lower heating costs, their price by 20 %. The own-price elasticity of demand for vegetables is taken as −0.6 (within the range reviewed by Andreyeva et al., 2010). (a) By how much does tomato consumption change? (b) By how much does the total footprint of tomato consumption change? (c) What is the rebound? (d) Separately: a 10 % rise in food prices hits a landless household spending 38 % of its budget on food and a farming household that spends 52 % and earns 70 % of its income from selling food. What is the first-order effect on each?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(\Delta Q/Q = (-0.6)(-0.20) = +12\ \%\).
  2. (b) \(E_1/E_0 = 0.70\times1.12 = 0.784\): the total footprint falls by 21.6 %, not 30 %.
  3. (c) \(R = (0.30-0.216)/0.30 = 28\ \%\) of the saving is taken back — unless the extra tomatoes replace higher-footprint foods, in which case the system-level effect could be better than the engineering estimate.
  4. (d) Landless household: \((0-0.38)\times10\ \% = -3.8\ \%\) of income. Farming household: \((0.70-0.52)\times10\ \% = +1.8\ \%\) — a net seller gains. The same price change is a loss for one poor household and a gain for another; policy must know who is who.
(a) +12 %; (b) −21.6 %; (c) rebound 28 %; (d) −3.8 % and +1.8 % of income

Food sovereignty, culture and fair comparisons of pathways

Access is not only about money. The peasant movement La Vía Campesina launched the idea of food sovereignty at the 1996 World Food Summit, and the 2007 Declaration of Nyéléni, adopted in Mali by more than 500 representatives from over 80 countries, defined it as "the right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food produced through ecologically sound and sustainable methods, and their right to define their own food and agriculture systems" (Nyéléni, 2007). The definition shifts the question from "how much food?" to "whose food, produced how, decided by whom?" — agency and recognition. Cultural appropriateness links this lesson to Module 13: a protein that is cheap and green but alien to a community's cuisine is not accessible in any meaningful sense (Lesson 13.1).

Much of the debate about the future of food is framed as a contest between two pathways: high-tech intensification (controlled environments, precision fermentation, gene editing, robotics, data) and agroecology, which the HLPE (2019) describes through a consolidated list of 13 principles organised around resource efficiency, resilience and social equity — recycling, input reduction, soil and animal health, biodiversity, synergy, economic diversification, co-creation of knowledge, social values and diets, fairness, connectivity, land and natural-resource governance and participation (see also Wezel et al., 2020). A fair comparison needs the same discipline as a fair LCA:

  • Same function. Compare nutrition delivered (protein, micronutrients, a healthy diet), not kilograms of lettuce against kilograms of beans.
  • Same time and maturity. Compare mature technologies with mature practices, or prospective with prospective (Lesson 15.1).
  • Full costs and benefits. Include capital, labour, energy, externalities, public subsidies and research funding on both sides.
  • Distribution and process. Report who gains and who decides, not only totals.
  • Complementarity. The pathways are not mutually exclusive: digital tools can support agroecological systems (Rotz et al., 2019), and a vertical farm can be run by a cooperative.

Key terms

Six dimensions of food security
Availability, access, utilisation and stability, extended by agency and sustainability (HLPE, 2020).
Distributive, procedural and recognition justice
Fairness in who gains and pays, in who decides, and in whose identities, knowledge and cultures are respected (Schlosberg, 2007).
Just transition
A transition to sustainability designed so that its burdens do not fall disproportionately on the workers, farmers and regions whose livelihoods depend on the old system (ILO, 2015).
Cost of a healthy diet (CoHD)
The cost of the least expensive locally available foods meeting energy needs and dietary guidelines, in PPP dollars per person per day; 4.28 globally in 2025.
Prevalence of unaffordability (PUA)
The share of a population whose income, net of basic non-food needs, is below the cost of a healthy diet.
Engel's law
The poorer a household, the larger the share of its budget it spends on food.
Capital recovery factor (CRF)
\(r(1+r)^n/[(1+r)^n-1]\): converts an investment into an equivalent constant annual cost at discount rate \(r\) over \(n\) years.
Levelised cost of food (LCOF)
The constant price per kilogram at which a production system recovers all its discounted costs, including the required return on capital.
Net present value (NPV)
The sum of discounted cash flows minus the investment; the internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which NPV is zero.
Learning rate
The fractional reduction in unit cost for each doubling of cumulative production (Wright's law).
Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient
The cumulative share of a total held by the poorest fraction of units; the Gini is one minus twice the area under the curve (0 = equality, 1 = total concentration).
Rebound effect
The part of an expected saving that is taken back by behavioural or market responses, such as higher consumption of a product that became cheaper.
Net benefit ratio
The difference between the share of income earned from selling a food and the share spent on buying it; it determines whether a price rise helps or hurts a household.
Food sovereignty
The right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food produced through ecologically sound and sustainable methods, and their right to define their own food and agriculture systems (Nyéléni, 2007).
Agroecology
An approach to food systems based on ecological processes, reduced purchased inputs, co-created knowledge and social equity, described by 13 principles (HLPE, 2019).
