Life-cycle thinking: measuring the footprint of food

Is a Swedish greenhouse tomato better for the climate than one trucked from Spain? Is a vertical-farm salad greener than a field salad? Is beef worse than cheese — per kilogram, per gram of protein or per calorie? Questions like these can only be answered with life-cycle assessment (LCA), the standardised method for tracing every input and emission of a product from cradle to grave. This lesson teaches you to do an LCA calculation by hand, to read published footprints critically, and to recognise the choices — functional unit, system boundary, allocation, metric — that can flip a conclusion.

What you will be able to do

  • Describe the four phases of an LCA according to ISO 14040/14044 and define goal, scope, functional unit and system boundary for a food product (LO1, LO3).
  • Build a simple greenhouse-gas inventory from activity data and emission factors, and convert methane and nitrous oxide to CO₂-equivalents with IPCC AR6 GWP100 values; explain what GWP* measures differently (LO3).
  • Handle multifunctional processes with mass, economic and system-expansion allocation, and explain the difference between attributional and consequential LCA (LO3, LO7).
  • Calculate and interpret land occupation, water-scarcity (AWARE) and carbon-opportunity-cost indicators (LO1, LO7).
  • Compare foods per kilogram, per gram of protein and per kilocalorie using the Poore & Nemecek (2018) data, and identify common pitfalls in food footprint claims (LO1, LO7).

Why life-cycle thinking?

Every food product is the visible tip of a long chain of processes: fertiliser plants, tractors, irrigation pumps, feed mills, barns, slaughterhouses, dairies, packaging lines, lorries, cold stores, supermarkets, refrigerators, stoves and waste bins. Each consumes energy and materials and emits substances to air, water and soil. Looking at only one stage invites burden shifting: a vertical farm with no pesticides and tiny land use can emit more greenhouse gas than a field if its electricity is fossil; a "local" product can have a larger footprint than an imported one if it is grown in a heated greenhouse. Life-cycle thinking means refusing to judge a product until the whole chain has been accounted for.

The ISO 14040/14044 framework

LCA is standardised in ISO 14040:2006 (principles and framework) and ISO 14044:2006 (requirements and guidelines, amended in 2017 and 2020). An LCA has four iterative phases (Figure 2):

  1. Goal and scope definition — why is the study done, for whom, and what will be compared? It fixes the functional unit, the system boundary, the allocation procedures, the impact categories and the data-quality requirements.
  2. Life-cycle inventory (LCI) — quantify all relevant inputs (materials, energy, land, water) and outputs (products, emissions, waste) of each process, scaled to the functional unit.
  3. Life-cycle impact assessment (LCIA) — group inventory flows into impact categories (climate change, eutrophication, land use, water scarcity …) and multiply by characterisation factors to obtain indicator results.
  4. Interpretation — check completeness, sensitivity and consistency; draw conclusions and recommendations within the limits of the goal and scope.
EU context

The European Commission's Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) method (Commission Recommendation (EU) 2021/2279) is an ISO-based LCA method with fixed rules — sixteen impact categories, prescribed allocation and data requirements — intended to make footprints of products in the same category comparable. Product-category rules exist for several foods and drinks.

Functional units and system boundaries

The functional unit (FU) is the quantified function the product delivers; all flows are scaled to it. For food the choice is not obvious, because food delivers several functions at once — energy, protein, micronutrients, taste, culture. Common choices are 1 kg of product, 1,000 kcal, 100 g of protein, 1 L of milk, a meal, or a nutrient-density unit such as the Nutrient Rich Foods index (Fulgoni et al., 2009). Sonesson et al. (2017) proposed correcting protein for its quality (digestible indispensable amino acids), and Saarinen et al. (2017) and McLaren et al. (2021) review ways to integrate nutrition into LCA. Converting between functional units is simple arithmetic — but it can reverse a ranking.

Functional-unit conversion(1.3.1)
\[ I_{\text{FU}} = I_{\text{kg}}\;\frac{m_{\text{FU}}}{c}, \qquad \text{e.g.}\quad I_{100\,\text{g prot}} = I_{\text{kg}}\,\frac{100\ \text{g}}{c_{\text{prot}}},\qquad I_{1000\,\text{kcal}} = I_{\text{kg}}\,\frac{1000\ \text{kcal}}{c_{\text{E}}} \]

the impact per functional unit equals the impact per kilogram times the amount of the functional quantity in one functional unit, divided by the content of that quantity per kilogram of food.

  2. Its impact is that mass times the impact per kg: \(I_{\text{kg}}\,m_{\text{FU}}/c\).

Assumptions and validity

Protein-based units treat all protein as equal unless corrected for digestibility and amino-acid composition (DIAAS; see Lesson 12.1). Energy-based units favour energy-dense foods such as oils and sugar that are not nutritionally desirable in large amounts. No single FU captures everything a food does — which is why a diet (a complete set of nutritional functions) is often the most meaningful unit of comparison.

Inventory: activity data × emission factors

Most inventories are built with one simple, powerful equation: multiply each activity (litres of diesel, kg of fertiliser, kWh of electricity, number of animals) by an emission factor — the mass of a substance emitted per unit of activity — and add up.

Inventory: activity × emission factor(1.3.2)
\[ E_{j} = \sum_{i} A_i\,\mathrm{EF}_{i,j} \]

the total emission of substance j is the sum, over all activities i, of the activity level times the emission factor of substance j for that activity.

\(\mathrm{EF}_{i,j}\)emission factor of substance \(j\) for activity \(i\)kg CO₂ L⁻¹, kg N₂O-N (kg N)⁻¹≈ 2.7 kg CO₂ per L diesel burned
\(E_j\)total emission of substance \(j\)kg ha⁻¹ or kg FU⁻¹–
Where do emission factors come from?

Combustion factors follow from fuel chemistry (almost all the carbon in diesel ends up as CO₂). Biological emissions are more uncertain: the IPCC Guidelines for national inventories provide default ("Tier 1") factors, for example that about 1 % of nitrogen applied to soils is emitted directly as N₂O-N (with lower values for dry and higher for wet climates in the 2019 Refinement); Tier 2 and Tier 3 methods use country-specific factors and models. Life-cycle databases (ecoinvent, Agribalyse and national food databases) supply factors for purchased inputs such as fertiliser and electricity.

(L ha⁻¹) × (kg CO₂ L⁻¹) = kg CO₂ ha⁻¹ ✓

An inventory lists many substances; the impact assessment combines them. For climate change, each greenhouse gas is weighted by its global warming potential (GWP): the time-integrated radiative forcing of a pulse emission of 1 kg of the gas over a horizon \(H\) (usually 100 years), relative to 1 kg of CO₂.

CO₂-equivalents with GWP100 (IPCC AR6)(1.3.3)
\[ \text{CO}_2\text{e} = \sum_{j} m_j\,\mathrm{GWP}_{100,j} = m_{\text{CO}_2} + 27.0\,m_{\text{CH}_4,\text{bio}} + 29.8\,m_{\text{CH}_4,\text{fossil}} + 273\,m_{\text{N}_2\text{O}} \]

the carbon-dioxide equivalent is the sum of the masses of each gas multiplied by its 100-year global warming potential: 1 for CO₂, 27.0 for methane of biological origin, 29.8 for fossil methane and 273 for nitrous oxide.

Why fossil and biogenic methane differ

Methane is oxidised in the atmosphere to CO₂. For fossil methane that CO₂ is new to the atmosphere and adds warming; for biogenic methane (from grass eaten by a cow), the carbon was taken from the atmosphere by photosynthesis a short time earlier, so the resulting CO₂ is not counted again. AR6 therefore gives fossil methane a slightly higher GWP100 (29.8) than non-fossil methane (27.0) (Forster et al., 2021, Table 7.15). Older studies use AR4 or AR5 values (e.g., 25, 28 or 34 for methane), which is one reason why published footprints differ.

kg × (kg CO₂e kg⁻¹) = kg CO₂e ✓ (GWP is dimensionless in mass terms)

Direct N₂O is only part of the story: indirect N₂O arises after ammonia volatilisation and nitrate leaching, and the production of mineral nitrogen fertiliser emits CO₂ (natural gas as feedstock and fuel) and, from nitric-acid plants without abatement, N₂O. GWP* — a different question

GWP100 asks: how much warming does a single pulse of this gas cause over 100 years, relative to CO₂? But methane lives only about 12 years, so a herd emitting a constant amount of methane year after year keeps atmospheric methane — and its warming — roughly constant, whereas a constant CO₂ emission keeps adding warming indefinitely. Allen et al. (2018) and Cain et al. (2019) proposed GWP* to convert short-lived emissions into "warming-equivalent" CO₂ emissions that reproduce the temperature effect.

Warming-equivalent emissions (GWP*)(1.3.5)
\[ E^{*}_{\text{CO}_2\text{-we}}(t) = \mathrm{GWP}_{H}\left[\,r\,\frac{E(t)-E(t-\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\,H + s\,E(t)\right] = \mathrm{GWP}_{100}\,\big[\,4.0\,E(t) - 3.75\,E(t-20)\,\big] \]

warming-equivalent CO₂ emissions equal the GWP times the sum of a term proportional to the change in methane emission rate over the last twenty years and a smaller term proportional to the current emission rate; with r = 0.75, s = 0.25, Δt = 20 years and H = 100 years this becomes four times current emissions minus 3.75 times emissions twenty years earlier.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(E(t)\)short-lived (methane) emission rate in year \(t\)t CH₄ yr⁻¹–
\(\Delta t\)time over which the emission change is evaluatedyr20
\(H\)GWP time horizonyr100
\(r,\ s\)weights of the rate-change and stock terms–0.75, 0.25 (Cain et al., 2019)
Follow the derivation
  1. Expand: \(\mathrm{GWP}_{100}[0.75\times(100/20)(E_t - E_{t-20}) + 0.25E_t] = \mathrm{GWP}_{100}[3.75E_t - 3.75E_{t-20} + 0.25E_t]\).
  2. Collect terms: \(\mathrm{GWP}_{100}[4.0E_t - 3.75E_{t-20}]\).
  3. Constant emissions (\(E_t = E_{t-20}\)): \(E^* = 0.25\,\mathrm{GWP}_{100}E_t\) — a quarter of the GWP100 value, reflecting the small additional warming from slow climate adjustment.
  4. Falling emissions (e.g., \(E_t = 0.9E_{t-20}\)): \(4.0\times0.9 - 3.75 = -0.15\): a negative warming-equivalent emission, i.e. the herd is causing cooling relative to today.
Assumptions and controversy

GWP* is good at describing how temperature responds to a methane emission pathway, and is used in scientific assessments of warming. Used as an accounting metric for an individual farm or country, it rewards those who already emit a lot (a large herd that shrinks slightly gets "negative emissions"), raising fairness concerns (Rogelj & Schleussner, 2019). GWP100 remains the metric required by the Paris Agreement reporting rules and by ISO and PEF footprints. Report both, and always state which metric you use.

A national dairy herd emitted 110 kt of enteric methane per year in 2005 and emits 100 kt in 2025. Express the 2025 emissions (a) as GWP100 CO₂e with the AR6 value for biogenic methane and (b) as GWP* warming-equivalents. (c) What would GWP* give if the herd had instead grown to 120 kt?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. GWP100: \(100\ \text{kt}\times27.0 = 2\,700\) kt CO₂e (2.7 Mt).
  2. GWP*: \(27.0\times(4.0\times100 - 3.75\times110) = 27.0\times(400 - 412.5) = -338\) kt CO₂-we: the 9 % decline over 20 years is reducing warming.

Multifunctionality and allocation

Many food processes deliver more than one product: a dairy farm produces milk and meat (culled cows and calves); a soybean crusher produces oil and meal; a flour mill produces flour and bran; a slaughterhouse produces meat, hides and offal. How should the environmental burdens be shared? ISO 14044 prescribes a hierarchy:

  1. Avoid allocation — by dividing the process into sub-processes (e.g., separate the milking parlour from the calf barn) or by system expansion: enlarge the system to include the alternative way of producing the co-product, and credit the avoided burden (substitution).
  2. If allocation cannot be avoided, partition by an underlying physical relationship — mass, energy or protein content, or a causal biological relationship (e.g., the feed energy needed for milk versus growth).
  3. Otherwise, use another relationship, most commonly economic value.
Allocation factors and system expansion(1.3.6)
\[ \mathrm{AF}_i = \frac{m_i\,w_i}{\sum_{k} m_k\,w_k}, \qquad I_i = \mathrm{AF}_i\,I_{\text{total}}, \qquad I_{\text{main}}^{\text{SE}} = I_{\text{total}} - \sum_{\text{co}} m_{\text{co}}\,\mathrm{EF}_{\text{avoided}} \]

the allocation factor of product i is its output mass times its weighting property (1 for mass allocation, the price for economic allocation, the protein content for protein allocation) divided by the same sum over all products; each product carries that share of the total burden. With system expansion, the main product carries the total burden minus the burden avoided by the co-products.

\(w_i\)allocation property: 1 (mass), price (economic), energy or protein content–, € kg⁻¹, MJ kg⁻¹ …
\(\mathrm{AF}_i\)allocation factor (shares sum to 1)–

Assumptions and validity

Allocation is a convention, not a law of nature; ISO requires a sensitivity analysis when the choice matters. Economic allocation follows the reason the process exists but fluctuates with prices. System expansion is conceptually closest to consequential thinking (Section 1.3.6) but depends on what the co-product really displaces — and can produce negative footprints if the credit exceeds the burden. The dairy sector uses a standardised physical ("biophysical") allocation between milk and meat based on the feed energy required for each, published by the International Dairy Federation.

Attributional versus consequential LCA

An attributional LCA (ALCA) describes the share of global burdens that can be attributed to a product, using average data: the average electricity mix, the average feed, the average yield. It answers "what is the footprint of this product, as it is produced today?". A consequential LCA (CLCA) describes how global burdens change as a consequence of a decision, using marginal data: which power plant runs more when demand increases, which land is converted when demand for soy rises, which product is displaced by a co-product (Ekvall & Weidema, 2004). It answers "what happens if we do this?".

Neither mode is "right"; they answer different questions. Carbon labels and national inventories are attributional; a policy analysis of "what if Sweden replaced imported tomatoes with LED-lit greenhouse tomatoes?" is consequential. A classic consequential effect in food LCA is indirect land-use change: using land for one crop pushes production of another crop elsewhere, where forest may be cleared.

Beyond carbon: land, water and nutrients

Land occupation

Land use in LCA is measured as land occupation: area × time, in m²·yr (square-metre years). One hectare occupied for one year is 10,000 m²·yr. For a crop harvested once a year, the land occupation per kilogram is simply the inverse of the yield.

Land occupation per kilogram(1.3.7)
\[ \mathrm{LO} = \frac{A\,t_{\text{occ}}}{Q} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{m}^2\,\text{ha}^{-1}\times t_{\text{occ}}}{Y} \]

land occupation per kilogram equals area times occupation time divided by the quantity produced; for an annual crop, ten thousand square metres per hectare times the occupation time, divided by the yield in kilograms per hectare.

Carbon opportunity cost

Searchinger et al. (2018) argued that land used for food has an opportunity cost: if it were not farmed, it could store carbon in native vegetation and soils. They proposed charging each food with the carbon that its land could otherwise hold — a way of making land use visible in climate terms. Hayek et al. (2021) applied the idea to animal-source foods and concluded that shifting diets could free land whose regrowing vegetation would remove large amounts of CO₂ from the atmosphere.

Carbon opportunity cost (simplified, annualised)(1.3.8)
\[ \mathrm{COC} = \frac{\mathrm{LO}}{10\,000}\;\Delta C\;\frac{44}{12}\;\frac{1}{T} \]

the annualised carbon opportunity cost per kilogram equals the land occupation (converted to hectare-years) times the carbon that native vegetation and soils could store per hectare, converted from carbon to CO₂ by forty-four twelfths, spread over an amortisation period T.

Water: volumes and scarcity

A litre of water withdrawn in rainy Sweden does not have the same consequence as a litre withdrawn from a depleted aquifer in southern Spain. LCA therefore distinguishes withdrawal (water taken), consumption (water not returned to the same catchment, e.g., evaporated) and scarcity-weighted use. The consensus method of the UNEP-SETAC Life Cycle Initiative's WULCA group is AWARE (Available WAter REmaining; Boulay et al., 2018).

Water-scarcity footprint (AWARE)(1.3.9)
\[ \mathrm{CF}_i = \frac{\mathrm{AMD}_{\text{world avg}}}{\mathrm{AMD}_i},\quad 0.1 \le \mathrm{CF}_i \le 100, \qquad \mathrm{WSF} = \sum_i V_{\text{cons},i}\;\mathrm{CF}_i \]

the characterisation factor of a watershed and month is the world-average available water remaining per area divided by the local value, bounded between 0.1 and 100; the water-scarcity footprint is the sum of consumed water volumes multiplied by their local factors.

  2. B: \(0.030\times0.8 = 0.024\) m³ world-eq kg⁻¹.
  3. Ratio: 50 times, although the volumes are identical.
  4. To match B, A would need to consume only \(0.024/40 = 0.0006\) m³ = 0.6 L kg⁻¹ — only possible with closed recirculating systems (Lesson 5.4) or by growing in the less water-scarce season.
A: 1.2 m³ world-eq kg⁻¹; B: 0.024 m³ world-eq kg⁻¹ (×50)

Eutrophication and other categories

Eutrophication potential aggregates emissions of nitrogen and phosphorus compounds (nitrate, ammonia, phosphate) using characterisation factors, often expressed in phosphate-equivalents (g PO₄³⁻-eq) or, in newer methods such as ReCiPe 2016, separately as kg P-eq for fresh water and kg N-eq for marine water (Huijbregts et al., 2017). Food production causes about 78 % of global eutrophication (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). Other categories relevant to food include terrestrial acidification (mainly ammonia), ecotoxicity and human toxicity (pesticides), and biodiversity loss from land use.

Variability, uncertainty and pitfalls

The most important lesson of the Poore & Nemecek (2018) meta-analysis — 38,700 farms and 1,600 processors, packaging types and retailers across 119 countries and 40 products — is not the averages but the variation. Ninetieth-percentile GHG emissions of beef are 105 kg CO₂e per 100 g of protein and its land use 370 m²·yr — 12 and 50 times the 10th-percentile values of dairy beef, whose 10th-percentile values are in turn 36 and 6 times those of peas. Across all products, 25 % of producers contribute on average 53 % of each product's impact, and for beef herds the highest-impact quarter accounts for 56 % of GHG emissions and 61 % of land use. Yet the impacts of the lowest-impact animal products typically still exceed those of vegetable substitutes.

Common misconception

"Eating local is the most effective way to reduce my food's climate footprint." For most foods, transport is a small share of the life-cycle emissions; what is produced and how it is produced matter far more (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). Exceptions exist — air-freighted fresh produce has a large transport footprint — and a Swedish greenhouse product can have a higher footprint than an imported field-grown one if the greenhouse is heated with fossil fuel, or a lower one if it runs on waste heat and renewables. The only way to know is a consistent LCA.

Frontier · as of 2026

Food LCA is moving in three directions. First, nutritional LCA: combining footprints with nutrient-density and protein-quality functional units (Sonesson et al., 2017; McLaren et al., 2021). Second, prospective (ex-ante) LCA of technologies that do not yet exist at scale — cultivated meat, precision fermentation, microbial protein — where the future energy system dominates the result (Sinke et al., 2023; Lesson 15.1). Third, harmonised footprints for policy and labelling, such as the EU PEF method and national food climate databases. All three rely on exactly the equations in this lesson.

Key terms

Life-cycle assessment (LCA)
A standardised method (ISO 14040/14044) for quantifying the resource use and emissions of a product system from raw-material extraction to end of life.
Functional unit
The quantified function of a product system to which all inputs and outputs are scaled (e.g., 1 kg, 1,000 kcal, 100 g protein).
System boundary
The set of processes included in an LCA, e.g., cradle-to-farm-gate, cradle-to-retail or cradle-to-grave.
Life-cycle inventory
The compiled inputs and outputs of all processes in the system, usually computed as activity data times emission factors.
Characterisation factor
A factor converting an inventory flow into its contribution to an impact category (e.g., GWP100 of 273 for N₂O).
GWP100
The 100-year global warming potential: integrated radiative forcing of 1 kg of a gas over 100 years relative to 1 kg of CO₂.
GWP*
A metric converting short-lived climate pollutants into warming-equivalent CO₂ emissions based on changes in their emission rate.
Allocation
Partitioning the burdens of a multifunctional process between its co-products by mass, energy, protein, economic value or another relationship.
System expansion
Avoiding allocation by enlarging the system and crediting co-products with the burden of the products they displace.
Attributional vs consequential LCA
Attributional: the share of current burdens attributed to a product, with average data. Consequential: the change in burdens caused by a decision, with marginal data.
Land occupation
Area × time needed to produce a product, in m²·yr.
AWARE
The consensus water-scarcity characterisation model based on available water remaining per area, with factors from 0.1 to 100 (world average = 1).
Carbon opportunity cost
The carbon that land used for food production could store if it were returned to native vegetation.
