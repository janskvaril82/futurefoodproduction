Innovation pathways: from laboratory to market

In May 2019 the shares of a plant-based burger company rose 163 % on their first day of trading. In 2021 an indoor-farming start-up was valued at US$2.3 billion, and investors put a record amount of money into alternative proteins. By November 2024 that farming company had closed, and in 2025 alternative-protein companies raised less than a fifth of their 2021 total. Were the technologies wrong — or was the path from laboratory to market misjudged? This lesson gives you the concepts and the mathematics to analyse that path: readiness scales, the valley of death, diffusion curves, transition theory, business models, investment cycles and public policy.

What you will be able to do

  • Assess the maturity of a food technology with technology and manufacturing readiness levels, combine readiness dimensions into a score, and explain the "valley of death" with a stage-gate model of risk and capital (LO6, LO3).
  • Explain Rogers' diffusion of innovations — the attributes that speed adoption and the adopter categories of 2.5, 13.5, 34, 34 and 16 % — and relate them to S-curves (LO6).
  • Derive and apply the Bass diffusion model: its closed-form solution, the time and height of the adoption peak, the time to a given market share and the effect of regulatory delay (LO6, LO3).
  • Analyse transitions in the food system with the multi-level perspective (niche, regime, landscape), and interpret business models, experience curves and investment cycles in agrifood technology (LO6, LO1).
  • Evaluate policy instruments — research funding, Horizon Europe, EIT Food, public procurement, subsidies, standards and sandboxes — and EU and Swedish food strategies through the lens of responsible research and innovation (LO6, LO7).

From invention to innovation

An invention is a new idea, device or process; an innovation is an invention that has reached its users. The OECD's Oslo Manual defines a product innovation as a new or improved product that differs significantly from the firm's previous products and "has been made available to potential users" (OECD & Eurostat, 2018). The difference is the subject of this lesson. A cell line that doubles every 20 hours, a strain of yeast that secretes milk protein or an LED recipe that halves the energy per kilogram of lettuce are inventions. They become innovations only when they are produced at scale, authorised, bought — and bought again.

The older linear model of innovation imagined a one-way sequence: basic research, applied research, development, production, marketing. Kline and Rosenberg (1986) replaced it with a chain-linked model, in which knowledge flows back and forth between stages: market signals redirect research, problems in production send engineers back to the laboratory, and users modify products. For new foods the pathway is even more coupled, because five tracks must advance together (Figure 1): the technology itself, manufacturing at scale, regulation (Lessons 14.1–14.3), consumer acceptance (Module 13) and finance. Failure on any one track stops the whole pathway — an authorised product nobody buys fails as surely as a delicious product that cannot be made cheaply.

Big idea

An innovation pathway is a coupled system. Progress is limited by the least ready track — technology, manufacturing, regulation, market or money — not by the most exciting one. The rest of the lesson gives you a quantitative tool for each track.

Readiness levels: how mature is a technology?

The technology readiness level (TRL) scale was developed at NASA in the 1970s to compare technologies of very different kinds, was expanded to nine levels in the 1990s (Mankins, 2009), and was later standardised for space systems in ISO 16290:2013. The European Union adopted it for Horizon 2020 in 2014, and it is now used across EU research and innovation funding. Its great strength is a shared language: an investor, a regulator and an engineer can agree on what "TRL 6" means.

Technology readiness says nothing about whether the product can be made reliably and cheaply at scale. The US Department of Defense therefore added manufacturing readiness levels (MRL 1–10), which run from "basic manufacturing implications identified" (MRL 1) through the capability to produce in a laboratory (MRL 4), a production-relevant prototype (MRL 6), a pilot line ready for low-rate production (MRL 8) to full-rate production with lean practices (MRL 10). For future foods the gap between TRL and MRL is often the whole story: vertical farms and cultivated meat were technically proven long before anyone could show competitive production costs. In practice analysts add further dimensions — regulatory and market readiness — and must decide how to combine them.

Gated TRL and composite readiness(14.4.1)
\[ \text{TRL} = \max\{\,L : e_\ell = 1\ \ \forall\,\ell \le L\,\} \]\[ R_{\text{comp}} = \sum_k w_k\,\frac{L_k}{L_k^{\max}}, \qquad R_{\text{wl}} = \min_k \frac{L_k}{L_k^{\max}} \]

the technology readiness level is the highest level for which the evidence for that level and every level below it is in place; a composite readiness index is the weighted average of the normalised levels of each dimension, and the weakest-link index is the lowest normalised level.

Why "gated", and why two indices?
  1. TRLs are cumulative: a pilot plant (TRL 6) is meaningless if laboratory repeatability (TRL 4) was never shown. The gated rule therefore stops at the first missing level, however much later evidence exists.
  2. A weighted average is compensatory: excellent technology can hide a missing authorisation. The weakest-link index is non-compensatory, matching the coupled-system view of Figure 1. The same issue appears in multi-criteria assessment (Lesson 15.4).
  3. Comparing both indices is informative: a large gap \(R_{\text{comp}} - R_{\text{wl}}\) points to a bottleneck dimension that deserves attention.
Assumptions and validity

Readiness scales are ordinal: TRL 8 is not "twice as ready" as TRL 4, so arithmetic on levels is a convention, not a measurement. Assessments are judgements and should document the evidence behind each level. The regulatory and market scales used in Figure 2 are teaching scales defined in this lesson.

The valley of death

Why do so many promising technologies stall between the laboratory and the market? Two curves explain it. Public research funding is generous for early, uncertain work but thins out once a technology has left the laboratory. Private investors, who need a credible route to returns, prefer later stages with lower risk. Between them lies a stage — pilot and demonstration plants, first commercial production — where capital needs rise steeply while risk is still high. This gap is known as the valley of death; Auerswald and Branscomb (2003) preferred the image of a "Darwinian sea" in which many ventures sink and some reach land, and Beard et al. (2009) formalised the economics of the gap.

Firms and funders manage the journey with a stage-gate process (Cooper, 1990): development is divided into stages, and at each gate the project is continued, redirected or stopped. A little probability arithmetic shows why the valley is so deep for capital-intensive foods.

Probability of success and expected cost of a stage-gate pipeline(14.4.2)
\[ P_{\text{s}} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i, \qquad \mathbb{E}[C] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i \prod_{j<i} p_j, \qquad C_{\text{per success}} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[C]}{P_{\text{s}}} \]

the probability that a project passes all n stages is the product of the stage success probabilities; the expected cost is the sum of each stage's cost weighted by the probability of reaching that stage; and dividing the expected cost by the probability of success gives the capital spent, on average, per successful product.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(p_i\)probability that stage \(i\) succeeds, given that it is reached–
\(C_i\)cost of stage \(i\)M€
\(P_{\text{s}}\)probability of reaching the market–
\(\mathbb{E}[C]\)expected capital spent on one projectM€
Follow the derivation
  1. Stage \(i\) is reached only if stages \(1,\dots,i-1\) all succeed; if success events are independent, that probability is \(\prod_{j<i}p_j\) (empty product = 1 for the first stage).
  2. Stage \(i\)'s cost is paid only if it is reached, so its expected cost is \(C_i\prod_{j<i}p_j\); summing over stages gives \(\mathbb{E}[C]\).
  3. An investor funding many similar projects needs, on average, \(\mathbb{E}[C]\) per project and \(1/P_{\text{s}}\) projects per success, hence \(C_{\text{per success}} = \mathbb{E}[C]/P_{\text{s}}\).
Assumptions and validity

Independence of stage outcomes; costs known in advance; no partial salvage value when a project stops. The order of stages matters: because late stages are the most expensive, cheap early tests that kill bad projects save far more money than they cost — the core logic of stage gates.

Common misconception

"A good technology always finds money." Capital flows to risk-adjusted returns on a time scale investors accept. Indoor farming illustrates the point: Bowery Farming raised about US$472 million and was valued at US$2.3 billion in 2021, yet ceased operations in November 2024; AeroFarms filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in June 2023 and emerged in restructured form in September 2023. The problems were economic — energy, capital cost and prices — rather than technical (compare Lesson 7.2).

Diffusion of innovations

Once a product is on the market, how does it spread? Everett Rogers synthesised decades of studies — the most famous being the adoption of hybrid seed corn by Iowa farmers in the 1930s (Ryan & Gross, 1943) — into the theory of diffusion of innovations: "the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system" (Rogers, 2003). Four elements matter: the innovation, communication channels, time and the social system.

Rogers identified five perceived attributes that explain much of the variation in adoption rates. Apply them to any new food:

  • Relative advantage — is it better (cheaper, tastier, healthier, more sustainable) than what it replaces?
  • Compatibility — does it fit values, habits, recipes and kitchens? Food neophobia (Lesson 13.1) is largely a compatibility problem.
  • Complexity — is it easy to understand and use?
  • Trialability — can people try it cheaply? Tastings, samples and restaurant launches exist for this reason.
  • Observability — are the results visible to others? Many benefits of new foods (lower emissions) are invisible on the plate.

When adoption times in a population are roughly normally distributed, Rogers divided adopters by standard deviations from the mean adoption time into five categories (Figure 4). The cumulative number of adopters then follows an S-shaped curve. Moore (1991) later argued that a "chasm" separates the enthusiastic first 16 % — innovators and early adopters — from the pragmatic early majority, who want proven, complete, affordable products.

Rogers' adopter categories from a normal distribution of adoption times(14.4.3)
\[ s_1 = \Phi(-2),\qquad s_2 = \Phi(-1)-\Phi(-2),\qquad s_3 = \Phi(0)-\Phi(-1) \]\[ s_4 = \Phi(1)-\Phi(0),\qquad s_5 = 1-\Phi(1) \]

the shares of innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards are the areas under the standard normal curve beyond two standard deviations before the mean, between two and one, between one and the mean, between the mean and one after, and beyond one standard deviation after the mean.

Where do the numbers come from?
  1. Standardise adoption time: \(z = (t-\mu)/\sigma\). The category boundaries are \(z = -2, -1, 0, 1\).
  2. \(\Phi(-2) = 0.0228\), \(\Phi(-1) = 0.1587\), \(\Phi(0) = 0.5\), \(\Phi(1) = 0.8413\).
  3. Differences give 2.28, 13.59, 34.13, 34.13 and 15.87 %. Rogers rounded them to 2.5, 13.5, 34, 34 and 16 % so that they sum to 100.
  4. The early-adopter category ends at \(\mu - \sigma\), so 16 % of adopters (innovators plus early adopters) have adopted when the early majority begins — Moore's "chasm".
Assumptions and validity

Real adoption-time distributions are often skewed, and categories are ideal types, not natural groups. Rogers himself warned against a pro-innovation bias (assuming that everyone should adopt) and an individual-blame bias (blaming "laggards" rather than the system that makes adoption hard for them).

The Bass model: a quantitative S-curve

Rogers explains why adoption follows an S-curve; Frank Bass (1969) explained how fast. His model starts from a simple behavioural assumption: the probability that someone who has not yet adopted does so now depends on an external influence that acts on everyone (advertising, media, a regulator's approval) and on an internal influence that grows with the number of people who have already adopted (word of mouth, imitation, visibility). Originally written for consumer durables, it has become the standard model of new-product diffusion; a meta-analysis of 213 applications found average values of about \(p = 0.03\) and \(q = 0.38\) per year (Sultan et al., 1990; Mahajan et al., 1990).

The Bass diffusion model(14.4.4)
\[ \frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)} = p + q\,F(t) \]\[ \Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} = \big[p + qF(t)\big]\big[1 - F(t)\big], \qquad F(0) = 0 \]

the rate of adoption among those who have not yet adopted — the hazard — equals a constant coefficient of innovation plus a coefficient of imitation times the fraction who have already adopted. Equivalently, the growth rate of the adopted fraction is the hazard times the fraction that has not yet adopted.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(F(t)\)fraction of the eventual market that has adopted by time \(t\)–0 → 1
\(f(t) = \mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}t\)adoption rate: new adopters per unit time as a fraction of the marketa⁻¹–
\(p\)coefficient of innovation (external influence)a⁻¹≈ 0.03 (0.001–0.1)
\(q\)coefficient of imitation (internal influence)a⁻¹≈ 0.38 (0.1–0.8)
\(m\)market potential: number who will eventually adopt; \(N(t) = mF(t)\)adopters–
Where does the model come from?
  1. Let \(h(t)\) be the conditional probability per unit time that a non-adopter adopts (a hazard rate, as in reliability engineering). Among non-adopters \(1-F\), the adoption rate is \(f = h(1-F)\).
  2. Bass assumed that the hazard is linear in the adopted fraction: \(h = p + qF\). The constant \(p\) acts even when nobody has adopted; \(qF\) grows as adopters become visible.
  3. Special cases: with \(q = 0\), \(F = 1 - e^{-pt}\) (pure external influence, a modified exponential curve); with \(p \to 0\) the model becomes logistic growth, which needs an initial seed of adopters to start.
\(\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}t\) in a⁻¹; \([p + qF]\) in a⁻¹ times the dimensionless \([1-F]\) → a⁻¹ ✓
Closed-form solution of the Bass model(14.4.5)
\[ F(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-(p+q)t}}{1 + \dfrac{q}{p}\,e^{-(p+q)t}} \]\[ f(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{(p+q)^2}{p}\,\frac{e^{-(p+q)t}}{\Big(1 + \dfrac{q}{p}\,e^{-(p+q)t}\Big)^{2}} \]

the adopted fraction equals one minus a decaying exponential, divided by one plus the ratio of imitation to innovation times the same exponential; the adoption rate is its time derivative, a bell-shaped curve.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(p + q\)overall speed of diffusiona⁻¹
\(q/p\)relative strength of imitation; controls the shape (skew) of the curve–
Follow the derivation
  1. Separate the variables in Eq. 14.4.4: \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}F}{(p+qF)(1-F)} = \mathrm{d}t\).
  2. Partial fractions. Write \(\dfrac{1}{(p+qF)(1-F)} = \dfrac{A}{p+qF} + \dfrac{B}{1-F}\). Then \(1 = A(1-F) + B(p+qF)\). Setting \(F = 1\) gives \(B = 1/(p+q)\); setting \(F = -p/q\) gives \(A = q/(p+q)\).
  3. Integrate: \(\dfrac{1}{p+q}\left[\ln(p+qF) - \ln(1-F)\right] = t + K\), i.e., \(\dfrac{1}{p+q}\ln\dfrac{p+qF}{1-F} = t + K\).
  4. Initial condition \(F(0) = 0\): \(K = \dfrac{\ln p}{p+q}\), so \(\ln\dfrac{p+qF}{p(1-F)} = (p+q)t\), and \(\dfrac{p+qF}{p(1-F)} = e^{(p+q)t}\).
  5. Solve for F. With \(E = e^{(p+q)t}\): \(p + qF = pE - pEF\) ⇒ \(F(q + pE) = p(E-1)\) ⇒ \(F = \dfrac{p(E-1)}{q + pE}\). Divide numerator and denominator by \(pE\) to obtain Eq. 14.4.5.
  6. Differentiate. Writing \(x = e^{-(p+q)t}\) and \(F = (1-x)/(1 + \tfrac{q}{p}x)\): \(\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}x = -(1 + \tfrac{q}{p})/(1+\tfrac{q}{p}x)^2\) and \(\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = -(p+q)x\), which gives \(f(t)\).
  7. Check: \(F(0) = 0\); \(F(\infty) = 1\); \(f(0) = p\) — at launch only innovators adopt.
exponent \((p+q)t\): a⁻¹ × a → dimensionless ✓; \(f\): (a⁻¹)²/a⁻¹ → a⁻¹ ✓
Peak of adoption and time to a market share(14.4.6)
\[ t^{*} = \frac{\ln(q/p)}{p+q}, \qquad F(t^{*}) = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{p}{2q}, \qquad f(t^{*}) = \frac{(p+q)^2}{4q} \]\[ t_s = \frac{1}{p+q}\,\ln\frac{1 + \frac{q}{p}\,s}{1-s} \]

adoption peaks at a time equal to the logarithm of the imitation-to-innovation ratio divided by their sum; at the peak slightly less than half the market has adopted, and the peak rate is the square of the sum divided by four times q. The time needed to reach any share s follows from inverting the solution.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(t^{*}\)time of the adoption peak (exists only if \(q > p\))a

Follow the derivation
  1. With \(x = e^{-(p+q)t}\), \(f \propto x/(1+\tfrac{q}{p}x)^2\). Its derivative with respect to \(x\) is proportional to \((1+\tfrac{q}{p}x)(1 - \tfrac{q}{p}x)\), which vanishes at \(x = p/q\).
  2. So \(e^{-(p+q)t^*} = p/q\) ⇒ \(t^* = \ln(q/p)/(p+q)\). If \(q \le p\), \(t^* \le 0\): adoption is highest at launch and falls thereafter (no interior peak).
  3. Substituting \(x = p/q\): \(F(t^*) = (1 - p/q)/2\) and \(f(t^*) = \dfrac{(p+q)^2}{p}\cdot\dfrac{p/q}{4} = \dfrac{(p+q)^2}{4q}\).
  4. Inverting Eq. 14.4.5 for \(F = s\): \(e^{-(p+q)t} = (1-s)/(1 + \tfrac{q}{p}s)\), which gives \(t_s\).

A precision-fermented dairy protein is launched in 2027 with \(p = 0.01\) a⁻¹ and \(q = 0.35\) a⁻¹ (word of mouth dominates). Its market potential is 2 million households. Compute (a) the share and number of adopting households after 5 years, (b) the time and calendar year of the adoption peak and the number of new households in the peak year, (c) when half the potential market has adopted.

Worked example 4What does a regulatory delay cost in adoption?

The same product is launched in Singapore in 2026 but in the EU only 3.3 years later, the average length of the novel food procedure (Lesson 14.1). Assuming identical \(p = 0.01\) and \(q = 0.35\) in both markets, what fraction of each market has adopted by 2036?

Reveal the reasoning

S-curves, substitution and experience curves

Many new foods do not create a new market; they substitute for an existing product — plant-based milk for cow's milk, LED for high-pressure sodium lamps in greenhouses, hybrid for open-pollinated corn. Fisher and Pry (1971) observed that such substitutions often follow a logistic curve in the market share \(f\) of the new product. Griliches (1957) had found the same shape for hybrid corn across US states, with the timing and speed of adoption explained largely by its profitability in each region.

Fisher–Pry substitution model(14.4.7)
\[ \ln\frac{f}{1-f} = b\,(t - t_{h}), \qquad \Delta t_{10\to90} = \frac{\ln 81}{b} = \frac{4.39}{b} \]

the logarithm of the ratio between the new product's share and the old product's share rises linearly with time at rate b, passing zero at the half-substitution time; the time to go from 10 to 90 per cent market share is the natural logarithm of 81 divided by b.

\(b\)substitution rate constanta⁻¹

\(\Delta t_{10\to90}\)"takeover time" from 10 % to 90 % sharea
Follow the derivation
  1. At \(f = 0.9\): \(\ln(0.9/0.1) = \ln 9\); at \(f = 0.1\): \(\ln(1/9) = -\ln 9\).
  2. The difference is \(2\ln 9 = \ln 81 = 4.394\), reached in time \(\Delta t = 4.394/b\).
  3. Plotting \(\ln[f/(1-f)]\) against time turns the S-curve into a straight line whose slope is \(b\) — a simple way to fit and to extrapolate substitution data.
Assumptions and validity

Assumes that the substitution, once started, proceeds to completion and that the market's total size is not what changes. Many food substitutions stall at partial shares (plant-based milks coexist with dairy); extrapolating a logistic fit from early data is risky.

\(\ln\) is dimensionless; dimensionless / a⁻¹ → a ✓

Why do some substitutions proceed fast and others stall? Price is usually decisive, and prices of new technologies fall as producers gain experience. Wright (1936) noticed that the labour needed to build an aircraft fell by a constant fraction every time cumulative production doubled. This experience curve (Wright's law) has since described solar panels, wind turbines, batteries and many other technologies; Way et al. (2022) showed that cost forecasts based on such curves have been well calibrated in the past, whereas many energy-economy models repeatedly underestimated how fast the costs of solar power, wind power and batteries would fall. Whether cultivated meat or precision fermentation will follow a steep experience curve is one of the central open questions of this course (Lesson 12.4).

Experience curve (Wright's law)(14.4.8)
\[ C(X) = C_0\left(\frac{X}{X_0}\right)^{-b} = C_0\,(1-\text{LR})^{\log_2(X/X_0)}, \qquad \text{LR} = 1 - 2^{-b} \]\[ n = \frac{\ln(C_{\text{target}}/C_0)}{\ln(1-\text{LR})} \]

the unit cost after cumulative production X equals the initial cost times the growth factor of cumulative production raised to minus b; equivalently, every doubling of cumulative production multiplies cost by one minus the learning rate, and the number of doublings needed to reach a target cost is the logarithm of the cost ratio divided by the logarithm of one minus the learning rate.

  3. Setting \(C = C_{\text{target}}\) and solving for the exponent gives \(n\); the required growth of cumulative production is \(2^n\).
Assumptions and validity

Experience curves are empirical regularities, not laws of nature. Costs bounded by physics or by commodity inputs (feedstock sugars, amino acids, energy) fall more slowly than those dominated by equipment and know-how, and learning can stall.

Transitions: the multi-level perspective

Diffusion models treat an innovation as a product meeting consumers. Food-system change is more than that: it involves farms, processors, retailers, rules, infrastructures, subsidies, habits and meanings that have co-evolved for decades and stabilise each other. Frank Geels' multi-level perspective (MLP) describes such socio-technical transitions as the interplay of three levels (Geels, 2002):

  • Niches — protected spaces (research projects, start-ups, subsidised pilots, dedicated consumer groups) where radical innovations are developed and learned about.
  • The socio-technical regime — the dominant, aligned set of technologies, industries, markets, user practices, policies, science and cultural meanings. Regimes change mostly incrementally and resist radical change.
  • The socio-technical landscape — slow trends and sudden shocks outside the direct influence of regime actors: climate change, demographic and dietary trends, pandemics, wars, energy crises, interest rates.

Transitions occur when niche innovations gain internal momentum, landscape pressures destabilise the regime, and the resulting windows of opportunity let niche innovations break through. Geels and Schot (2007) distinguished four typical pathways: transformation (the regime reorients under pressure while incumbents survive), reconfiguration (niche innovations are first adopted as add-ons and then change the regime's architecture), technological substitution (a mature niche replaces the regime under strong pressure) and de-alignment and re-alignment (the regime erodes before a successor is ready). Strategic niche management asks how policy can create and protect niches for learning (Kemp et al., 1998). The MLP has been applied to the protein transition, for example to the rise of plant-based meat substitutes (Tziva et al., 2020), and criticised for vagueness about agency and power, which later work addresses (Geels, 2011, 2019; Markard et al., 2012).

Regulation as a regime element

Seen through the MLP, the rules of Lessons 14.1–14.3 are not neutral background: novel-food procedures, organic definitions, reserved meat names and bans are part of the regime. They can protect incumbents (a ban), open windows (the NGT regulation, the UK Precision Breeding Act, Singapore's framework) or create protected spaces for learning (the UK sandbox). Incumbents also move: the Swiss meat processor Bell Food Group has partnered with the cultivated-meat company Mosa Meat, a typical reconfiguration move.

Business models and investment cycles

A business model describes how a firm creates, delivers and captures value: its value proposition, customers and channels, key resources and partners, revenue streams and cost structure (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010). The last row explains a recurring story. Asset-heavy models need cheap capital, and between 2019 and 2021 capital was cheap. The Good Food Institute describes the investment environment after 2022 as "fundamentally different from the low-interest-rate period before 2022, when many of the largest rounds in privately held alternative protein companies were raised" (GFI, 2026). Investment in alternative proteins peaked at about US$5 billion in 2021; companies raised US$1.1 billion in 2024, US$881 million in 2025 and US$359 million in the first half of 2026, bringing the total since 2017 to more than US$19.5 billion. In 2025 plant-based companies raised US$450 million (up from US$342 million in 2024), fermentation companies US$357 million (down from US$632 million) and cultivated meat and seafood companies US$74 million (down from US$144 million).

Public markets told the same story. Beyond Meat raised US$240 million in its initial public offering in May 2019 and its shares rose 163 % on the first day; in 2024 the company reported revenue of US$326 million and a net loss of US$160 million. Such boom-and-bust patterns are often described with Gartner's "hype cycle" — a peak of inflated expectations, a trough of disillusionment and a slope of enlightenment — but a review found little empirical support for the hype cycle as a general model (Dedehayir & Steinert, 2016). The Bass model offers a more testable explanation: investors extrapolated the fast early growth of the steepest part of an S-curve, or mistook an innovators-and-early-adopters market (16 %) for the mass market.

Lessons from the 2021 peak

Three patterns recur in the failures: (1) scaling production before unit economics were proven — a violation of stage-gate logic; (2) business models that depend on cheap capital and cheap energy, exposed by the landscape shocks of 2022; and (3) confusing trial with adoption — first purchases driven by curiosity, not followed by repeat purchase. None of these is a verdict on the underlying technologies.

Policy instruments, strategies and responsible innovation

Why should governments intervene at all? Because markets under-supply innovation when knowledge spills over to competitors, when environmental costs such as greenhouse-gas emissions are not priced, when actors cannot coordinate investments in shared infrastructure, and when regulatory uncertainty deters investment. Policy instruments are commonly grouped into supply-push measures that lower the cost of producing innovations (research grants, pilot facilities, tax credits), demand-pull measures that create or enlarge markets (public procurement, subsidies, taxes, standards) and systemic measures that build networks, rules and capabilities (innovation communities, regulatory sandboxes, missions). Public procurement is a powerful but under-used demand-side tool (Edler & Georghiou, 2007), and mission-oriented policy combines all three types around a societal goal (Mazzucato, 2018).

The European Union

  • Horizon Europe, the EU research and innovation programme for 2021–2027, has a budget of €95.5 billion. Its Cluster 6 covers food, bioeconomy, natural resources, agriculture and environment, and its missions include "A Soil Deal for Europe".
  • The European Institute of Innovation and Technology (EIT), established in 2008, funds Knowledge and Innovation Communities that integrate education, research and business. EIT Food, created from the 2016 call, runs education, start-up and innovation programmes across the food value chain.
  • The Farm to Fork Strategy, presented on 20 May 2020 as part of the European Green Deal, set targets for 2030: halving the use and risk of chemical pesticides, reducing nutrient losses by at least 50 % and fertiliser use by at least 20 %, halving sales of antimicrobials for farmed animals and aquaculture, and reaching 25 % of agricultural land under organic farming. Several of its legislative follow-ups stalled: the proposed Sustainable Use of Pesticides Regulation was rejected by Parliament in November 2023 and withdrawn in 2024, and the promised framework law for sustainable food systems was not proposed.
  • The Vision for Agriculture and Food (COM(2025) 75, 19 February 2025) shifted the emphasis to competitiveness, resilience and simplification. Its four priority areas are an attractive agri-food sector, a competitive and resilient sector, a future-proof sector, and fair living and working conditions. It announces a new strategic approach to research and innovation, a plan to address protein supply challenges, a long-term strategy on livestock and a stronger role for public procurement.

Sweden

Sweden's national food strategy (prop. 2016/17:104), approved by the Riksdag in 2017, sets an overall goal for 2030 of "a competitive food supply chain in which total food production increases, while relevant national environmental objectives are met", to create growth and employment throughout the country. In March 2025 the government presented Livsmedelsstrategin 2.0, which emphasises robust food supply and increased production in peacetime as the basis for preparedness in crisis and war, together with exports. In July 2026 a committee of inquiry (dir. 2026:77) was asked to propose a growth agenda with concrete, measurable targets for Swedish food production for 2031–2045, reporting by 8 June 2028. The directive notes that technological development "now in some cases makes food production possible in ways and places that were previously not possible" — a direct reference to the systems of this course (Regeringen, 2026). Public funders of food research and innovation in Sweden include Vinnova and Formas, alongside EU programmes.

Responsible research and innovation

Policies decide not only how fast innovations spread but also which innovations, and for whom. The concept of responsible research and innovation (RRI), promoted in EU research policy from the early 2010s, answers the question of how to steer innovation towards societal goals. Von Schomberg (2013) defined it as "a transparent, interactive process by which societal actors and innovators become mutually responsive to each other with a view to the (ethical) acceptability, sustainability and societal desirability of the innovation process and its marketable products". Key terms

Innovation
A new or improved product or process that differs significantly from previous ones and has been made available to users or brought into use (Oslo Manual).
Technology readiness level (TRL)
A nine-level scale of technological maturity, from basic principles observed (TRL 1) to an actual system proven in its operational environment (TRL 9).
Manufacturing readiness level (MRL)
A ten-level scale of maturity of production capability, from manufacturing implications identified to full-rate production.
Valley of death
The funding gap between public research support and private investment, typically at the pilot and demonstration stages.
Stage-gate process
Development divided into stages separated by decision points (gates) at which projects are continued, redirected or stopped.
Diffusion of innovations
The process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system (Rogers).
Adopter categories
Innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards — 2.5, 13.5, 34, 34 and 16 % of adopters.
Bass model
A diffusion model in which the hazard of adoption is \(p + qF\); its closed-form solution gives an S-shaped cumulative adoption curve.
Coefficients of innovation and imitation
The Bass parameters \(p\) (external influence) and \(q\) (internal influence, word of mouth), in a⁻¹.
Fisher–Pry substitution
A logistic model of a new product replacing an old one, in which \(\ln[f/(1-f)]\) rises linearly with time.
Experience curve
The regularity that unit cost falls by a constant fraction (the learning rate) with each doubling of cumulative production (Wright's law).
Multi-level perspective
A framework that explains socio-technical transitions as interactions between niches, regimes and the landscape (Geels).
Socio-technical regime
The dominant, mutually aligned set of technologies, industries, markets, practices, rules and meanings in a sector.
Supply-push and demand-pull
Policies that lower the cost of producing innovations (grants, pilots) versus policies that create markets for them (procurement, prices, standards).
Responsible research and innovation
An approach to steering innovation through anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion and responsiveness towards acceptable, sustainable and desirable outcomes.
