Water relations, transpiration and vapour pressure deficit

A lettuce plant in a vertical farm drinks several times its own fresh weight in water before harvest — and releases almost all of it back into the room as vapour. That invisible stream cools the leaves, carries calcium to growing tissues, and forms the largest latent-heat load that the air-conditioning system must remove. This lesson follows water from the nutrient solution to the air with the physics of water potential, builds the conductance models that engineers use to predict transpiration, derives the Penman–Monteith equation step by step, and turns it all into practical rules for managing vapour pressure deficit in greenhouses and plant factories.

What you will be able to do

  • Define water potential and its osmotic, pressure, gravitational and matric components, and calculate osmotic potential with the van 't Hoff relation (LO2, LO3).
  • Describe the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum with the cohesion–tension theory and an Ohm's-law analogy, and compute water potentials along the pathway (LO2, LO3).
  • Explain how stomata regulate gas exchange, and calculate transpiration from stomatal and boundary-layer conductances and the leaf-to-air vapour pressure deficit (LO2, LO3).
  • Set up the leaf energy balance and derive and apply the Penman–Monteith equation to a greenhouse or vertical-farm canopy (LO3).
  • Justify target VPD ranges for controlled environments and explain the links between transpiration, calcium transport, tipburn and water-use efficiency (LO2, LO7).

Why water is an engineering problem in CEA

Of all the water a crop takes up through its roots, only a tiny fraction stays in the plant: typically more than 95 % is transpired, a few per cent end up as tissue water, and well under one per cent is split in photosynthesis (Taiz et al., 2022). Transpiration is not a design flaw. It is the unavoidable price of photosynthesis: leaves must open pores to let CO₂ in, and a wet surface exposed to unsaturated air loses water. But plants have turned the price into a service. The water flow cools leaves under strong light, it delivers nutrients — above all calcium, which moves almost exclusively with the transpiration stream — and it keeps cells turgid so that they can expand.

For an environmental engineer designing a plant factory, transpiration has a second face: every kilogram of water transpired carries about 2.45 MJ of latent heat into the air, which must be removed again by dehumidification. Graamans et al. (2017) showed that crop transpiration is therefore a central design parameter for the climate systems of closed plant factories, and in Lesson 8.1 you will see that dehumidification can be one of the largest electricity uses after lighting. In a greenhouse, by contrast, transpiration is the main cooling mechanism in summer and a source of disease-promoting humidity in winter. Controlling water vapour is therefore a balancing act between plant physiology, disease risk and energy.

Big idea

Water always moves from higher to lower water potential. From the root zone to the leaf the water potential falls by a fraction of a megapascal; from the leaf to the air it falls by tens of megapascals. The steepest drop is at the leaf surface, which is why stomata and the vapour pressure deficit of the air control how fast a plant transpires.

Water potential: the currency of water movement

Why does water move into a root from a nutrient solution, up a stem against gravity, and out of a leaf into the air? In each case the driving force is a difference in the free energy of water. Plant physiologists express that free energy per unit volume, which gives it the units of pressure, and call it water potential, Ψ (psi). Pure, free water at atmospheric pressure and the same temperature and height is the reference, Ψ = 0. Anything that lowers the free energy of water — dissolved solutes, binding to surfaces, suction — makes Ψ negative; positive pressure makes it positive.

Water potential and its components(2.2.1)
\[ \Psi_w = \frac{\mu_w-\mu_w^{\circ}}{\bar V_w} = \Psi_s + \Psi_p + \Psi_g + \Psi_m, \qquad \Psi_g = \rho_w\,g\,h \]

water potential is the chemical potential of water relative to pure water, divided by the molar volume of water. It is the sum of an osmotic (solute) component, a pressure component, a gravitational component and a matric component; the gravitational component equals the density of water times gravitational acceleration times height above the reference level.

  1. The chemical potential \(\mu_w\) (J mol⁻¹) is the free energy per mole of water. Water moves spontaneously from high to low \(\mu_w\).
  2. Dividing by the molar volume \(\bar V_w\) (m³ mol⁻¹) converts J mol⁻¹ into J m⁻³ = N m m⁻³ = N m⁻² = Pa. That is why water potential has pressure units and can be compared directly with turgor pressure or xylem tension.
  3. Because the free-energy effects of solutes, pressure, height and surface binding are (to good approximation) independent, they add. Plant physiologists often combine \(\Psi_m\) into \(\Psi_p\) inside cells and ignore \(\Psi_g\) over short heights.
  4. Height: \(\Psi_g = \rho_wgh = 1000\times9.81\times1 = 9810\) Pa ≈ 0.01 MPa per metre — negligible for a lettuce, but 0.1 MPa at the top of a 10 m tree.
Assumptions and validity

Components are defined relative to pure water at the same temperature. Across a membrane that is not perfectly semipermeable, only a fraction (the reflection coefficient σ) of the osmotic potential difference drives flow. In the xylem, which has very few solutes, \(\Psi_w\approx\Psi_p\) (a negative tension).

The osmotic component deserves a closer look, because it connects plant water relations to the chemistry of the nutrient solution. For dilute solutions it is given by a relation that Jacobus van 't Hoff derived by analogy with the ideal-gas law — he received the first Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1901) partly for it:

Osmotic potential (van 't Hoff relation)(2.2.2)
\[ \Psi_s = -\,c_s\,R\,T \]

the osmotic potential equals minus the total concentration of dissolved particles (osmolality) times the gas constant times the absolute temperature.

Follow the derivation
  1. The chemical potential of water in a solution is \(\mu_w = \mu_w^\circ + RT\ln a_w\), where \(a_w\) is the activity (≈ mole fraction \(x_w\)) of water.
  2. For a dilute solution \(x_w = 1-x_s\) and \(\ln(1-x_s)\approx -x_s\), so \(\mu_w-\mu_w^\circ\approx -RTx_s\).
  3. Divide by \(\bar V_w\): \(\Psi_s = -RTx_s/\bar V_w\). In a dilute solution \(x_s/\bar V_w\) is the number of moles of solute particles per volume of water, i.e., the concentration \(c_s\). Hence \(\Psi_s = -c_sRT\).
  4. Worked number: cell sap of 300 mmol L⁻¹ = 300 mol m⁻³ at 25 °C: \(\Psi_s = -300\times8.314\times298.15 = -7.44\times10^5\) Pa = −0.74 MPa.
Assumptions and validity

Ideal, dilute solution. Electrolytes dissociate (count all ions) and interact (activity coefficients below 1), so a measured osmolality is more reliable than a calculation from the recipe. The relation is excellent for nutrient solutions and adequate for cell sap.

mol m⁻³ · J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ · K = J m⁻³ = Pa ✓ (divide by 10⁶ for MPa)

Water crosses membranes mostly through aquaporins, water-channel proteins whose opening is regulated by phosphorylation, pH and calcium; they allow plants to change the hydraulic conductance of roots and leaves within minutes (Chaumont & Tyerman, 2014). The volume flux across a membrane is proportional to the water-potential difference, \(J_v = L_p\,\Delta\Psi\), where \(L_p\) is the hydraulic conductivity — a first appearance of the Ohm's-law thinking that we develop next.

The soil–plant–atmosphere continuum

How does water reach the top of a tomato plant three metres tall, or a 100 m redwood? Not by being pushed from below, and not by capillarity alone. According to the cohesion–tension theory proposed by Dixon and Joly (1895), evaporation from the cell walls inside the leaf pulls water up through the xylem as a continuous column held together by the strong cohesion of water molecules (hydrogen bonds). The xylem sap is therefore under tension (negative pressure), typically a few tenths of a megapascal in well-watered crops and several megapascals in drought-stressed trees (Tyree & Zimmermann, 2002). The whole pathway — nutrient solution or soil, root, stem, leaf and air — is called the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum (SPAC).

Why is the air value so extreme? Water vapour in air is in equilibrium with liquid water of a certain water potential, given by a relation that follows directly from the chemical potential of an ideal gas:

Water potential of air (Kelvin relation)(2.2.3)
\[ \Psi_{\text{air}} = \frac{R\,T}{\bar V_w}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{RH}}{100}\right) = \frac{R\,T}{\bar V_w}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{e}{e_s}\right) \]

the water potential of moist air equals the gas constant times absolute temperature divided by the molar volume of water, times the natural logarithm of the relative humidity expressed as a fraction (the ratio of actual to saturation vapour pressure).

Follow the derivation
  1. For an ideal gas, the chemical potential of water vapour at partial pressure \(e\) is \(\mu = \mu_{\text{sat}} + RT\ln(e/e_s)\), where \(\mu_{\text{sat}}\) is its value at saturation — which equals the chemical potential of pure liquid water at the same temperature (vapour and liquid in equilibrium).
  2. Hence \(\mu - \mu_w^\circ = RT\ln(e/e_s)\). Divide by \(\bar V_w\) (Eq. 2.2.1) to get \(\Psi_{\text{air}}\).
  3. At 20 °C: \(RT/\bar V_w = 8.314\times293.15/18.05\times10^{-6} = 1.35\times10^8\) Pa = 135 MPa.
  4. At RH = 60 %: \(\Psi = 135\times\ln0.60 = -69\) MPa. Even at 98 % RH, Ψ = −2.7 MPa — lower than the leaf. Only in nearly saturated air does the gradient vanish.
Why is the leaf's own internal air nearly saturated?

Inside the leaf, the air spaces are in contact with wet cell walls at Ψ ≈ −0.5 MPa. Inverting Eq. 2.2.3, the equilibrium relative humidity is \(\exp(-0.5/135) = 0.9963\): 99.6 %. For practical calculations we therefore assume that the vapour pressure inside the leaf equals the saturation vapour pressure at leaf temperature, \(e_s(T_{\text{leaf}})\) — the basis of Eq. 2.2.6.

Within the plant, van den Honert (1948) recognised that the steady flow of water resembles an electric current through resistors in series: the same flux passes every segment, and each segment consumes a share of the total potential difference in proportion to its resistance.

Ohm's-law analogy for the SPAC(2.2.4)
\[ E = \frac{\Psi_{\text{rz}}-\Psi_{\text{leaf}}}{R_{\text{root}}+R_{\text{stem}}+R_{\text{leaf}}} = K_{\text{plant}}\,(\Psi_{\text{rz}}-\Psi_{\text{leaf}}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Psi_{\text{leaf}} = \Psi_{\text{rz}} - \frac{E}{K_{\text{plant}}} \]

the transpiration flux equals the drop in water potential from the root zone to the leaf divided by the sum of the hydraulic resistances in series, or equivalently times the whole-plant hydraulic conductance. Rearranged: leaf water potential is the root-zone potential minus the flux divided by the conductance.

  1. In steady state no water accumulates in any segment, so the same flux \(E\) passes through root, stem and leaf (like current through resistors in series).
  2. For each segment, flux = conductance × potential drop: \(E = (\Psi_{i}-\Psi_{i+1})/R_i\).
  3. Adding the drops: \(\Psi_{\text{rz}}-\Psi_{\text{leaf}} = E(R_{\text{root}}+R_{\text{stem}}+R_{\text{leaf}})\).
  4. The larger the transpiration, the more negative the leaf water potential must become — which is why leaves lose turgor at midday under high VPD even in a well-watered hydroponic system when root conductance is low (for instance in cold or poorly oxygenated nutrient solution; see Lesson 5.3).
Assumptions and validity

Steady state; resistances independent of flux (in reality root conductance changes with aquaporin activity and xylem conductance falls when cavitation embolises vessels). Capacitance (water stored in tissues) buffers fast changes and is neglected. The vapour phase from leaf to air is not treated with this equation, because its driving force is a vapour-pressure difference, not a water-potential difference (Eq. 2.2.5).

(mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ MPa⁻¹) · MPa = mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ ✓

Where does the resistance sit? In the root, water must cross the endodermis, whose radial walls carry a band of hydrophobic suberin and lignin, the Casparian strip (Figure 4). Water and ions that travelled through the porous cell walls (the apoplastic path) are forced here to cross a plasma membrane, which gives the plant control over what enters its xylem. Water also moves from cell to cell through plasmodesmata (symplastic path) or across membranes through aquaporins (transcellular path). Steudle (2000) combined these routes in a composite transport model that explains why root hydraulic conductance varies so much with conditions.

(c) The solution cools to 10 °C and root conductance halves. What is the new leaf water potential? (d) Compare the leaf-to-air drop with the root-to-leaf drop if the air is at 22 °C and 70 % RH.

Root pressure and guttation

At night, when stomata are nearly closed and transpiration stops, roots keep loading ions into the xylem. The xylem sap then has a lower osmotic potential than the root zone, water follows osmotically, and a positive root pressure of up to a few tenths of a megapascal develops. If the air is humid, water is pushed out through hydathodes at the leaf margins as droplets — guttation (Figure 5). Guttation is harmless in itself, but the droplets contain salts and sugars and are a pathway for pathogens (Singh, 2014), and in greenhouses it is a visible sign that night-time humidity is high and transpiration very low. As you will see in Section 2.2.8, root pressure is also thought to help deliver calcium to leaves that barely transpire.

Stomata: adjustable valves

Leaves are wrapped in a waxy cuticle that is almost impermeable to water and CO₂. Gas exchange happens through stomata, microscopic pores each bordered by two guard cells (Figure 6). Stomata typically cover only about 1 % of the leaf surface when open, yet they can carry most of the leaf's gas exchange because diffusion through many small pores is remarkably efficient. Guard cells open the pore by accumulating K⁺ and anions (Cl⁻, malate), driven by a plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase: their water potential falls, water flows in, turgor rises, and the radially reinforced walls bow apart. Losing the solutes reverses the process.

Guard cells integrate many signals. Light opens stomata, both through a specific blue-light response (phototropins) and through signals linked to photosynthesis; high CO₂ inside the leaf closes them partly; abscisic acid (ABA), a hormone produced under water deficit, closes them; and high VPD makes them close, partly because it lowers leaf water status (Buckley, 2019; Grossiord et al., 2020). Stomata respond in minutes, not seconds, so under fluctuating light they lag behind photosynthesis, which costs both carbon and water — an active research area (Lawson & Vialet-Chabrand, 2019).

Engineers quantify the openness of stomata as a conductance, the flux per unit driving force. Two unit systems are in use: conductance in m s⁻¹ (a velocity; the flux is density × conductance × concentration difference) and molar conductance in mol m⁻² s⁻¹ (flux in mol m⁻² s⁻¹ per unit difference in mole fraction). They are related by \(g_{\text{mol}} = g_{\text{m/s}}\,P/(RT)\): at 20 °C and sea level, 1 mm s⁻¹ ≈ 0.041 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The molar form is preferred because it hardly depends on temperature and pressure. Well-watered crop leaves in good light have stomatal conductances of the order of a few tenths of a mol m⁻² s⁻¹; closed stomata and the cuticle together allow only a few per cent of that (Jones, 2014).

Transpiration and the vapour pressure deficit

Once the vapour leaves the wet cell walls, its movement is pure diffusion, driven by the difference in vapour pressure (or mole fraction) between the leaf interior and the air. The capacity of air to hold water vapour rises steeply with temperature. The saturation vapour pressure is given to better than 0.1 % between 0 and 40 °C by the Tetens (1930) formula in the form adopted by FAO-56 (Allen et al., 1998; see also Murray, 1967):

Saturation vapour pressure and leaf-to-air VPD(2.2.5)
\[ e_s(T) = 0.6108\,\exp\!\left(\frac{17.27\,T}{T+237.3}\right), \qquad \text{VPD}_{\text{air}} = e_s(T_a)\left(1-\frac{\text{RH}}{100}\right), \qquad \text{VPD}_{\text{leaf}} = e_s(T_{\ell}) - \frac{\text{RH}}{100}\,e_s(T_a) \]

the saturation vapour pressure in kilopascals is 0.6108 times the exponential of 17.27 T over T plus 237.3, with T in degrees Celsius. The air's vapour pressure deficit is the saturation vapour pressure at air temperature times one minus the relative humidity. What drives transpiration is the leaf-to-air deficit: saturation vapour pressure at leaf temperature minus the actual vapour pressure of the air.

Where does the formula come from?
  1. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation, \(\mathrm{d}\ln e_s/\mathrm{d}T = \lambda M_w/(RT^2)\), states that the logarithm of the saturation vapour pressure increases with temperature at a rate set by the latent heat of vaporisation.
  2. Integrating it exactly requires the temperature dependence of λ. Tetens (1930) proposed the empirical form \(e_s = a\exp[bT/(T+c)]\), which mimics the integrated equation with three fitted constants; FAO-56 uses a = 0.6108 kPa, b = 17.27, c = 237.3 °C.
  3. The slope needed later for Penman–Monteith follows by differentiation: \(\Delta = \mathrm{d}e_s/\mathrm{d}T = 4098\,e_s(T)/(T+237.3)^2\) kPa °C⁻¹ (0.145 kPa °C⁻¹ at 20 °C).
Why leaf temperature matters

A leaf 2 °C warmer than the air has an internal saturation vapour pressure about 12 % higher; a leaf 2 °C cooler, 12 % lower. Under LEDs, which emit much of their heat by convection rather than as thermal radiation towards the crop, leaves tend to be slightly cooler than under high-pressure sodium lamps — though Nelson and Bugbee (2015) showed that evaporative cooling and water status matter more than the lamp type. Measure leaf temperature (e.g., with an infrared thermometer) when you calculate VPD for a crop.

The exponent is dimensionless (°C/°C); \(e_s\) inherits kPa from the constant 0.6108 kPa ✓

With the driving force defined, transpiration is simply conductance times driving force. Because the stomata and the boundary layer are in series, their conductances combine like resistances in series:

Leaf transpiration(2.2.6)
\[ E = g_{tw}\,\frac{e_s(T_\ell) - e_a}{P} = g_{tw}\,\frac{\text{VPD}_{\text{leaf}}}{P}, \qquad \frac{1}{g_{tw}} = \frac{1}{g_{sw}} + \frac{1}{g_{bw}} \]

transpiration equals the total conductance to water vapour times the leaf-to-air vapour pressure difference divided by atmospheric pressure (which converts vapour pressure into mole fraction). The total conductance is the series combination of the stomatal and boundary-layer conductances: their reciprocals (resistances) add.

Leaf temperature must be known — it is itself set by the energy balance (Section 2.2.6). For leaves with stomata on both surfaces (amphistomatous, like lettuce), the two sides act in parallel: add the conductances of the two sides. The small "ternary" correction for the mass flow of the escaping vapour is neglected.

The boundary layer is the thin film of slowly moving air that clings to every surface. Its conductance increases with wind speed and decreases with leaf size, because the layer thickens as air travels across the leaf. For a flat leaf in forced convection, Campbell and Norman (1998) give:

Boundary-layer conductance of a leaf(2.2.7)
\[ g_{bw} = 0.147\,\sqrt{\frac{u}{d}}, \qquad g_{bH} = 0.135\,\sqrt{\frac{u}{d}}, \qquad d = 0.72\,w \]

the boundary-layer conductance to water vapour for one leaf surface, in mol per square metre per second, is 0.147 times the square root of wind speed divided by the characteristic dimension of the leaf; the conductance to heat has the coefficient 0.135. The characteristic dimension is about 0.72 times the leaf width.

Follow the reasoning
  1. Laminar boundary-layer theory gives a layer thickness that grows as \(\delta\propto\sqrt{\nu x/u}\) with distance \(x\) along a plate (ν = kinematic viscosity).
  2. Conductance is diffusivity divided by thickness, so \(g\propto D/\delta\propto\sqrt{u/x}\); averaging over the leaf gives \(\sqrt{u/d}\).
  3. The coefficients contain the diffusivities of heat and vapour and the molar density of air; they include an empirical factor (≈ 1.4) because real leaves in turbulent surroundings have thinner boundary layers than smooth plates in a wind tunnel.
  4. Example: u = 0.3 m s⁻¹ (a typical vertical-farm air speed) and w = 8 cm: d = 0.0576 m, \(g_{bw} = 0.147\sqrt{5.21} = 0.34\) mol m⁻² s⁻¹ per side, 0.67 for both sides together.

In a dense canopy, inner leaves see much lower air speeds than the air-handling design value — the root of many tipburn problems (Section 2.2.8).

The leaf energy balance

Leaf temperature is not a given: it is the outcome of an energy balance. A leaf absorbs short-wave radiation (PAR and near-infrared from the sun or lamps) and long-wave thermal radiation from its surroundings; it loses energy by emitting thermal radiation, by convection to the air (sensible heat) and by evaporating water (latent heat). Only a few per cent of absorbed energy is stored in photosynthesis or in warming the leaf, so for practical purposes:

Leaf energy balance(2.2.8)
\[ R_{\text{ni}} = c_p\,(g_{Ha}+g_r)\,(T_\ell - T_a) + \lambda\,g_{v}\,\frac{e_s(T_\ell)-e_a}{P}, \qquad g_r = \frac{4\,\varepsilon\,\sigma\,T_a^3}{c_p} \]

the isothermal net radiation absorbed by the leaf is shared between sensible heat — molar heat capacity of air times the heat and radiative conductances times the leaf-to-air temperature difference — and latent heat — molar latent heat times the vapour conductance times the vapour-pressure difference divided by pressure. The radiative conductance linearises the extra thermal radiation emitted when the leaf is warmer than the air.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(R_{\text{ni}}\)isothermal net radiation (net radiation if the leaf were at air temperature)W m⁻²50–400
\(c_p\)molar heat capacity of airJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹29.3
\(g_{Ha}\)boundary-layer conductance for heat (both sides, Eq. 2.2.7)mol m⁻² s⁻¹
\(g_r\)radiative conductancemol m⁻² s⁻¹≈ 0.19 at 20 °C
\(\varepsilon, \sigma\)leaf emissivity; Stefan–Boltzmann constant–; W m⁻² K⁻⁴0.97; 5.67 × 10⁻⁸

Follow the derivation
  1. Energy conservation (steady state, storage and photosynthesis neglected): absorbed radiation = emitted thermal radiation + H + λE.
  2. Write the emitted term as the value at air temperature plus a correction: \(\varepsilon\sigma T_\ell^4 \approx \varepsilon\sigma T_a^4 + 4\varepsilon\sigma T_a^3(T_\ell-T_a)\) (first-order Taylor expansion).
  3. Absorbed radiation minus \(\varepsilon\sigma T_a^4\) is the isothermal net radiation \(R_{\text{ni}}\); the correction behaves like an extra conductance \(g_r = 4\varepsilon\sigma T_a^3/c_p\) acting in parallel with convection.
  4. Sensible heat: \(H = c_pg_{Ha}(T_\ell-T_a)\); latent heat: \(\lambda E\) with \(E\) from Eq. 2.2.6. Substituting gives the equation above, which has one unknown, \(T_\ell\). Because \(e_s\) is non-linear, it is solved numerically (the calculator uses bisection) — or linearised, which leads straight to Penman–Monteith.
Assumptions and validity

A single flat leaf with a uniform temperature; the calculator assumes an 8 cm wide leaf with stomata on both sides. \(R_{\text{ni}}\) of a lettuce leaf under 300–400 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ of LED light is of the order of 60–100 W m⁻² (absorbed PAR plus a little near-infrared, with surroundings near air temperature).

J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ · mol m⁻² s⁻¹ · K = W m⁻² ✓; J mol⁻¹ · mol m⁻² s⁻¹ · kPa/kPa = W m⁻² ✓

Play with the calculator: with 100 W m⁻², 22 °C and 70 % RH, a transpiring leaf is within about 1 °C of the air; close the stomata and it warms by several degrees. Raise the net radiation to 400 W m⁻² (full sun on a greenhouse crop) with low air speed and closed stomata, and the leaf can be 10 °C or more above air temperature — the same order as the worst-case values that Nelson and Bugbee (2015) modelled for water-stressed leaves. Leaf temperature, VPD and transpiration are therefore coupled; the next section solves them together.

The Penman–Monteith equation, step by step

In 1948 Howard Penman derived an equation for evaporation from open water that avoided the need to know the surface temperature, by linearising the saturation vapour pressure curve. In 1965 John Monteith extended it to vegetation by adding the stomatal (surface) resistance. The Penman–Monteith equation is now the international standard for crop evapotranspiration (Allen et al., 1998) and the core of greenhouse transpiration models (Stanghellini et al., 2019). It applies to a single leaf or — as a "big leaf" — to a whole canopy.

Penman–Monteith equation(2.2.9)
\[ \lambda E = \frac{\Delta\,(R_n-G) + \rho_a\,c_p\,D\,g_a}{\Delta + \gamma\left(1+\dfrac{g_a}{g_s}\right)} \]

the latent heat flux equals the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve times the available energy, plus the air density times its specific heat times the vapour pressure deficit times the aerodynamic conductance, all divided by the slope plus the psychrometric constant times one plus the ratio of aerodynamic to surface conductance.

\(\lambda E\)latent heat flux (transpiration × latent heat)W m⁻²
\(\Delta\)slope of \(e_s(T)\) at air temperature, \(4098\,e_s/(T+237.3)^2\)kPa K⁻¹0.145 at 20 °C
\(R_n, G\)net radiation absorbed by the canopy; heat flux into the substrate/soilW m⁻²

Follow the derivation (8 steps)
  1. Energy balance of the surface (canopy or leaf, storage neglected): \(R_n - G = H + \lambda E\).
  2. Sensible heat flows from the surface at \(T_s\) to the air at \(T_a\) through the aerodynamic conductance: \(H = \rho_ac_p\,g_a\,(T_s - T_a)\).
  3. Latent heat flows from the saturated interior (vapour pressure \(e_s(T_s)\)) through the stomata and the boundary layer in series: \(\lambda E = \dfrac{\rho_ac_p}{\gamma}\,\dfrac{e_s(T_s)-e_a}{1/g_s + 1/g_a}\). (The factor \(\rho_ac_p/\gamma\) converts a vapour-pressure difference in kPa into an energy flux; it follows from the definition of γ.)
  4. The problem: \(T_s\) is unknown and \(e_s(T_s)\) is non-linear. Penman's trick: linearise around air temperature, \(e_s(T_s)\approx e_s(T_a) + \Delta\,(T_s - T_a)\).
  5. Then \(e_s(T_s)-e_a = D + \Delta\,(T_s-T_a)\), where \(D = e_s(T_a)-e_a\) is the VPD of the air, which we can measure.
  6. Eliminate \(T_s\) with steps 1 and 2: \(T_s-T_a = H/(\rho_ac_pg_a) = (R_n-G-\lambda E)/(\rho_ac_pg_a)\).
  7. Substitute into step 3: \(\lambda E\,\gamma\,(1/g_s+1/g_a) = \rho_ac_pD + \Delta\,(R_n-G-\lambda E)/g_a\).
  8. Collect the λE terms and multiply by \(g_a\): \(\lambda E\,[\gamma(1+g_a/g_s) + \Delta] = \Delta(R_n-G) + \rho_ac_pDg_a\). Dividing gives Eq. 2.2.9. ✓
Two limits worth remembering
  • Wet surface (\(g_s\to\infty\)): \(\lambda E = [\Delta(R_n-G) + \rho_ac_pDg_a]/(\Delta+\gamma)\) — Penman's (1948) open-water equation.
  • Closed canopy in still, humid air (\(D\to0\) or \(g_a\to0\)): \(\lambda E \to \Delta(R_n-G)/(\Delta+\gamma)\) with \(g_s\) large — the "equilibrium evaporation", driven by radiation only. The first term of the numerator is the radiative term, the second the aerodynamic (VPD-driven) term; Figure 10 separates them.
  • The FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration \(ET_0\) used in Lesson 11.3 is Eq. 2.2.9 for a standard grass surface (\(r_s = 70\) s m⁻¹, \(r_a = 208/u_2\)) with the constants evaluated; it is implemented as et0PenmanMonteith in the course physics library.
Assumptions and validity

Steady state; the same aerodynamic conductance for heat and vapour; linearisation error small when \(|T_s - T_a|\) is a few kelvin; canopy treated as one big leaf (Stanghellini's greenhouse version adds leaf area index and separate radiation terms). In closed plant factories the air's VPD is itself changed by the transpired water unless dehumidification keeps up — the feedback that Graamans et al. (2017) built into their model.

Numerator: (kPa K⁻¹)(W m⁻²) + (kg m⁻³)(J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)(kPa)(m s⁻¹) = kPa K⁻¹ · W m⁻² ✓ (J m⁻³ K⁻¹ · m s⁻¹ = W m⁻² K⁻¹); denominator: kPa K⁻¹ → λE in W m⁻² ✓
Worked example 4How much water does a vertical-farm tier transpire — and what does it cost to remove?

A lettuce tier absorbs a net radiation of 120 W m⁻² (floor area) from its LEDs during a 16 h photoperiod. The air is at 22 °C and 70 % RH, the aerodynamic conductance is 20 mm s⁻¹ and the canopy surface conductance 15 mm s⁻¹; G = 0. Use ρₐ = 1.196 kg m⁻³, cₚ = 1005 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, P = 101.3 kPa. Calculate λE, the transpiration in L m⁻² d⁻¹, the latent heat to be removed per day, and the electricity for dehumidification if the dehumidifier removes 3 L of water per kWh.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Air properties: \(e_s(22) = 2.644\) kPa, \(D = 0.30\times2.644 = 0.793\) kPa, \(\Delta = 4098\times2.644/259.3^2 = 0.161\) kPa K⁻¹, \(\gamma = 0.000665\times101.3 = 0.0674\) kPa K⁻¹.
  2. Radiative term: \(\Delta R_n = 0.161\times120 = 19.33\).
  3. Aerodynamic term: \(\rho_ac_pDg_a = 1.196\times1005\times0.793\times0.020 = 19.06\).
  4. Denominator: \(0.161 + 0.0674\times(1+20/15) = 0.161 + 0.157 = 0.318\).
  5. λE = (19.33 + 19.06)/0.318 = 121 W m⁻² — radiative and aerodynamic terms contribute almost equally, and λE ≈ Rₙ: nearly all absorbed light energy leaves the canopy as latent heat (H ≈ 0).
  6. Water: λ = 2.449 MJ kg⁻¹ at 22 °C → E = 121/2.449 × 10⁶ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁵ kg m⁻² s⁻¹ = 0.178 mm h⁻¹ → × 16 h = 2.8 L m⁻² d⁻¹.
  7. Latent heat: 2.8 kg × 2.449 MJ kg⁻¹ = 6.9 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹ = 1.9 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ of heat that the HVAC must handle (compare with the lighting electricity of the same order; Lesson 8.1).
  8. Dehumidification electricity at 3 L kWh⁻¹: 2.8/3 = 0.95 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ per tier — and the recovered water (condensate) can be returned to the nutrient tank, which is why plant factories can have very high water-use efficiency.

Managing VPD in greenhouses and vertical farms

Why do growers talk about VPD rather than relative humidity? Because the same 70 % RH means very different drying power at different temperatures: 0.70 kPa at 20 °C but 1.27 kPa at 30 °C. VPD is the variable that the plant "feels". Reviewing recommendations for greenhouse tomato, Shamshiri et al. (2018) found optimal VPD values recommended between about 0.3 and 1.0 kPa in different sources, with commercial guidelines such as 0.5–1.2 kPa; they summarised that VPD below ≈ 0.3 kPa is associated with mineral deficiencies, guttation, fungal diseases and soft growth, and VPD above ≈ 1.5 kPa with wilting, leaf roll and stunted plants. They also point out that VPD should not be used directly as the feedback variable of a climate controller, because the same VPD can result from many combinations of temperature and humidity — control temperature and humidity, and use VPD to choose their set-points.

Calcium, transpiration and tipburn in lettuce

Calcium is a structural nutrient of cell walls and membranes (Lesson 2.3). Unlike potassium or nitrogen, it moves almost only in the xylem with the transpiration stream and is hardly redistributed in the phloem (White & Broadley, 2003). Organs that transpire little — the young, enclosed inner leaves of a lettuce head, fruits, growing tips — therefore receive little calcium, however much there is in the nutrient solution. When such tissue grows very fast, the local supply cannot keep up and the margins of young leaves collapse and turn brown: tipburn (Collier & Tibbitts, 1982; Barta & Tibbitts, 2000). Saure (1998) reviewed the evidence and stressed that tipburn is a disorder of rapidly growing tissue under conditions that restrict calcium supply, with other stress factors contributing.

Controlled environments make tipburn both more likely and more controllable. More likely, because the conditions that maximise growth — high PPFD, CO₂ enrichment (which also partly closes stomata), warm temperatures — accelerate leaf expansion faster than calcium can follow, and because dense canopies and low air speeds create humid, still air inside the heads. More controllable, because air flow can be engineered: Frantz et al. (2004) eliminated tipburn by blowing air directly onto the meristem, which doubled edible yield at the highest light level; Lee et al. (2013) showed in a closed plant factory that air temperature and air-flow rate both affect tipburn occurrence in leaf lettuce; and vertical farms increasingly use perforated ducts or fans that blow down into the canopy. Root pressure at night, when transpiration stops, is thought to deliver part of the calcium that reaches low-transpiring organs, which is one reason why night-time humidity management matters too (Collier & Tibbitts, 1982).

Research frontier (2026)

How much air movement is enough — and can it be too much? Dupont et al. (2026) grew lettuce and tomato under two low air speeds (0.22 and 0.65 m s⁻¹). The higher air speed raised transpirational demand; stomata partly closed and intrinsic water-use efficiency rose, but lettuce fresh weight and leaf length fell by about 13 %. Small differences in air flow, often ignored in experiments, change the leaf boundary layer, stomatal behaviour and growth — an important reminder for your own growth experiments (Lesson 3.1) and for vertical-farm air-handling design.

Water-use efficiency

Because CO₂ and water vapour share the stomatal pathway, the ratio of carbon gained to water lost is set by physics and by how far the stomata let the internal CO₂ drop:

Leaf water-use efficiency(2.2.10)
\[ \text{WUE} = \frac{A}{E} = \frac{g_c\,(C_a - C_i)}{g_w\,\text{VPD}/P} = \frac{(C_a-C_i)\,P}{1.6\,\text{VPD}} = \frac{C_a\,(1-C_i/C_a)\,P}{1.6\,\text{VPD}} \]

water-use efficiency, the CO₂ assimilated per water transpired, equals the CO₂ drawdown from ambient to intercellular air times atmospheric pressure, divided by 1.6 times the vapour pressure deficit. It rises with CO₂ enrichment and falls with VPD.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(A, E\)net CO₂ assimilation, transpirationmol m⁻² s⁻¹
\(C_a, C_i\)ambient and intercellular CO₂ mole fractionmol mol⁻¹\(C_i/C_a\) ≈ 0.7 for C3
\(g_c, g_w\)conductances to CO₂ and water vapourmol m⁻² s⁻¹\(g_w = 1.6\,g_c\)
1.6ratio of the diffusivities of water vapour and CO₂ in air–
Follow the derivation
  1. CO₂ enters by diffusion: \(A = g_c(C_a - C_i)\) (boundary layer neglected).
  2. Water leaves by diffusion: \(E = g_w\,\text{VPD}/P\) (Eq. 2.2.6).
  3. Water vapour is lighter and diffuses faster: \(g_w = 1.6\,g_c\) through stomatal pores.
  4. Dividing, \(g_c\) cancels: \(A/E = (C_a-C_i)P/(1.6\,\text{VPD})\). Example: \(C_a\) = 400 µmol mol⁻¹, \(C_i\) = 280, VPD = 1.0 kPa, P = 101.3 kPa → WUE = 120 × 10⁻⁶ × 101.3/1.6 = 7.6 × 10⁻³ mol CO₂ per mol H₂O = 7.6 mmol mol⁻¹, i.e., about 130 water molecules per CO₂.
Assumptions and validity

Instantaneous leaf-level WUE; night-time water loss, respiration and the difference between leaf and air temperature are ignored. Crop-level WUE (g biomass per litre) also depends on how much water is lost by evaporation from substrates and by drainage — in closed plant factories where condensate is recycled, system-level water-use efficiency can be far higher than in the field (Lesson 8.4).

(mol mol⁻¹)(kPa)/(kPa) = mol CO₂ per mol H₂O ✓

Equation 2.2.10 contains the whole CEA strategy in one line. CO₂ enrichment raises \(C_a\) and — as stomata partly close — lowers \(C_i/C_a\) slightly, so WUE rises strongly; keeping VPD moderate avoids wasting water per unit of carbon. The catch: very low VPD also lowers transpiration and calcium delivery. The art of climate control is to keep VPD in the window where both carbon gain and calcium transport are adequate, with enough air movement to thin the boundary layers inside the canopy.

Key terms

Water potential (Ψ)
Chemical potential of water relative to pure water, per unit molar volume (MPa); water moves from high to low Ψ.
Osmotic potential (Ψₛ)
Component of Ψ due to dissolved solutes; Ψₛ = −cRT for dilute solutions.
Turgor pressure (Ψₚ)
Positive hydrostatic pressure inside a cell pressed against its wall; negative pressure (tension) in the xylem.
Cohesion–tension theory
Explanation of the ascent of sap: evaporation in leaves pulls a cohesive water column up through the xylem under tension.
Soil–plant–atmosphere continuum (SPAC)
The continuous water pathway from root zone through the plant to the air, analysed as resistances in series.
Casparian strip
Hydrophobic band in endodermal cell walls that blocks apoplastic flow and forces water and ions through membranes.
Stomatal conductance
Measure of how easily gases diffuse through the stomata, usually in mol m⁻² s⁻¹.
Boundary-layer conductance
Conductance of the layer of still air on a leaf surface; increases with air speed and decreases with leaf size.
Vapour pressure deficit (VPD)
Difference between saturation and actual vapour pressure of air (kPa); leaf-to-air VPD uses the saturation pressure at leaf temperature.
Transpiration
Loss of water vapour from plants, mainly through stomata; E = g·VPD/P.
Latent heat flux
Energy carried away by evaporating water (λE), ≈ 2.45 MJ per kg of water.
Penman–Monteith equation
Combination equation giving evapotranspiration from available energy, VPD, aerodynamic and surface conductances.
Guttation
Exudation of xylem sap droplets from hydathodes, driven by root pressure when transpiration is low.
Tipburn
Necrosis of young leaf margins caused by local calcium shortage in fast-growing, low-transpiring tissue.
Water-use efficiency
Carbon gained per water lost; at leaf level A/E = (Cₐ − Cᵢ)P/(1.6 VPD).
