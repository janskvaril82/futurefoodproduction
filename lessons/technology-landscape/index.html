Mapping the landscape of emerging food technologies

Lettuce grown under LEDs in windowless buildings, protein brewed from carbon dioxide and electricity, cereals that might one day fix their own nitrogen, meat grown from cells, robots that weed with cameras and lasers. Every year brings new promises that technology will fix the food system — and every year some of those promises quietly fail. This lesson gives you a map and a compass: a structured taxonomy of emerging food technologies, the readiness scale that engineers and funders use, the mathematics of how technologies spread and become cheaper, a critical look at hype, and a matrix that confronts each technology with the planetary boundaries of Lesson 1.2. The aim is not to pick winners, but to learn to ask the right questions of every technology you will meet in the rest of this course.

What you will be able to do

  • Classify emerging food technologies by what they do (efficiency, substitution, redesign) and where they act in the food system, and find where each one is treated in depth in this course (LO1).
  • Assign technology readiness levels with the EU definitions, and explain what a TRL does and does not tell you (LO1, LO6).
  • Model the diffusion and cost decline of a technology with logistic, Bass and experience-curve equations, and estimate how soon it could matter at scale (LO1, LO7).
  • Describe the hype cycle and its scientific critique, and separate expectations from evidence (LO1, LO6).
  • Evaluate a technology against the planetary boundaries, including rebound, burden shifting and lock-in, and apply the principles of responsible research and innovation (LO1, LO7).

Why we need a map

The first three lessons of this module quantified the challenge. About 8.2 billion people today and roughly 9.7 billion by 2050 must be fed (UN DESA, 2024); food production dominates several of the planetary boundaries that are already transgressed (Richardson et al., 2023; te Wierik et al., 2025); and the footprints of different foods differ by one to two orders of magnitude (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). Into this picture steps a crowded field of technologies, each promising to close part of the gap. They compete for students' careers, investors' money, researchers' grants and politicians' attention. Some are incremental improvements of what farmers already do; others would replace entire supply chains. Some exist only in laboratories; others are sold in every supermarket.

A map protects you against two opposite errors. The first is techno-optimism: the belief that the next innovation will solve the problem, so that difficult choices about diets, waste, land and justice can be postponed. The second is techno-pessimism: the belief that because many promises failed, none deserve attention. Both are lazy. The history of agriculture — from synthetic nitrogen fertiliser to hybrid maize and LED lighting — shows that technology has transformed food production, sometimes for the better and sometimes with severe side effects. The engineering question is always specific: which technology, replacing what, where, powered by which energy, at what scale, and for whom?

This lesson answers four questions that structure every technology assessment in the course:

  1. What does it do, and what does it replace? — a taxonomy (Section 1.4.2).
  2. How mature is it? — technology readiness levels (Section 1.4.3).
  3. How fast could it spread and become cheaper, and how much of the excitement is justified? — S-curves, experience curves and hype cycles (Sections 1.4.4–1.4.5).
  4. What would happen to the planetary boundaries if it did spread — including indirect effects? (Sections 1.4.6–1.4.7).

A fifth question runs through all of them: who decides, and who benefits? It is the subject of the final section on responsible research and innovation.

Key idea

No technology is sustainable in itself. It is more or less sustainable than something else — the incumbent it replaces — for a stated function (a functional unit, Lesson 1.3), in a given place, powered by a given energy system, at a given scale. A vertical farm is not "green" or "unsustainable"; it may be better than trucked field lettuce for water and pesticides and worse for climate, depending on the electricity. Every judgement in this lesson is therefore a comparison.

A taxonomy of emerging food technologies

Classifications help us think, but no single one captures everything. We use three complementary axes. The first is where in the food system a technology acts (Figure 1). The second is the type of change, using the efficiency–substitution–redesign (ESR) framework that Hill and MacRae (1995) proposed for the transition to sustainable agriculture: efficiency fine-tunes existing practices to reduce consumption and waste; substitution replaces a resource-intensive or harmful input or product with a more benign one; redesign restructures the system so that the problem does not arise in the first place. The third axis is which physical constraint the technology relaxes: land (more food per hectare, or food without farmland), water, reactive nitrogen, fossil energy, labour or seasonality. Controlled and precision production

Controlled environment agriculture (CEA) decouples crops from weather and season. Its most radical form, the plant factory with artificial lighting or vertical farm, stacks lettuce, herbs or microgreens in tiers under LEDs in a thermally insulated building, with hydroponic nutrient solutions and precise control of temperature, humidity and CO₂ (Modules 4, 5 and 7). It achieves yields per square metre of building that are one to two orders of magnitude above field yields, recirculates water and nutrients, and needs no pesticides. The price is electricity: Graamans et al. (2018) computed that producing 1 kg of lettuce dry weight required about 247 kWh of electricity in a plant factory, compared with 70 kWh in a Dutch greenhouse, 111 kWh in a greenhouse in the United Arab Emirates and 182–211 kWh in Swedish greenhouses. Whether that is acceptable depends on the carbon intensity and the price of electricity — a theme of Lessons 8.1 and 15.1. High-tech greenhouses are the less radical but far larger part of CEA: they combine free sunlight with LED supplementary lighting, heat pumps, thermal screens and biological pest control. Aquaponics couples fish farming with hydroponics so that nitrogen and phosphorus from fish feed become plant fertiliser (Module 6).

Precision agriculture applies the right input at the right place, time and rate: satellite positioning, yield mapping, remote sensing and variable-rate machines (Module 11). Field robotics adds autonomy — weeding robots that distinguish crop from weed with cameras and remove weeds mechanically, by laser or with micro-doses of herbicide. Digital agriculture and AI are not a single technology but a data layer: sensors and IoT networks (Module 9), machine-learning models, digital twins and model-predictive control (Module 10). Their environmental effect is indirect — they help other technologies use inputs better — which makes them hard to assess and easy to over-sell.

Frontier · as of 2026 — the vertical-farming shake-out

After a decade of rapid investment, several of the best-funded indoor farming companies in the United States failed: AppHarvest and AeroFarms filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in 2023, Bowery Farming closed in November 2024, and Plenty — which had raised close to a billion US dollars — filed for Chapter 11 in March 2025 (Vertical Farm Daily, 2025). The technology works (it sits at TRL 9); what failed was the business model — high capital and electricity costs, low prices for leafy greens and optimistic growth plans. Meanwhile LED-lit greenhouses kept expanding. The case is a textbook illustration of why technical readiness is not the same as economic or environmental viability (Sections 1.4.3 and 1.4.5), and of why the vertical farm explorer and the levelised-cost laboratory matter.

Genetic and biological innovation

Gene editing with CRISPR–Cas and related tools makes targeted changes to a genome — often a small deletion or substitution that could also arise by natural mutation, but found in months instead of decades of crossing and selection. The first CRISPR-edited foods on the market were a tomato with elevated γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), sold in Japan from 2021, and less pungent mustard greens launched in the United States in 2023. In the EU, plants from new genomic techniques (NGTs) fall under a new regulation discussed in Lesson 14.2. Biologicals comprise biostimulants (substances or micro-organisms that improve nutrient-use efficiency, stress tolerance or quality, defined in EU law by the Fertilising Products Regulation (EU) 2019/1009), biofertilisers and biological control agents such as predatory mites and entomopathogenic fungi. Their field efficacy varies strongly with soil, climate and crop, which is why independent trials matter.

Two longer-term ideas target the most transgressed boundary, nitrogen (Lesson 1.2). Nitrogen-fixing cereals would obtain part of their nitrogen from the air, as legumes do with rhizobia. Nature offers a hint: a maize landrace from the Sierra Mixe region of Mexico grows aerial roots that secrete a sugary mucilage hosting nitrogen-fixing bacteria, and over five years of field experiments atmospheric fixation supplied 29–82 % of the plants' nitrogen (Van Deynze et al., 2018). Transferring such traits, or engineering the nitrogenase enzyme or a legume-like symbiosis into cereals, remains at low TRL. Biological nitrification inhibition (BNI) is nearer: Subbarao et al. (2021) transferred a chromosome region from the wild grass Leymus racemosus into elite wheat, giving roots that release natural nitrification inhibitors and so keep nitrogen as ammonium, which is less prone to leaching and N₂O loss. Perennial grains — such as the intermediate wheatgrass Kernza and perennial rice — regrow for several years without ploughing. Perennial rice in southern China gave up to eight consecutive harvests from one planting, with yields comparable to replanted annual rice, and soils accumulated about 0.95 t of organic carbon per hectare per year over four years (Zhang et al., 2023).

Frontier · as of 2026 — gene editing and the law

The EU's regulation on plants obtained by certain new genomic techniques, Regulation (EU) 2026/1388, was published in the Official Journal on 26 June 2026 and entered into force on 16 July 2026; it will apply from 17 July 2028. Category 1 NGT plants — those with limited modifications that could also occur naturally, excluding herbicide tolerance — will be treated much like conventional varieties, with seed labelling but no consumer labelling; category 2 plants remain under adapted GMO rules. In England, the Genetic Technology (Precision Breeding) Regulations 2025 came into force on 13 November 2025. The regulatory "readiness" of gene editing in Europe is thus moving from TRL-independent prohibition to case-by-case verification — a reminder that regulation is part of the innovation system (Lessons 14.2–14.4).

Alternative proteins

Animal products are where the largest environmental pressures of the food system are concentrated. In the meta-analysis of Poore and Nemecek (2018), meat, aquaculture, eggs and dairy used about 83 % of the world's farmland and contributed 56–58 % of food's greenhouse-gas emissions, while providing 37 % of the protein and 18 % of the calories in the global food supply. Alternative proteins try to deliver similar nutrition and eating experience with far fewer resources (Module 12). Plant-based analogues use extrusion and other texturisation processes to turn soy, pea or wheat proteins into meat-like fibres (Lesson 12.2). Fermentation comes in two flavours: biomass fermentation grows a whole micro-organism as food — the fungal mycoprotein sold since 1985 is the classic example — while precision fermentation uses engineered microbes to secrete one specific protein, such as a milk protein or a heme protein, that is then purified. Gas fermentation grows hydrogen-oxidising bacteria on hydrogen from water electrolysis and on CO₂, so that protein is made from air, water, minerals and electricity; Leger et al. (2021) calculated that, per unit of land including the photovoltaic panels that power it, such single-cell protein could deliver more than ten times the protein yield and at least twice the calorie yield of any staple crop. Cultivated meat grows animal cells in bioreactors (Lesson 12.4). Insects and microalgae and seaweed complete the family (Lesson 12.5).

Models suggest that the environmental leverage of alternative proteins can be large. Humpenöder et al. (2022) projected that replacing 20 % of per-capita ruminant meat consumption with microbial protein by 2050 would offset the future expansion of pasture and cut annual deforestation and the associated CO₂ emissions roughly in half (by 56 % between 2020 and 2050), while also lowering methane emissions. Whether such substitution happens depends on price, taste, trust and regulation — the subjects of Modules 13 and 14.

Frontier · as of 2026 — alternative proteins after the first wave

Cultivated meat has been authorised for sale in Singapore (2020), the United States (chicken in 2023; cultivated salmon from Wildtype in May 2025), Israel (2024) and Australia–New Zealand (Vow's cultured quail, approved by FSANZ in April 2025 and permitted in the Food Standards Code from June 2025); in the UK, cultivated chicken for pet food went on sale in February 2025. At the same time Italy (2023) and eight US states — Alabama, Florida, Indiana, Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, South Dakota (a five-year moratorium from July 2026) and Texas, some temporarily — have banned its production or sale. In the EU, Gourmey (duck cells, July 2024) and Mosa Meat (beef fat, January 2025) have applied for novel-food authorisation; none has been authorised as of 2026 (Lesson 14.1). Microbial protein from CO₂ and hydrogen (Solar Foods' Solein, Finland) was approved in Singapore in 2022, reached self-affirmed GRAS status in the United States in 2024 and appeared in US consumer products in 2026, while the EU application submitted in 2021 still awaited EFSA's opinion in mid-2026. Plant-based meat has cooled: US retail dollar sales of plant-based meat and seafood fell by 7 % in 2024 and by a further 10 % in 2025, to about US$1 billion (GFI, 2026). And in insects, the French company Ÿnsect, once the sector's best-funded firm, was placed in judicial liquidation in December 2025 (AgFunderNews, 2025).

Redesigning land use and processing

Agrivoltaics places photovoltaic panels above or between crops so that one hectare yields both food and electricity. Dupraz et al. (2011) introduced the land equivalent ratio to this problem: if a hectare of agrivoltaics produces 80 % of the crop and 70 % of the electricity of separate single-use hectares, its land equivalent ratio is 1.5, meaning that separate systems would need 1.5 ha to do the same (Lesson 11.5). Partial shade can even benefit crops in hot, dry climates. Food 3D printing deposits food pastes layer by layer to create personalised shapes, textures and nutrient contents — for example texture-modified meals for people with swallowing difficulties — and has been used for plant-based meat analogues. Its environmental relevance at system scale is, for now, small and uncertain: a useful reminder that technical novelty and sustainability impact are different things.

How ready is it? Technology readiness levels

Engineers need a shared vocabulary for maturity. The technology readiness level (TRL) scale was developed at NASA to assess the maturity of technologies for space missions and was formalised in its nine-level form by Mankins (1995). The European Commission adopted it for its research framework programmes; the definitions in Table 2 are those of the Horizon 2020 general annexes, which Horizon Europe continues to use (European Commission, 2014). TRLs are now used by funders, investors and companies worldwide and appear in many assessments of food innovations, such as the review of emerging food-system technologies by Herrero et al. (2020).

A TRL is useful precisely because it is narrow: it describes the technical maturity of a component or system in a given environment. It says nothing, by itself, about cost, market demand, regulatory approval, social acceptance or environmental performance. A technology can be at TRL 9 and still fail commercially, as vertical farming companies have shown, or cause harm, as pesticides that were perfectly "ready" once did. Interviews with practitioners in several industries found that TRL assessments are often subjective, that they struggle with systems made of components at different levels, and that they ignore how hard it is to integrate a technology into a larger system (Olechowski et al., 2015). Complementary scales therefore exist — manufacturing readiness levels, market or commercial readiness, and "societal readiness" — and for food, regulatory readiness is often the binding constraint: a novel food cannot be sold in the EU before it is authorised, however mature its technology (Lesson 14.1).

Common misconception

"A high TRL means a technology is a good bet, and a low TRL means it is a bad one." TRL measures distance from the laboratory, not merit. Some of the most important food technologies of the next decades (for example low-emission nitrogen management) are simple and ready; some of the most ambitious (nitrogen-fixing cereals) are far from ready but could be transformative. A portfolio needs both — and every technology needs evidence of benefit, not only of maturity.

S-curves: how technologies spread and get cheaper

A technology can only relieve the planetary boundaries in proportion to how widely it is used. The spread of innovations — from hybrid maize to smartphones — typically follows an S-shaped curve: slow at first, when only a few pioneers adopt; rapid in the middle, as adoption becomes self-reinforcing through imitation, learning and falling prices; and slow again as the market saturates. You met the same mathematics for population growth in Lesson 1.1.

History — hybrid corn

The founding empirical study of technology diffusion is about food. Zvi Griliches (1957) analysed the share of US maize area planted with hybrid seed between the 1930s and 1950s, state by state and district by district. Every region followed a logistic curve, but the curves differed in three parameters — their origin (when adoption started), their slope (how fast it proceeded) and their ceiling. Griliches showed that these differences were largely explained by economics: seed companies entered the most profitable markets first, and farmers adopted faster where the gain in profit was larger. Hybrids also had to be bred separately for each region, so availability — not only farmers' attitudes — shaped the curve.

The sociologist Everett Rogers classified adopters by when they adopt: the first 2.5 % are innovators, the next 13.5 % early adopters, then 34 % early majority, 34 % late majority and 16 % laggards (Rogers, 2003). The engineer Frank Bass turned these ideas into a model with two kinds of adopters: innovators, who adopt independently of others (for example after advertising or advice), and imitators, who adopt because others have (Bass, 1969).

The Bass diffusion model(1.4.2)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}t} = \big(p + q\,F\big)\big(1 - F\big), \qquad F(t) = \frac{1 - e^{-(p+q)\,t}}{1 + \dfrac{q}{p}\,e^{-(p+q)\,t}}, \qquad t^{*} = \frac{\ln(q/p)}{p + q} \]

the rate at which the remaining potential adopters adopt equals the innovation coefficient plus the imitation coefficient times the fraction who have already adopted; the resulting cumulative adoption curve rises in an S-shape, and adoption per year peaks at time t-star, the logarithm of q over p divided by their sum.

The market potential \(m\) is fixed; repeat purchases, competing products and price are ignored (extended Bass models add them). In a meta-analysis of 213 applications across products and countries, Sultan, Farley and Lehmann (1990) found average coefficients of \(p = 0.03\) and \(q = 0.38\): imitation dominates. Agricultural innovations have often diffused more slowly, because farms differ, benefits vary from year to year and adoption requires investment. Performance S-curves and experience curves

Not only adoption but also performance tends to follow S-curves: each technology improves slowly while its principles are explored, rapidly while engineers exploit them, and slowly again as it approaches physical limits — at which point a new technology with a higher ceiling may take over. LED lighting illustrates the rapid phase: the photon efficacy of the best horticultural fixtures rose from about 1.7 µmol J⁻¹ in 2014 to 2.5–3.0 µmol J⁻¹ in 2020 (Lesson 4.3), approaching but not reaching the physical limits set by photon energies. Cost follows a related regularity. In 1936 the aeronautical engineer Theodore Wright observed that each doubling of cumulative aircraft production reduced the labour needed per plane by a constant percentage (Wright, 1936). This experience curve, or Wright's law, has since been tested on more than fifty technologies and is the basis of statistically validated cost forecasts for solar PV, wind power, batteries and electrolysers (Way et al., 2022).

Experience curve (Wright's law)(1.4.3)
\[ C(X) = C_0\left(\frac{X}{X_0}\right)^{-b}, \qquad \mathrm{LR} = 1 - 2^{-b} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b = -\log_2(1-\mathrm{LR}) \]

the unit cost after a cumulative production X equals the initial cost multiplied by the ratio of cumulative production to its initial value raised to the power minus b; each doubling of cumulative production reduces the cost by the learning rate LR.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(C(X)\)unit cost (or price) at cumulative production \(X\)€ kg⁻¹, € per fixture …–
\(C_0,\ X_0\)cost and cumulative production at a reference point€ kg⁻¹; kg, t, units–
\(b\)experience exponent–0.15–0.5
\(\mathrm{LR}\)learning rate: fractional cost reduction per doubling of cumulative production–0.1–0.3 for many manufactured goods
Follow the derivation
  1. Assume every doubling of cumulative output multiplies unit cost by the same factor \(1-\mathrm{LR}\): \(C(2X) = (1-\mathrm{LR})\,C(X)\).
  2. A power law has exactly this property: \(C(2X)/C(X) = 2^{-b}\) for \(C \propto X^{-b}\). Hence \(1 - \mathrm{LR} = 2^{-b}\) and \(b = -\log_2(1-\mathrm{LR})\).
  3. On log–log axes the curve is a straight line with slope \(-b\): \(\log C = \log C_0 - b\,\log(X/X_0)\).
  4. After \(n\) doublings (\(X/X_0 = 2^n\)), \(C = C_0(1-\mathrm{LR})^n\). A 20 % learning rate halves cost after \(\ln 0.5/\ln 0.8 = 3.1\) doublings.
  5. With a floor: if raw materials and energy set an irreducible cost \(C_f\), a better model is \(C = C_f + (C_0 - C_f)(X/X_0)^{-b}\) — see Worked example 4.
Assumptions and validity

Wright's law describes modular, manufactured technologies whose cost is dominated by learning in production: PV modules, LEDs, batteries. Processes limited by biology or thermodynamics learn more slowly and hit floors. Humbird (2021) argued that for cultivated meat, low cell growth rates, metabolic inefficiency, oxygen transfer and shear damage limit bioreactor size and cell density, so that costs cannot fall indefinitely the way semiconductor costs did. The same caution applies to anything whose cost is dominated by feedstock (sugar for fermentation), energy (electricity for vertical farms) or land.

  4. (c) With a floor: \(10 = 6 + 44\,(X/X_0)^{-0.2345}\) ⇒ \((X/X_0)^{-0.2345} = 4/44 = 0.0909\) ⇒ \(X/X_0 = e^{2.398/0.2345} = e^{10.23} \approx 2.8\times10^{4}\): about 28 Mt, i.e. \(\log_2(2.8\times10^4) = 14.8\) doublings — some 30 years at the same pace.

Hype cycles — and why to distrust them

Expectations about a technology rarely track its real progress. In the 1990s the consultancy Gartner introduced the hype cycle, which describes five phases in the life of a technology's public image: an innovation trigger; a peak of inflated expectations, when media coverage and investment surge; a trough of disillusionment, when early products disappoint; a slope of enlightenment, when realistic applications emerge; and a plateau of productivity (Fenn & Raskino, 2008). One way to rationalise the shape, used by Gartner's own analysts, is to add a short-lived pulse of enthusiasm to the slow S-curve of real maturity. Eq. 1.4.4 turns this idea into a toy model that you can explore in Figure 4.

A stylised hype-cycle model(1.4.4)
\[ V(t) = \underbrace{A\,\frac{t}{\tau}\,e^{\,1 - t/\tau}}_{\text{hype pulse}} \; + \; \underbrace{\frac{B}{1 + e^{-r\,(t - t_m)}}}_{\text{real maturity (S-curve)}} \]

the visibility of, or expectations about, a technology equals a pulse of enthusiasm that rises to a maximum A at time tau and then decays, plus an S-shaped curve of real maturity that rises towards the plateau B around the midpoint t-m.

SymbolMeaningUnitDefault
\(V(t)\)expectations or visibility (e.g., media attention, investment), relative to the productive plateau––
\(A,\ \tau\)height and timing of the hype pulse–, yr1.0, 2 yr
\(B\)plateau of real, productive use–1
\(r,\ t_m\)maturation rate and midpoint of the real S-curve (as in Eq. 1.4.1)yr⁻¹, yr0.45, 10 yr
Follow the derivation — and when does a trough appear?
  1. The pulse \(g(t) = A(t/\tau)e^{1-t/\tau}\) is zero at \(t = 0\), has derivative \(g' = (A/\tau)e^{1-t/\tau}(1 - t/\tau)\), and therefore a single maximum \(g(\tau) = A\). It is the simplest smooth "rise and fall" curve (a gamma-shaped pulse).
  2. The maturity term is the logistic curve of Eq. 1.4.1 with ceiling \(B\).
  3. A trough is a local minimum of \(V\) after the peak: it requires that, at some time after \(\tau\), the pulse falls faster than maturity rises, \(|g'(t)| > \mathrm{d}(\text{maturity})/\mathrm{d}t\). This happens when the hype comes early and is large (small \(\tau\), large \(A\)) and maturity comes late or slowly (large \(t_m\), small \(r\)).
  4. If hype and maturity are well synchronised, \(V\) rises monotonically — no trough. If maturity never comes (\(B = 0\)), the curve is a pulse followed by oblivion.
Assumptions and validity

This is a curve-shaping device, not a law of nature. The two components are not measured independently, and any hump-shaped time series can be "explained" by choosing parameters. Use it to reason about why expectations and reality diverge, not to forecast when a trough will end.

Both terms are dimensionless (the exponent \(1 - t/\tau\) and the ratio \(t/\tau\) are dimensionless) ✓

How good is the hype cycle as a description of reality? When Dedehayir and Steinert (2016) searched Gartner's own published hype cycles for evidence, they found that the model lacks a robust empirical foundation: few technologies actually travelled the whole path from trigger to plateau, many disappeared, and some reappeared in later years. Van Lente, Spitters and Peine (2013) compared hypes around voice-over-internet telephony, gene therapy and high-temperature superconductivity and found that the shapes of hype patterns differ markedly between fields, depending on how expectations at different levels — a whole field versus a single product — interact. The sociology of expectations adds a deeper point: expectations are performative. Promises mobilise money, researchers and political support; they can become self-fulfilling (funding creates the progress it anticipated) or self-defeating (inflated claims destroy trust when they fail) (Borup et al., 2006).

Food technologies offer vivid examples. Vertical farming attracted billions of dollars of venture capital around 2020 and then lost several flagship companies (Section 1.4.2). Plant-based meat enjoyed a celebrated stock-market debut in 2019, followed by several years of falling US retail sales. Insect protein attracted large industrial investments, followed by insolvencies. Yet in each case the underlying technology continued to improve and to find niches. Whether these are "troughs" before a "slope of enlightenment", or plateaus at a modest level, can only be known afterwards — which is exactly why the hype cycle cannot be used for prediction.

Common misconception

"Every technology goes through the trough of disillusionment and then reaches the plateau of productivity." The canonical curve is a narrative, not a law. Some technologies never mature; some mature without ever being hyped; some are hyped repeatedly. The practical lesson is to judge a technology by evidence — readiness, independent performance data, life-cycle and techno-economic assessments — not by where commentators place it on a curve.

Technologies against the planetary boundaries

We can now bring together the taxonomy and the planetary boundaries of Lesson 1.2. Assessing a technology against a boundary requires the life-cycle thinking of Lesson 1.3: compare it with the incumbent that it replaces, for the same function, and include every stage. Two different questions must be kept apart. The first is intensity: per functional unit, does the technology add or remove pressure on each boundary? The second is scale: how much of the total pressure could it remove if it spread? The second question needs a simple but powerful equation.

The substitution wedge: from product to planet(1.4.5)
\[ \frac{\Delta I}{I_0} = -\,\alpha\;s\;\big(1 - \rho\big)\,\big(1 - R\big), \qquad \rho = \frac{I_{\text{new}}}{I_{\text{old}}}\ \text{per functional unit} \]

the relative change in the total pressure on a boundary equals minus the share of that pressure caused by the product category being replaced, times the share of that category that is replaced, times one minus the intensity of the new technology relative to the old one, times one minus the rebound.

SymbolMeaningUnitExample
\(I_0\)total pressure on a boundary from the food system (e.g., GHG emissions, land)e.g., Gt CO₂e yr⁻¹–
\(\alpha\)share of \(I_0\) caused by the category that the technology can replace–0.56–0.58: animal products' share of food GHG
\(s\)adoption: share of that category actually replaced–0–1 (from Eqs. 1.4.1–1.4.2)
\(\rho\)relative intensity of the new technology per functional unit–0 (no pressure) to > 1 (worse)
\(R\)rebound: share of the potential saving cancelled by induced consumption or production–0–1 (Lesson 1.1, Eq. 1.1.9)
Follow the derivation
  1. Split the total pressure into the replaceable category, \(\alpha I_0\), and the rest, \((1-\alpha)I_0\).
  2. Replace a share \(s\) of the category's functional output by the new technology, whose pressure per functional unit is \(\rho\) times that of the incumbent: \(I_1 = (1-\alpha)I_0 + \alpha I_0\,[(1-s) + s\rho] = I_0\,[1 - \alpha s (1-\rho)]\).
  3. The potential saving is \(\alpha s (1-\rho) I_0\). If induced demand takes back a fraction \(R\) of it (cheaper protein leads to more protein eaten, or freed land is used for something else), the realised saving is \((1-R)\) times the potential: Eq. 1.4.5.
  4. If \(\rho > 1\) — the new technology is worse per unit for this boundary — the "saving" is negative: burden shifting (Section 1.4.7).
Assumptions and validity

Linear substitution at constant intensities: in reality \(\rho\) changes with scale and with the energy system, and substitution is not one-to-one (a plant-based burger may replace some beef, some chicken and some beans). \(\alpha\) must refer to the same boundary and the same system boundary as \(\rho\). The equation is a first-order teaching tool; integrated assessment models such as that of Humpenöder et al. (2022) resolve land markets and trade explicitly.

Systemic effects: rebound, burden shifting and lock-in

A technology's effect on the planet has several layers. The direct effect is what a product-level comparison captures: a kilogram of the new product versus a kilogram of the old. Indirect effects arise through markets and behaviour: a cheaper product is bought more, and resources freed in one place are used elsewhere. Structural effects change the system itself: new infrastructure, new dependencies, new winners and losers. Most sustainability claims describe only the first layer.

Rebound

You met the rebound effect in Lesson 1.1: when yields rose, cropland did not always shrink, because lower prices raised consumption or let a region capture export markets (Hertel et al., 2014; Pellegrini & Fernández, 2018). In energy economics the phenomenon has been debated since Jevons in 1865; a rebound larger than 100 % — efficiency gains that increase total resource use — is called backfire (Sorrell, 2009). Food technologies can trigger rebound in several ways: a cheaper alternative protein may be eaten in addition to meat rather than instead of it; a more efficient greenhouse may expand its area; land freed from pasture may be converted to bioenergy or cropland rather than restored. Eq. 1.4.5 contains the rebound explicitly for this reason.

Burden shifting

Burden shifting occurs when a technology reduces one pressure by increasing another — the typical trade-off being land against energy. Vertical farms and microbial protein from hydrogen use very little farmland but a great deal of electricity; if that electricity comes from solar panels, the panels need land too. Lock-in and the multi-level perspective

Technologies are embedded in systems of infrastructure, skills, regulations, business models, habits and meanings. Arthur (1989) showed with simple models how increasing returns — learning by doing, network effects, complementary investments — can make a technology dominant through historical accident and then lock the system into it, even if an alternative would have been better. Food systems are full of lock-ins: supermarket supply chains optimised for a few products, livestock-feed infrastructure, machinery sized for monocultures, subsidies tied to land area. The multi-level perspective of Geels (2002) describes how transitions happen despite lock-in: radical innovations develop in protected niches; the established regime is stable but not static; and pressure from the landscape — climate change, energy prices, geopolitical shocks, shifting values — can open windows of opportunity in which niche innovations break through (Figure 7).

Finally, technologies have distributional effects. A capital-intensive vertical farm in a wealthy city, a patented seed trait or a premium alternative protein may do little for the 2.69 billion people who cannot afford a healthy diet (Lesson 1.1), and may shift income from farmers to technology owners. Conversely, cheap, open technologies — improved storage, biological pest control, locally adapted varieties — can have large social returns. Lesson 15.3 returns to these questions.

EU context

The EU's Farm to Fork Strategy (2020) set aspirational targets for 2030: halve the use and risk of chemical pesticides and nutrient losses, reduce fertiliser use by at least 20 % and reach 25 % organic farmland. Several technologies in Table 1 — precision agriculture, robotics, biologicals, gene-edited disease resistance — are explicitly expected to help meet them. Note the tension this creates: the NGT regulation excludes NGT plants from organic production, so two policy goals pull in different directions (Lesson 14.2).

Responsible research and innovation

If technologies have systemic effects that are hard to foresee, how should societies steer them? The engineer and philosopher David Collingridge formulated the dilemma in 1980: when a technology is young, it is easy to change but we know little about its effects; when its effects are known, it is entrenched and hard to change (Collingridge, 1980). The Collingridge dilemma (Figure 8) is a strong argument against both blind acceleration and blanket prohibition, and for governance that learns as it goes.

Responsible research and innovation (RRI) is the European answer. Von Schomberg (2013) defined it as a transparent, interactive process in which societal actors and innovators become mutually responsive to one another with a view to the ethical acceptability, sustainability and societal desirability of the innovation process and its products. Stilgoe, Owen and Macnaghten (2013) proposed four dimensions that have become the standard framework:

  • Anticipation — systematically asking "what if?" about intended and unintended consequences, using scenarios, foresight and prospective assessments (prospective LCA, techno-economic analysis, Lesson 15.1).
  • Reflexivity — scientists and engineers examining their own assumptions, values and framings: why this problem, why this solution, whose knowledge counts?
  • Inclusion — opening up innovation to farmers, workers, consumers, civil society and those who may be harmed, early enough to matter (Module 13 on acceptance shows why this is not only an ethical nicety).
  • Responsiveness — the capacity to change direction in response to new knowledge and stakeholder views: stage-gates, adaptive regulation, sunset clauses, open data.

The regularity that unit cost falls by a constant fraction, the learning rate, for every doubling of cumulative production.

Gartner's narrative of expectations: trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment, plateau of productivity.
Sociology of expectations
The study of how promises about technologies mobilise resources and shape the technologies themselves.
Substitution wedge
The share of a total pressure removed by a technology: \(\alpha s(1-\rho)(1-R)\).
Burden shifting
Reducing one environmental pressure by increasing another, or moving it elsewhere in the life cycle or the world.
Lock-in
Self-reinforcing dominance of a technology or system through increasing returns, infrastructure and institutions.
Multi-level perspective
Geels' framework in which transitions arise from interactions between niches, regimes and landscape pressures.
Responsible research and innovation (RRI)
Governance of innovation through anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion and responsiveness; a response to the Collingridge dilemma.
