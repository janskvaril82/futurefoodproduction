Designing and analysing a sensory study

The tasting session is over. On the table lie 24 ballots with ticks, ratings and ranks — and questions to answer: can people taste the 10 % cricket flour, do they like the bread less, and what should the next recipe change? This lesson is the statistical toolkit for that moment and, more importantly, for the moment before it, when you decide in which order to serve the samples and how many panellists you need. Every method is built from two ingredients you already know — the binomial distribution and the analysis of variance — applied with care to the peculiar data of sensory science: forced choices with a known guessing rate, ratings that come in blocks of one person, and ticks in boxes.

What you will be able to do

  • Construct Williams Latin-square serving orders for even and odd numbers of samples and explain how they balance position and first-order carry-over effects (LO4).
  • Analyse triangle and other forced-choice tests with the exact binomial test and critical numbers, and estimate the size of the difference as a proportion of discriminators and as a Thurstonian d′ with confidence intervals (LO4).
  • Calculate the power and required panel size of a discrimination test, and test for similarity instead of claiming “no difference” (LO4).
  • Analyse paired preferences with a two-sided binomial test, and hedonic ratings as a randomised complete block design with panellists as blocks, followed by Tukey HSD and effect sizes (LO4).
  • Analyse CATA data with Cochran's Q and McNemar tests and JAR data with penalty analysis, and interpret them for product reformulation (LO4, LO6).
  • Plan a realistic student panel (about 20–40 people) and report a sensory study completely and honestly (LO4, LO6).

From question to analysis plan

Statistics cannot rescue a badly designed sensory study, but a well-designed one almost analyses itself. The design decisions — which test, which samples, which serving orders, how many panellists — determine which statistical model is valid. So before anyone tastes anything, write down an analysis plan: the research question, the primary outcome, the null and alternative hypotheses, the test and significance level, the planned panel size with its justification, the serving design and the effect size you will report. This is the sensory version of the preregistration introduced in Lesson 3.1, and it protects you against the temptation to hunt through many outcomes for a significant one.

Sensory data come in five shapes, and each has its own sampling model (Table 1). Two features recur. First, the panellist is the unit of replication: 24 panellists give 24 independent judgements, however many samples each tastes. Second, when every panellist tastes every sample, the judgements of one panellist are correlated — some people rate everything high, some everything low — so the panellist must be treated as a block. Ignoring this is the most common error in student sensory reports, and as you will see it costs a great deal of statistical power.

Two kinds of error frame every decision in this lesson. A type I error (probability α) is concluding that there is a difference, a preference or an effect when there is none; a type II error (probability β) is missing a difference that really exists. The conventional choice α = 0.05 protects against false alarms, but with the small panels of a course project β is usually much larger than α — often 0.3 to 0.6 — so the typical failure of a student sensory study is not a false positive but a real difference that the panel was too small to detect. That is why this lesson treats power and sample size as part of the design rather than as an afterthought, and why every non-significant result must be reported together with what the study could have detected.

Plan the session as carefully as the statistics. A realistic 40-minute session for a student panel is: welcome, information and signed consent (5 min); the background questionnaire, including the Food Neophobia Scale if you use it, answered before tasting (5 min); the discrimination test, because it needs the freshest palate (10 min); the hedonic test with JAR and CATA questions (15 min); debriefing (5 min). Each additional sample adds a few minutes and some fatigue, so three or four samples per test is a sensible maximum for untrained panellists. Blind and informed conditions belong in separate sessions or in separate, clearly ordered parts of a session (Lesson 13.3).

Finally, plan the data file. Record every judgement as one row in a “long” table — panellist, session, test, serving position, three-digit code, answer — and decode the codes into product names only after the session, from a key kept by the panel leader. Keep the paper ballots or the raw export until the report is graded, and have a second person check every manually entered value. Nearly all later analyses (Figures 2, 5 and 6) start from such a tidy table, reshaped into the format they need.

Key idea — decide the analysis before you see the data

Name one primary outcome (for example, overall liking of the 10 % cricket bread compared with wheat bread) and one test for it. Everything else — the other attributes, the CATA words, subgroup analyses by neophobia — is secondary and must be reported as exploratory. If you test eight CATA words at α = 0.05 when nothing really differs, you expect \(8\times0.05 = 0.4\) false positives; test twenty and you expect one.

Serving orders: Williams Latin squares

When a panellist tastes several samples in a row, two nuisance effects creep in. Position effects: a sample may be rated differently simply because it came first (the first sample of a session is often rated more extremely) or last (fatigue). Carry-over effects: a sample may be rated differently because of what came just before it — wheat bread tastes blander after an earthy cricket bread, and the bitterness of a seaweed crisp lingers into the next sample (Lesson 13.3). If every panellist tasted the products in the same order, product effects would be completely confounded with position and carry-over effects. Randomising the order for each panellist removes the systematic confounding but leaves chance imbalances that are large in a small panel. The better solution is to balance the orders by design.

A Latin square of order \(t\) is a \(t\times t\) table in which each of \(t\) samples appears once in every row (panellist or group of panellists) and once in every column (serving position). The simplest one is cyclic — ABC, BCA, CAB — but in it, B always follows A, C always follows B and A always follows C: carry-over is not balanced at all. Williams (1949) showed how to construct Latin squares in which every sample is also preceded by every other sample equally often, which balances first-order carry-over. MacFie et al. (1989) introduced these designs to consumer testing, where they are now standard.

Williams design: construction of carry-over-balanced serving orders(13.4.1)
\[ a = \bigl(0,\;1,\;t-1,\;2,\;t-2,\;3,\;t-3,\;\dots\bigr) \] \[ s_{i,j} = \left(a_j + i\right) \bmod t, \qquad i = 0,\dots,t-1 \]

write the first sequence by alternating from the bottom and the top of the list of sample numbers: zero, one, t minus one, two, t minus two, and so on. Every further sequence adds one to each number of the previous sequence, wrapping around after t minus one. For an even number of samples these t sequences form a Williams square; for an odd number, add the t sequences written backwards, giving 2t sequences.

Follow the derivation — why does the zig-zag balance carry-over?
  1. Latin property. Adding the same number \(i\) (mod \(t\)) to every entry of a sequence that contains each sample once gives another such sequence, and in column \(j\) the \(t\) rows contain \(a_j, a_j+1, \dots, a_j+t-1\) (mod \(t\)) — every sample once. So the \(t\) sequences form a Latin square: positions are balanced.
  2. Differences. The ordered pair “sample \(u\) followed by sample \(v\)” appears in row \(i\) at positions \(j, j+1\) when \(v - u \equiv a_{j+1} - a_j \pmod t\). The differences of the zig-zag first row are \(1, -2, 3, -4, \dots\), which modulo \(t\) are \(1, t-2, 3, t-4, \dots\).
  3. Even \(t\). These \(t-1\) differences are all different and non-zero modulo \(t\) (for \(t = 4\): 1, 2, 3). Each difference therefore occurs in exactly one position pair, and cycling through the \(t\) rows makes every ordered pair \((u, v)\) with that difference appear exactly once: every sample follows every other sample exactly once.
  4. Odd \(t\). The \(t-1\) differences cannot all be different (for \(t = 3\): 1 and \(-2 \equiv 1\)). Adding the mirror image of each sequence (read backwards) supplies the missing differences, so in the \(2t\) sequences every ordered pair appears exactly twice.
Assumptions and validity

Balance is for first-order carry-over (the effect of the immediately preceding sample only). The panel size should be a multiple of \(t\) (even \(t\)) or \(2t\) (odd \(t\)) so that every sequence is used equally often; otherwise balance is approximate. Assign the sequences to panellists at random, and give every sample a random three-digit code. For analysis, balance means that product means are not biased by position or carry-over; position can be added to the model as a factor if you want to estimate it.

Three practical questions arise when you apply the designs. What if the panel size is not a multiple of the number of sequences? Use complete sets of sequences for as many panellists as possible and draw the remaining sequences at random; the imbalance is then limited to a few panellists, and the generator below shows exactly how large it is. Recruiting a few extra panellists so that no-shows do not break the balance is a cheap insurance. What if there are too many samples for one person to taste? With more than about five or six samples, fatigue and adaptation make the later judgements unreliable. The options are to split the evaluation over two sessions (each a complete Williams design over a subset, with some samples in common) or to use a balanced incomplete block design, in which every panellist tastes only some of the samples but every pair of samples is tasted together equally often; such designs need specialised analysis and are beyond a student project. Should the first sample be a “warm-up”? Some laboratories serve an unscored warm-up sample to absorb the first-position effect. It costs one sample of fatigue and is worth considering when position effects are expected to be large, for example with strongly flavoured products.

Balancing does not remove position and carry-over effects from the data; it spreads them evenly over the products, so that they inflate the error variance instead of biasing the product means. If you want to know how large they were, add serving position as a factor to the analysis of variance: in a balanced design its effect can be estimated separately from the product effect, and removing it from the error term can even increase power slightly.

Triangle-test statistics: the exact binomial test

In Lesson 13.3 you met the binomial model of guessing. Turned into a hypothesis test, it becomes the standard analysis of every forced-choice discrimination test. For the triangle test the hypotheses are

\(H_0\): \(p_c = 1/3\) (nobody perceives a difference)   versus   \(H_1\): \(p_c > 1/3\) (some people do).

The alternative is one-sided: a perceptible difference can only increase the probability of a correct answer, never decrease it below the guessing rate. The same logic applies to the duo–trio and 2-AFC tests with \(p_0 = 1/2\) and to the 3-AFC and tetrad with \(p_0 = 1/3\).

Exact binomial test and critical number of correct answers(13.4.2)
\[ p = P(X \ge x \mid n, p_0) = \sum_{i=x}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\,p_0^{\,i}\,(1-p_0)^{\,n-i} \] \[ x_c = \min\Bigl\{k : P(X \ge k \mid n, p_0) \le \alpha\Bigr\} \] \[ x_c \approx \Bigl\lceil n p_0 + z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{n p_0 (1-p_0)} + \tfrac12 \Bigr\rceil \]

the p-value is the probability, if everyone were guessing, of obtaining at least the observed number of correct answers. The critical number is the smallest number of correct answers whose tail probability is at most alpha; it is approximately the expected number of correct guesses plus z standard deviations plus one half, rounded up.

Follow the derivation
  1. Test statistic. Under \(H_0\) the number of correct answers is \(X\sim\text{Bin}(n, p_0)\) exactly (Eq. 13.3.3). Large values of \(X\) are evidence for \(H_1\).
  2. p-value. The probability, under \(H_0\), of a result at least as extreme as the one observed is the upper tail \(P(X\ge x)\).
  3. Decision rule. Reject \(H_0\) if \(p \le \alpha\). Because the tail probability decreases as \(x\) increases, this is the same as rejecting when \(x \ge x_c\), the smallest \(k\) with \(P(X\ge k)\le\alpha\). Tables of \(x_c\) (ISO 4120:2021, Table A.1) are just this calculation done in advance.
  4. Normal approximation. For large \(n\), \(X \approx N(np_0,\, np_0(1-p_0))\). The critical value on the continuous scale is \(np_0 + z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{np_0(1-p_0)}\); adding ½ (continuity correction) and rounding up approximates the exact \(x_c\). For the triangle test \(np_0(1-p_0) = 2n/9\).
  5. Discreteness. Because \(X\) takes only integer values, the actual false-positive rate \(P(X\ge x_c)\) is usually below α (for n = 24: 0.028 instead of 0.05). The exact test is conservative.
Assumptions and validity

Independent judgements, a true forced choice and balanced presentation orders. Replicated evaluations by the same assessors are not independent. ISO 4120:2021 notes that pooling replicates is still valid for the difference test with its Table A.1, but not for the similarity test; replicated data are better analysed with beta-binomial models (Brockhoff & Christensen, 2010; Christensen, 2023). Avoid replicates if you can; if you cannot, give every assessor the same number.

The exact test deserves two comments. First, the p-value is not the probability that the products are identical, nor the probability that your conclusion is wrong; it is the probability of data at least this extreme if nobody could perceive a difference. Second, because a count of correct answers can only take whole-number values, the test can rarely use its full α: with 24 assessors, the probability of 13 or more correct answers by chance is 0.028, and of 12 or more 0.068, so the test with critical number 13 operates at an actual significance level of 0.028. This conservatism is harmless for a single test but is one more reason why discrimination tests with small panels have low power. Resist the temptation to “round” a result: 12 of 24 correct is not significant at α = 0.05, however close it feels.

How large is the difference? Discriminators and Thurstonian d′

A p-value answers “is there a difference?” but not “how big is it?”. Two measures of size are used for discrimination tests. The older one comes from a simple story about the panel; the modern one from a model of perception.

The guessing model: proportion of discriminators

Imagine that the population consists of two kinds of people: discriminators, who always perceive the difference and answer correctly, and non-discriminators, who always guess. If a proportion \(p_d\) are discriminators, the probability of a correct answer is the probability of being a discriminator plus the probability of being a non-discriminator who guesses right.

Guessing model: proportion of discriminators and its confidence interval(13.4.3)
\[ p_c = p_d + (1 - p_d)\,p_0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \hat p_d = \frac{\hat p_c - p_0}{1 - p_0} \] \[ \mathrm{SE}(\hat p_d) = \frac{1}{1-p_0}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)}{n}}, \qquad \text{95\% CI: } \hat p_d \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_d) \] \[ \text{triangle } \bigl(p_0 = \tfrac13\bigr):\quad \hat p_d = \frac{3\hat p_c - 1}{2} \]

the probability of a correct answer equals the proportion of discriminators plus the proportion of non-discriminators times the guessing probability. Solving for the proportion of discriminators gives the observed proportion correct minus the guessing probability, divided by one minus the guessing probability; its standard error is the standard error of the observed proportion scaled by the same factor. For the triangle test this simplifies to three times the proportion correct minus one, divided by two.

Assumptions and validity

The all-or-none story is psychologically unrealistic — perception is graded, not a switch (Ennis, 1993) — and \(p_d\) depends on the test: the same products give a larger \(p_d\) in a 2-AFC than in a triangle test. Use \(p_d\) for decisions within one method (ISO 4120 expresses its tables in terms of \(p_d\)); use \(d'\) to compare methods. The normal-approximation interval is rough for small \(n\) and can extend below 0; truncate it at 0.

The Thurstonian model: d′ for the triangle test

Signal detection theory (Eq. 13.3.4) describes the difference as the distance \(d'\) between the perceptual distributions of the two products, measured in standard deviations of perceptual noise. What differs between tests is the decision rule an assessor applies to the perceptions, and hence the psychometric function that links \(d'\) to the probability of a correct answer (Ennis, 1993; Brockhoff & Christensen, 2010).

Thurstonian psychometric function of the triangle test and estimation of d′(13.4.4)
\[ \begin{aligned} p_c = f_{\text{tri}}(d') &= 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigl[\Phi\bigl(-z\sqrt3 + d'\sqrt{2/3}\bigr) \\ &\qquad\quad + \Phi\bigl(-z\sqrt3 - d'\sqrt{2/3}\bigr)\Bigr]\,\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z \end{aligned} \] \[ \hat d' = f_{\text{tri}}^{-1}(\hat p_c), \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat d') \approx \frac{\sqrt{\hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)/n}}{f_{\text{tri}}'(\hat d')} \]

the probability of a correct triangle answer is twice the integral, over all positive values z of a standard normal variable, of the sum of two normal probabilities that depend on d-prime, weighted by the normal density of z. d-prime is estimated by finding the value whose predicted proportion correct equals the observed one; its standard error is the standard error of the observed proportion divided by the slope of the psychometric function at that point.

  1. Perceptions. Two samples of A give \(a_1, a_2 \sim N(0,1)\); the sample of B gives \(b \sim N(d', 1)\), all independent.
  2. Decision rule. The assessor judges the two samples whose perceptions are closest to be “the same” and names the third as odd (the rule analysed by Ennis, 1993). The answer is correct if \(|a_1 - a_2|\) is smaller than both \(|b - a_1|\) and \(|b - a_2|\).
  3. Change variables. Write \(a_{1,2} = m \pm \delta\) with midpoint \(m = (a_1+a_2)/2\) and half-distance \(\delta = (a_1 - a_2)/2\); for normal variables \(m\) and \(\delta\) are independent, \(\delta\sim N(0, \tfrac12)\). Let \(w = b - m \sim N(d', \tfrac32)\).
  4. Simplify the condition. \(|b - a_{1,2}| = |w \mp \delta|\) and \(|a_1 - a_2| = 2|\delta|\). Both distances exceed \(2|\delta|\) exactly when \(|w| > 3|\delta|\).
  5. Standardise. Put \(\delta = z/\sqrt2\) with \(z\sim N(0,1)\) and \(w = d' + \sqrt{3/2}\,\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)\). Then \(|w| > 3|z|/\sqrt2\) happens with conditional probability \(\Phi\!\bigl((d' - 3|z|/\sqrt2)/\sqrt{3/2}\bigr) + \Phi\!\bigl((-d' - 3|z|/\sqrt2)/\sqrt{3/2}\bigr) = \Phi(-\sqrt3|z| + d'\sqrt{2/3}) + \Phi(-\sqrt3|z| - d'\sqrt{2/3})\).
  6. Average over \(z\): \(E[g(|z|)] = 2\int_0^\infty g(z)\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\), which gives the formula. Check: at \(d' = 0\) it equals \(4\int_0^\infty\Phi(-\sqrt3 z)\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z = 4\times\tfrac{1}{12} = \tfrac13\) — pure guessing ✓.

Assumptions and validity

Equal-variance normal perceptions of a single underlying dimension and assessors who use the decision rule above. If \(\hat p_c \le 1/3\), the estimate is \(\hat d' = 0\) (the parameter cannot be negative). The Wald interval \(\hat d' \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\) is poor when \(\hat p_c\) is near 1/3 because the slope is almost zero there; likelihood-based intervals are better (Christensen, 2023). Because \(d'\) does not depend on the method, it is the right measure for comparing results from different tests or panels.

Table 3 makes Frijters' (1979) “paradox of discriminatory non-discriminators” concrete. Take two products separated by \(d' = 1\). A triangle test is answered correctly 41.8 % of the time, which the guessing model translates into \(p_d = (0.418 - 0.333)/0.667 = 0.13\): only 13 % “discriminators”. A 2-AFC test on the same products and the same people is answered correctly 76.0 % of the time, i.e., \(p_d = (0.760 - 0.5)/0.5 = 0.52\): suddenly half the population “discriminates”. Nothing about the products or the people has changed — only the task. The Thurstonian model resolves the paradox: both results correspond to the same \(d' = 1\); the triangle task is simply harder, because the assessor must compare three noisy perceptions without knowing which attribute to attend to. This is why \(d'\), not \(p_d\) or the percentage correct, is the quantity to compare across methods, panels and studies.

What does a given \(d'\) mean in practice? At \(d' = 1\), a randomly chosen perception of product B exceeds a randomly chosen perception of product A 76 % of the time — noticeable, but easily lost in the noise of a single tasting. At \(d' = 2\) this rises to 92 %; at \(d' = 0.5\) it is only 64 %. Whether such a difference matters for consumers is not a statistical question: a product developer replacing an ingredient may accept \(d' = 0.5\), while a claim of “tastes just like the original” demands much less.

Power and sample size for discrimination tests

Power is the probability that a test detects a difference that really exists. For a discrimination test it depends on four things: the method (through its psychometric function), the true size of the difference, the number of assessors and α. The calculation is exact, because we know the distribution of the number of correct answers both when there is no difference and when there is one.

Power of a discrimination test and the number of assessors needed(13.4.5)
\[ 1-\beta = P\bigl(X \ge x_c \mid n, p_{c1}\bigr) = \sum_{x=x_c}^{n}\binom{n}{x}p_{c1}^{\,x}(1-p_{c1})^{\,n-x} \] \[ p_{c1} = p_0 + p_d(1-p_0) \quad\text{or}\quad p_{c1} = f(d') \] \[ n \approx \left[\frac{z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)} + z_{1-\beta}\sqrt{p_{c1}(1-p_{c1})}}{p_{c1} - p_0}\right]^{2} \]

the power is the probability of reaching at least the critical number of correct answers when the true probability of a correct answer is p c one, which follows from the assumed proportion of discriminators or from d-prime through the method's psychometric function. The normal approximation gives the number of assessors as the square of the sum of two z-values, each weighted by a binomial standard deviation, divided by the difference between the alternative and the guessing probability.

The hardest step in a power calculation is not the arithmetic but the choice of the difference you want to detect. It should come from the purpose of the test, not from what your panel size happens to allow. If the question is “would a reasonable share of consumers notice the cricket flour?”, you might decide that a proportion of discriminators of 0.3 in a triangle test — roughly one in three tasters — is the smallest difference worth detecting. If you prefer the Thurstonian scale, \(d' = 1\) to 1.5 is a moderate difference for untrained assessors. Having chosen the effect, choose α and the target power: 0.05 and 0.80 are conventional for a difference test, but if missing a difference would be costly (a supplier change that consumers notice), a higher power — 0.90 — is justified. Similarity: showing that a difference is too small to matter

Often the hoped-for result is no difference: the cheaper seaweed supplier, the energy-saving baking programme or the hydroponic lettuce should taste “the same”. A non-significant difference test cannot show this (Lesson 13.3). ISO 4120:2021 instead offers a similarity test, in which “similar” does not mean identical but that any perceptible difference is so small that the products can be used interchangeably. The trick is to reverse the hypotheses: the null hypothesis is now that the difference is at least as large as a tolerable maximum \(p_{d0}\) that you choose in advance.

Similarity test for a triangle test(13.4.6)
\[ H_0: p_d \ge p_{d0} \quad\text{versus}\quad H_1: p_d < p_{d0} \] \[ p_{c0} = p_0 + p_{d0}(1-p_0) \] \[ p_{\text{sim}} = P\bigl(X \le x \mid n,\; p_{c0}\bigr) = \sum_{i=0}^{x}\binom{n}{i}p_{c0}^{\,i}(1-p_{c0})^{\,n-i} \] \[ \text{similar if } p_{\text{sim}} \le \beta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \hat p_d + z_{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_d) < p_{d0} \;\;\text{(approx.)} \]

the null hypothesis says that at least the tolerable proportion of discriminators exists. The similarity p-value is the probability of so few correct answers or fewer if the difference were exactly at that tolerable limit. If this probability is at most beta, you conclude similarity — which is approximately the same as the upper confidence limit of the proportion of discriminators lying below the tolerable limit.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(p_{d0}\)largest tolerable proportion of discriminators (decided before testing)–0.2–0.3
\(p_{c0}\)probability correct at that limit–0.47–0.53 (triangle)

\(z_{1-\beta}\)one-sided normal quantile–1.645
Follow the derivation
  1. Burden of proof reversed. To claim similarity you must reject “the difference is important”. Choose \(p_{d0}\) from the practical consequences (how many consumers may notice the change?) before testing.
  2. Least favourable case. Among all \(p_d \ge p_{d0}\), the one most likely to produce few correct answers is \(p_d = p_{d0}\), i.e., \(p_c = p_{c0}\). If few correct answers are improbable even there, they are improbable for every larger difference.
  3. p-value: small counts are now the evidence, so \(p_{\text{sim}}\) is the lower tail \(P(X\le x\mid n, p_{c0})\).
  4. Confidence-interval form. Rejecting \(H_0\) at level β is (approximately) equivalent to the one-sided \((1-\beta)\) upper confidence limit of \(p_d\) lying below \(p_{d0}\), using \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat p_d)\) from Eq. 13.4.3.
Assumptions and validity

Independent judgements: ISO 4120:2021 explicitly warns that replicated evaluations must not be treated as independent in the similarity test. Similarity needs large panels — ISO suggests about twice as many assessors as for a difference test, approximately 60 — because a small panel cannot rule out a moderate difference. Always report \(p_{d0}\) and β with the result.

All terms are probabilities — dimensionless ✓

The tolerable limit \(p_{d0}\) is a decision about consequences, and it should be written into the analysis plan with its justification. A company changing a supplier might accept that up to 20–30 % of consumers could notice the change in a direct comparison, because in everyday eating nobody compares the old and the new product side by side. A study aiming to support the statement “hydroponic lettuce tastes the same as soil-grown lettuce” should use a stricter limit. The choice of β matters in the same way: in a similarity test β, not α, is the risk of the error you most want to avoid — wrongly declaring the products interchangeable. The same logic of reversed hypotheses is known in other fields as equivalence testing, and it underlies, for example, the comparison of generic and original medicines.

Paired preference tests

In a paired preference test each consumer tastes two coded samples in a balanced order (AB for half the consumers, BA for the other half) and chooses the one he or she prefers. Under the null hypothesis of no preference, each sample is chosen with probability 1/2. Unlike the 2-AFC difference test, there is no “correct” answer: either product may turn out to be preferred, and before the test you usually do not know which. The test is therefore two-sided.

Two-sided exact binomial test for paired preference(13.4.7)
\[ p = \min\Bigl\{1,\; 2\min\bigl[P(X \ge x),\; P(X \le x)\bigr]\Bigr\}, \qquad X \sim \text{Bin}\!\left(n, \tfrac12\right) \] \[ z = \frac{\lvert x - n/2\rvert - \tfrac12}{\sqrt{n/4}} \]

the two-sided p-value is twice the smaller of the two tail probabilities of the number of consumers preferring product A, capped at one; for larger panels a normal approximation with continuity correction gives the z statistic.

Follow the derivation
  1. Under \(H_0\) every choice is a fair coin toss: \(X\sim\text{Bin}(n, \tfrac12)\), symmetric about \(n/2\).
  2. Results in either direction count as evidence, so the p-value adds both tails: by symmetry, twice the tail beyond the observed count.
  3. Normal approximation: mean \(n/2\), standard deviation \(\sqrt{n/4}\); subtracting ½ from the distance corrects for the discreteness of \(X\).
Assumptions and validity — and the “no preference” problem

Independent consumers and a balanced serving order (position bias favours the first or second sample). If you offer a “no preference” box, you must decide in advance what to do with those answers: dropping them reduces \(n\); splitting them equally between the two products is conservative; splitting them in proportion to the other answers keeps the observed ratio. A forced choice avoids the problem but frustrates consumers who genuinely have no preference. Using a one-sided test because the result came out in the “right” direction is a classic error: it halves the p-value.

Counts and probabilities are dimensionless ✓

A preference test and a difference test answer different questions, and a product can pass one and fail the other. Products that differ perceptibly may still be equally preferred, because half the consumers like the difference and half dislike it; conversely, a weak but consistent preference can be detected in a preference test with many consumers even when a triangle test with the same people finds nothing. For novel foods, preference tests are also sensitive to information: “Which bread do you prefer?” gives one answer when both samples are coded and another when one of them is labelled “with insect flour”. Decide which question you are asking — sensory preference (blind) or product preference (informed) — and report the condition.

Paired preference data are binary, so the effect size is simply the proportion of consumers preferring one product, with a confidence interval. With 30 consumers, the exact 95 % confidence interval for an observed proportion of 20/30 = 0.67 extends from 0.47 to 0.83, a reminder that even a “clear” preference in a class-sized panel is compatible with a wide range of population preferences.

Hedonic data: ANOVA with panellists as blocks

In a hedonic test each of \(b\) panellists rates each of \(t\) products — a randomised complete block design (RCBD) in which the panellists are the blocks (Eq. 3.1.5). People differ enormously in how they use the scale: one student gives everything 7–9, another 3–6. These differences are not interesting for comparing products, but they are large. The block design removes them from the error term, and that is where its power comes from.

Two-way ANOVA without interaction: products and panellists(13.4.8)
\[ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_j + \beta_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad SS_T = SS_{\text{prod}} + SS_{\text{pan}} + SS_E \] \[ SS_{\text{prod}} = b\sum_{j=1}^{t}(\bar y_{\cdot j} - \bar y)^2, \qquad SS_{\text{pan}} = t\sum_{i=1}^{b}(\bar y_{i\cdot} - \bar y)^2 \] \[ F = \frac{SS_{\text{prod}}/(t-1)}{SS_E/\bigl[(t-1)(b-1)\bigr]} \]

each rating is the overall mean plus a product effect plus a panellist effect plus random error. The total variation splits into variation between products, variation between panellists and residual error. The F ratio compares the product mean square with the error mean square, whose degrees of freedom are the product of the degrees of freedom of products and panellists.

Follow the derivation of the partition
  1. Decompose each deviation: \(y_{ij} - \bar y = (\bar y_{\cdot j} - \bar y) + (\bar y_{i\cdot} - \bar y) + (y_{ij} - \bar y_{\cdot j} - \bar y_{i\cdot} + \bar y)\) — product part, panellist part and residual.
  2. Square and sum over all \(i, j\). The cross-products vanish because each part sums to zero over the index it does not depend on; what remains is \(SS_T = SS_{\text{prod}} + SS_{\text{pan}} + SS_E\). (The product part is the same for all \(b\) panellists, hence the factor \(b\); the panellist part the same for all \(t\) products, hence \(t\).)
  3. Degrees of freedom: \(bt - 1 = (t-1) + (b-1) + (t-1)(b-1)\).
  4. Test. If products do not differ, \(MS_{\text{prod}}\) and \(MS_E\) both estimate \(\sigma^2\) and their ratio follows an F distribution with \((t-1)\) and \((t-1)(b-1)\) degrees of freedom.
  5. By hand use totals: correction factor \(CF = G^2/N\); \(SS_T = \sum y^2 - CF\); \(SS_{\text{prod}} = \sum_j T_j^2/b - CF\); \(SS_{\text{pan}} = \sum_i B_i^2/t - CF\); \(SS_E\) by subtraction (\(G\) grand total, \(T_j\) product totals, \(B_i\) panellist totals).
  6. Why blocking helps. In a one-way ANOVA that ignores panellists, \(SS_{\text{pan}}\) is left inside the error term. Blocking moves it out, so \(MS_E\) shrinks — at the cost of \(b-1\) error degrees of freedom.
Assumptions and validity

Additivity (a panellist who rates high rates all products higher by the same amount — no panellist × product interaction, which cannot be separated from error without replicates), normally distributed errors with equal variance, and independence between panellists. Hedonic categories are ordinal and bounded, so these assumptions hold only approximately; ANOVA is robust for balanced designs with 20 or more panellists (Næs et al., 2010). Check residuals; if they are badly skewed, confirm with the Friedman test (Eq. 13.3.6). With balanced complete data, treating panellists as a random effect in a mixed model gives the same F test for products.

Sums of squares are in points²; the F ratio is (points²/points²) — dimensionless ✓

Follow the derivation and interpretation
  1. \(\eta_p^2\) removes the panellist variation from the denominator, so it describes the product effect relative to the noise that remains after blocking (Lakens, 2013).
  2. For two products, each panellist's difference \(d_i\) removes that panellist's scale use; \(\bar d\) is the average preference in scale points, the most interpretable effect size.
  3. Dividing by \(s_d\) gives \(d_z\), which also equals the paired t statistic divided by \(\sqrt b\). Cohen's (1988) conventional benchmarks (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) were defined for independent groups and should not be applied mechanically to \(d_z\) (Lakens, 2013).
  4. The interval \(\bar d \pm t\,s_d/\sqrt b\) is the paired t confidence interval: report it in points — “the 10 % bread was liked 1.0 point less (95 % CI 0.3–1.7)” is a statement a product developer can use.
Assumptions and validity

The paired interval assumes approximately normal differences; with 20 or more panellists the central limit theorem helps. \(\eta_p^2\) depends on the design (which products were included) and is not comparable across studies with different product sets.

\(\bar d\) and \(s_d\) are in points, so \(d_z\) is dimensionless; SS ratios are dimensionless ✓

With the preloaded data the block ANOVA gives \(F(2, 46) = 5.56\), \(p = 0.007\), whereas the one-way ANOVA that ignores panellists gives \(F(2, 69) = 2.99\), \(p = 0.057\) — not significant. The same 72 ratings, analysed with and without the block structure, lead to different conclusions. Tukey's test on the block error term shows that the 10 % bread was liked less than the wheat bread (difference 1.00 point, simultaneous 95 % CI 0.23–1.77) and less than the 5 % bread (0.79, CI 0.03–1.56), while 0 % and 5 % did not differ detectably (0.21). Partial η² = 0.19, and for the wheat–10 % comparison \(d_z = 0.63\).

A good hedonic analysis follows the same sequence every time. 1. Look at the data: a table of means, SDs and n per product, and a plot like Figure 5 in which each panellist is a line; check for impossible values (a 0 or a 10 on a 9-point scale is a typing error). 2. Fit the block ANOVA and read the product F test. 3. Compare pairs with Tukey's HSD if there are more than two products, or with the paired t-test and its confidence interval if there are two. 4. Report effect sizes in scale points with confidence intervals, plus \(\eta_p^2\) or \(d_z\). 5. Check the assumptions: plot the residuals \(y_{ij} - \bar y_{i\cdot} - \bar y_{\cdot j} + \bar y\) against the fitted values and in a normal quantile plot; strong skewness, many ratings piled up at the scale ends or a few wild panellists call for a Friedman test as a check.

Two complications are common in student data. Missing ratings: if a panellist leaves before tasting the last sample, the block is incomplete. The simplest honest solution is to analyse only complete blocks and report how many were dropped; a linear mixed model with panellists as a random effect can use the incomplete blocks too (Næs et al., 2010). Hidden segments: a mean liking of 6 can arise because everybody likes the product moderately or because half the panel loves it and half dislikes it. For novel foods, segments often follow food neophobia (Lesson 13.1). Look at the distribution of ratings per product, and, as a clearly labelled exploratory analysis, compare liking between panellists with low and high neophobia scores. A segment that dislikes the product is a finding in itself — it tells you who the product is for.

CATA and JAR data

CATA: Cochran's Q and McNemar tests

A CATA question produces, for each consumer, product and word, a 0 (not ticked) or a 1 (ticked). The natural summary is the proportion of consumers who ticked the word for each product. Because the same consumers evaluated all products, the proportions are not independent — the consumers are again blocks — and the chi-square test for independent samples is not valid. The appropriate test is Cochran's (1950) Q, the standard test for differences in CATA citation frequencies between products (Meyners et al., 2013).

Cochran's Q test for a CATA word across k products(13.4.11)
\[ Q = \frac{(k-1)\left[k\sum_{j=1}^{k}C_j^{2} - N^{2}\right]}{k\,N - \sum_{i=1}^{n}R_i^{2}} \;\;\dot\sim\;\; \chi^2_{k-1} \] \[ N = \sum_j C_j = \sum_i R_i \]

Cochran's Q equals k minus one times the difference between k times the sum of the squared column totals and the squared grand total, divided by k times the grand total minus the sum of the squared row totals. Under the null hypothesis that the word is equally likely to be ticked for all products, Q follows approximately a chi-square distribution with k minus one degrees of freedom.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(k\)number of products–
\(n\)number of consumers (blocks)–
\(C_j\)column total: consumers ticking the word for product \(j\)–
\(R_i\)row total: products for which consumer \(i\) ticked the word (0 … \(k\))–
\(N\)total number of ticks for the word–
Follow the derivation
  1. Condition on each consumer's row total. Under \(H_0\) the products are interchangeable, so a consumer who ticked the word for \(R_i\) of the \(k\) products is equally likely to have ticked any \(R_i\) of them. Consumers who ticked the word for none or all products (\(R_i = 0\) or \(k\)) carry no information — note that they contribute nothing to the denominator.
  2. Column totals. Under \(H_0\), \(E[C_j] = N/k\). \(Q\) is a standardised sum of squared deviations \(\sum_j (C_j - N/k)^2\), with the variance computed from the row totals: \(\mathrm{Var} \propto \sum_i R_i(k - R_i)/[k^2(k-1)]\).
  3. Algebra turns \(\dfrac{k(k-1)\sum_j (C_j - N/k)^2}{\sum_i R_i(k-R_i)}\) into the computational form, because \(k\sum(C_j - N/k)^2 = k\sum C_j^2 - N^2\) and \(\sum R_i(k-R_i) = kN - \sum R_i^2\).
  4. Two products. For \(k = 2\), Q reduces to McNemar's (1947) test, \(\chi^2 = (b - c)^2/(b + c)\), where \(b\) and \(c\) count consumers who ticked the word for only the first or only the second product. For small counts use the exact version: a binomial test of \(b\) out of \(b + c\) with \(p_0 = 1/2\).
Assumptions and validity

Independent consumers who evaluated every product (complete blocks). The chi-square approximation needs enough informative consumers (those with \(0 < R_i < k\)). One test is run per word, so control the multiple-testing problem, for example by the Holm correction or by treating the word tests as exploratory. A significant Q is followed by pairwise McNemar tests. Correspondence analysis maps products and words together (Meyners et al., 2013); CATA can also be linked to liking (Ares et al., 2014).

Counts only — Q is dimensionless ✓
Worked example 8Is the cricket bread more “earthy”?

Ten consumers answered a CATA question for wheat bread (0 %), 5 % and 10 % cricket bread. Their ticks for “earthy” (0/1 for 0 %, 5 %, 10 %) were: 011, 001, 111, 011, 000, 011, 001, 101, 011, 001. (a) Compute Cochran's Q. (b) Compare 0 % and 10 % with an exact McNemar test.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Column totals: \(C = (2, 5, 9)\); \(N = 16\). Row totals: 2, 1, 3, 2, 0, 2, 1, 2, 2, 1; \(\sum R_i^2 = 4+1+9+4+0+4+1+4+4+1 = 32\).
  2. Numerator: \((k-1)[k\sum C_j^2 - N^2] = 2\,[3\times(4 + 25 + 81) - 256] = 2\,[330 - 256] = 148\).
  3. Denominator: \(kN - \sum R_i^2 = 48 - 32 = 16\). \(Q = 148/16 = 9.25\).
  4. p-value: \(\chi^2\) with 2 df: \(p = e^{-9.25/2} = 0.0098\). The frequency of “earthy” differs between the breads.
  5. (b) McNemar, 0 % vs 10 %: discordant consumers: 7 ticked “earthy” for 10 % only, 0 for 0 % only. Exact two-sided \(p = 2\times(1/2)^7 = 0.016\): the 10 % bread is described as earthy more often.
Q = 9.25 (2 df), p = 0.010; McNemar 0 % vs 10 %: 7 vs 0 discordant, p = 0.016

With the preloaded data of Figure 6 (30 consumers, eight words), the words “nutty” (Q = 16.8) and “earthy” (Q = 19.9) differ strongly between the breads and remain significant after the Holm correction, both being ticked more often for the cricket breads. “Dry” and “off-flavour” have unadjusted p-values below 0.05 but not after correction: they are candidates to watch in the next study, not findings. This is a typical CATA pattern: a few words carry the product differences, while most words are ticked at similar rates for all samples. Remember also what CATA does not measure: a tick records that a word applies, not how strongly — two products ticked “bitter” by the same number of consumers may differ greatly in bitterness intensity.

JAR data: penalty analysis

Just-about-right data tell you what consumers think is wrong; liking data tell you whether it matters. Penalty analysis combines them (Rothman & Parker, 2009). For each attribute, consumers are grouped into “too little” (below JAR), “just about right” and “too much” (above JAR). For each non-JAR group you compute how much lower its mean liking is than that of the JAR group — the penalty or mean drop — and how many consumers are in it.

Penalty (mean drop) and weighted penalty(13.4.12)
\[ \Delta_g = \bar L_{\text{JAR}} - \bar L_g, \qquad P_g = \frac{n_g}{n}\,\Delta_g \]

the penalty of a non-JAR group is the mean liking of the consumers who found the attribute just about right minus the mean liking of the group; the weighted penalty multiplies it by the fraction of all consumers in that group, giving the drop in overall mean liking attributable to the group.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(g\)non-JAR group of an attribute (“too little” or “too much”)–
\(\bar L_{\text{JAR}}, \bar L_g\)mean overall liking of the JAR group and of group \(g\)points
\(n_g, n\)consumers in group \(g\), all consumers–
\(\Delta_g, P_g\)penalty (mean drop) and weighted penaltypoints
Follow the reasoning
  1. If the non-JAR consumers of group \(g\) could be moved to “just about right” (by reformulation) and would then like the product as much as the JAR group does, their liking would rise by \(\Delta_g\).
  2. Their share of the panel is \(n_g/n\), so overall mean liking would rise by \((n_g/n)\Delta_g = P_g\) — the weighted penalty, a first estimate of the pay-off from fixing the attribute.
  3. A penalty plot shows \(\Delta_g\) against the percentage \(100\,n_g/n\); attributes in the upper right (many consumers, large drop) are the priorities. In practice, many analysts interpret only groups that contain at least about 20 % of consumers.
Assumptions and validity

Penalty analysis is descriptive and correlational: a consumer who finds the bread too bitter may dislike it for other reasons, and attributes are correlated. Small groups give unstable means — test the drop (for example with a Welch t-test) and interpret groups below about 20 % cautiously. Asking JAR questions may change how consumers rate liking; ask liking first (Lesson 13.3).

Mean liking values in points; the share is dimensionless; penalties are in points ✓
Worked example 9What should the next cricket-bread recipe change?

Forty consumers rated the 10 % cricket bread (mean liking 6.0) and answered JAR questions. Bitterness: 25 just about right (mean liking 6.84), 14 too bitter (4.57), 1 not bitter enough. Saltiness: 22 just about right (6.41), 16 not salty enough (5.50), 2 too salty. Compute the penalties and weighted penalties and recommend a change.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Too bitter: \(\Delta = 6.84 - 4.57 = 2.27\) points; share \(14/40 = 35\,\%\); weighted penalty \(0.35\times2.27 = 0.79\) points.
  2. Not salty enough: \(\Delta = 6.41 - 5.50 = 0.91\) points; share \(16/40 = 40\,\%\); weighted penalty \(0.40\times0.91 = 0.36\) points.
  3. Small groups (1 not bitter enough, 2 too salty) are below 20 % and not interpreted.
  4. Recommendation. Reducing bitterness has the largest expected pay-off — about 0.8 points of mean liking if the too-bitter group could be satisfied. Options: a milder cricket powder, less of it, or ingredients that mask bitterness; the salt increase is a secondary, cheaper step. Confirm with the next study — the penalty is a correlational estimate, not a guarantee.
Bitterness: penalty 2.27, weighted 0.79; saltiness: penalty 0.91, weighted 0.36 — reduce bitterness first

Before running a penalty analysis, look at the distribution of each JAR attribute. When most consumers answer “just about right”, the attribute is fine. When the answers are skewed to one side — as for bitterness in Worked example 9 — the direction of reformulation is clear. When they split into two groups on both sides of the centre, the panel contains consumers with opposite ideals, and moving the product towards the average would please neither group; this is again a sign of segments, and the average of the JAR scale would hide it completely. JAR scales therefore tell you where the product deviates from consumers' ideals, liking tells you whether the deviation matters, and only a new test of a reformulated product tells you whether your change actually helped.

How many panellists? Planning a student panel

Student panels of 20–40 are small by professional standards (Lesson 13.3, Table 3). That is acceptable if you plan for the effects you can realistically detect and say so. For the most common primary comparison — overall liking of two products rated by the same panellists — the calculation is the paired version of the sample-size formula of Eq. 3.1.8.

Panellists needed to detect a difference in mean liking (within-panellist design)(13.4.13)
\[ n \approx \left(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\right)^{2}\,\frac{\sigma_d^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}, \qquad \sigma_d^2 \approx 2\,\sigma_\varepsilon^2 \] \[ \text{MDD} = \bigl(t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1} + t_{1-\beta,\,n-1}\bigr)\frac{\sigma_d}{\sqrt n} \]

the number of panellists is the square of the sum of two normal quantiles times the variance of within-panellist differences divided by the square of the difference you want to detect. That variance is about twice the error variance of the block ANOVA. Turned around, a panel of n can detect a minimum difference MDD equal to the sum of two t-quantiles times the SD of differences divided by the square root of n.

Follow the derivation
  1. The paired t test uses \(\bar d/(s_d/\sqrt n)\). To have power \(1-\beta\) against a true difference \(\Delta\), the expected test statistic \(\Delta\sqrt n/\sigma_d\) must exceed the critical value by \(z_{1-\beta}\): \(\Delta\sqrt n/\sigma_d = z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}\).
  2. Solve for \(n\). Because the t distribution has heavier tails, replace the z values by \(t\) values with \(n-1\) df and iterate until \(n\) no longer changes (the live calculator does this).
  3. In the additive block model, \(d_i = (\tau_1 - \tau_2) + (\varepsilon_{i1} - \varepsilon_{i2})\), so \(\sigma_d^2 = 2\sigma_\varepsilon^2\): the panellist effect cancels, which is why within-panellist designs need far fewer people than comparisons of separate groups.
  4. Solving the same equation for \(\Delta\) gives the MDD for a given \(n\).
Assumptions and validity

Two products and a paired comparison; with more products and Tukey adjustment somewhat more panellists are needed. \(\sigma_d\) is the critical input: estimated from a small pilot it is itself uncertain, so plan with a conservative (larger) value. Hough et al. (2006) found that the residual error of consumer acceptability tests was similar across 108 studies in five countries, and that 112 consumers were needed to detect a difference of 10 % of the scale with α = 5 % and β = 10 %; Gacula and Rutenbeck (2006) found that differences of 0.6 points first became significant at about 40 consumers.

In a pilot with 8 classmates, the SD of the within-person differences between wheat and cricket bread was 1.6 points. You want 80 % power to detect a difference of 1.0 point in mean liking (α = 0.05, two-sided). (a) How many panellists do you need? (b) The pilot SD is uncertain; its one-sided 80 % upper confidence limit is 2.17 points. How many panellists would a cautious plan use? (c) What can 24 panellists detect if σd = 2.0?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Normal approximation: \(n = (1.960 + 0.842)^2\times(1.6/1.0)^2 = 7.849\times2.56 = 20.1\) → 21. Table 4 (discrimination tests) and Table 5 (hedonic comparisons) give the same message from two directions. With 20–40 panellists, a triangle test reliably detects only large differences (a proportion of discriminators of about 0.4–0.5, or \(d' \approx 2\)), a tetrad or 2-AFC test detects moderate differences (\(d' \approx 1\)–1.5), a hedonic comparison detects differences of about one point on the 9-point scale, and a CATA question typically reveals only words whose citation rates differ by something of the order of 30 percentage points (as for “nutty” and “earthy” in Figure 6). A similarity claim is essentially out of reach. None of this makes a student study pointless: a clear, large effect is detectable, and a well-documented null result with its minimum detectable difference is a useful piece of evidence for the next study.

A pilot is the best investment of an afternoon. Five to ten classmates tasting the real samples under the real protocol reveal practical problems (portions that are too small, codes that can be read through the cup, a ballot question nobody understands) and give a first estimate of \(\sigma_d\). Pilot data are used for planning only; they are not pooled with the main study, and they are never reported as results.

Getting more out of a small panel

Use within-panellist designs (every panellist tastes every product) and analyse them as blocks; use the most powerful discrimination method that fits the question (tetrad or 2-AFC rather than triangle); test few products; pool panels from two course groups if the protocol is identical; and choose the primary comparison in advance. What does not help: replicating the same panellist many times and treating replicates as new people.

Reporting a sensory study

A sensory result is only as credible as the description of how it was obtained. ISO 4120:2021 lists what a triangle-test report should contain — the objective, full identification of the samples (origin, preparation, quantity, storage, serving size and temperature), the number of assessors and correct responses, the statistical result with α, β and \(p_d\), the assessors' experience, age and gender, the information given to them, the test environment, the location, date and panel leader. Common mistakes in student sensory reports

(1) Analysing ratings with a one-way ANOVA or unpaired t-tests, ignoring that the same panellists rated all products. (2) Concluding “no difference” from a non-significant triangle test. (3) Using a one-sided test for a preference. (4) Reporting only p-values, without means, differences and confidence intervals. (5) Treating replicated evaluations as independent assessors. (6) Testing twenty CATA words and reporting the one that was significant. (7) Presenting pilot or simulated data as results.

Key terms

Williams design
A Latin-square serving design in which each sample appears once in each position and follows every other sample equally often (t sequences for even t, 2t for odd t).
First-order carry-over
The influence of the immediately preceding sample on the judgement of the next one.
Exact binomial test
A test of the number of correct answers against the guessing probability using the binomial distribution directly.
Critical number
The smallest number of correct answers that is significant at a chosen α for a given number of assessors.
Proportion of discriminators
\(p_d = (p_c - p_0)/(1 - p_0)\): the chance-corrected share of assessors assumed to perceive the difference; depends on the test method.
Psychometric function
The function that links the sensory difference d′ to the probability of a correct answer for a particular test method.
Thurstonian d′
The sensory difference in units of the standard deviation of perceptual noise, estimated by inverting the psychometric function; comparable across methods.
Power
The probability that a test detects a difference of a specified size; one minus the risk β of missing it.
Similarity test
A test with reversed hypotheses that shows that any difference is smaller than a pre-specified tolerable limit.
Two-sided test
A test in which deviations in either direction count as evidence, as in a paired preference test.
Randomised complete block design
A design in which every block (panellist) receives every treatment (product), analysed by a two-way ANOVA with products and blocks.
Tukey HSD
The smallest difference between two means that is significant when all pairs are compared with familywise error control.
Partial eta squared
\(SS_{\text{prod}}/(SS_{\text{prod}} + SS_E)\): the share of product-plus-error variation explained by products.
Cochran's Q
A test for differences in the proportion of 1s across several related binary variables, such as a CATA word across products rated by the same consumers.
Penalty analysis
Comparison of mean liking between JAR and non-JAR groups, weighted by group size, to prioritise reformulation.
