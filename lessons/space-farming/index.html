Farming in space and extreme environments

On 10 August 2015, three astronauts on the International Space Station ate a few leaves of red romaine lettuce that they had grown themselves — the first time NASA cleared its astronauts to eat a crop grown in space (Russian cosmonauts had eaten station-grown greens about a decade earlier). A handful of leaves is a long way from feeding a crew on the way to Mars, where every person needs about 0.8 kg of oxygen, 2.5 kg of water and 12.6 MJ of food energy every day, and resupply from Earth is months away. This lesson asks the engineering question behind every space greenhouse — how many square metres of crops, how many kilowatts of light and how much recycling does it take to keep one person alive? — and shows what half a century of closed-ecosystem experiments, from Siberia to Arizona and Antarctica, teaches us about food systems on Earth.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain what a bioregenerative life-support system (BLSS) is and how it differs from the physico-chemical life support of today's spacecraft (LO2, LO7).
  • Use NASA's baseline crew values to set up daily mass balances for oxygen, CO₂, water and food, and calculate the consumables of a mission (LO3).
  • Estimate the crop area and lighting power needed per crew member from crop productivity data, and explain why published estimates range from about 1 m² to more than 60 m² (LO3).
  • Describe Veggie, the Advanced Plant Habitat, MELiSSA, EDEN ISS, Lunar Palace 1 and Biosphere 2, and extract the main lesson each one taught (LO1, LO2, LO7).
  • Explain with the Bond number and capillary physics how microgravity changes water and air around plants, and how regolith chemistry and radiation constrain farming on the Moon and Mars (LO2, LO3).
  • Evaluate the mass case for growing food in space with the equivalent-system-mass method, and identify lessons and spin-offs for circular, resilient food systems on Earth (LO1, LO3, LO7).

Why grow plants in space?

Every spacecraft is a closed box that must supply its crew with oxygen, water and food and remove carbon dioxide, water vapour, heat and waste. On a short mission everything is simply carried up and thrown away. On the International Space Station (ISS) the physico-chemical life-support system recycles most of the water — since 2023 about 98 % of it, thanks to a brine processor that squeezes the last water out of the residue of urine processing (NASA, 2023) — makes oxygen by electrolysis of water, and scrubs CO₂ from the air. Food, however, still comes from Earth on cargo ships. For a mission to Mars, lasting two to three years with no resupply, the food becomes the largest item that cannot be recycled by chemistry alone.

Plants can do what no single machine does: in one process they take up CO₂ and release O₂, purify water — transpired vapour condenses as practically distilled water — and turn waste nutrients into food. A life-support system built around organisms is called a bioregenerative life-support system (BLSS). The idea is as old as spaceflight; since the 1950s algae, crops and closed ecosystems have been studied in the Soviet Union and Russia, the United States, Europe, Japan, Canada and China (Wheeler, 2017). In practice, spacecraft will combine both approaches: biological regeneration where it pays, physico-chemical machines as backup and for fast control.

Key idea

A life-support system is a set of mass balances that must close every day: oxygen, carbon dioxide, water, food (carbon and energy) and nutrients. Plants can close several balances at once, but only if their area, light and waste recycling are sized to the crew — and small imbalances accumulate relentlessly in a closed volume.

The second ratio links oxygen to energy. Every mole of O₂ that the body uses releases roughly the same amount of energy, whatever is being burned — which is why sports physiologists can measure energy expenditure from breathing alone.

How many square metres of crops keep one astronaut alive?

NASA grew wheat, soybean, lettuce, potato and other crops hydroponically for a decade in the sealed, multi-level Biomass Production Chamber at Kennedy Space Center from the late 1980s — one of the first demonstrations of vertical agriculture (Wheeler, 2017). Food is a different story. The crew eats only the edible part of the crop, but the whole plant — stalks, leaves and roots included — photosynthesises. So the area needed for food depends on the edible productivity and its energy content, and the light needed depends on how efficiently each crop turns photons into edible dry matter.

BVAD translates such calculations into diets. A diet of salad crops only — fresh vegetables to complement stored food — needs about 1.35 m² per crew member; adding carbohydrate crops (wheat, potato, sweet potato and others) raises this to 19.5 m²; a complete diet from all candidate crops needs about 65 m² per crew member, dominated by the low-yielding soybean and peanut that supply protein and fat (Anderson et al., 2018, Table 4-99). Planners also add 25–50 % of the canopy area for crew access unless the chamber is heavily automated (Anderson et al., 2018) — the aisle problem of Eq. 7.2.1 again.

Growing plants in orbit: Veggie, the Advanced Plant Habitat and microgravity

NASA's Veggie vegetable-production system, installed on the ISS in 2014, is deliberately simple: a unit about the size of a carry-on suitcase with a bank of red, blue and green LEDs (which make it glow magenta) above typically six "plant pillows" — fabric bags filled with a clay-based growth medium and fertiliser, in which the seeds are planted (NASA, n.d.). The first validation test, VEG-01, grew 'Outredgeous' red romaine lettuce and 'Profusion' zinnias. It revealed the central difficulty of spaceflight horticulture: water. The lettuce suffered from too little water and the zinnias from too much, which led to fungal growth, until crew members watered the pillows directly by hand (Massa et al., 2017). The second lettuce crop was harvested and partly eaten on 10 August 2015. Later microbiological and nutritional analyses of ISS-grown lettuce found no pathogenic contamination and concluded that leafy crops can supply safe, fresh food to supplement astronauts' diets (Khodadad et al., 2020). Three kinds of lettuce, Chinese cabbage, mizuna mustard, red Russian kale and zinnias have since been grown in Veggie (NASA, n.d.).

The Advanced Plant Habitat (APH) is Veggie's opposite: a fully enclosed, automated growth chamber for research, with more than 180 sensors reporting temperature, oxygen and moisture to the ground (NASA, n.d.). It was delivered to the ISS in 2017; its first test grew Arabidopsis thaliana and dwarf wheat in 2018. The APH has a growth area of 0.2 m², LEDs in blue, green, red, far-red and white that reach up to 1000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, fans that force air across the canopy, and a root zone in which porous ceramic tubes embedded in arcillite (a calcined clay) keep the substrate moist under a controlled suction. Its measurements showed that the daily canopy photosynthesis of dwarf wheat in microgravity matched that of wheat grown on the ground at 500 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — photosynthesis itself works normally in space if air and water are managed (Monje et al., 2020). In 2021 the Plant Habitat-04 experiment grew the first chile peppers on the station (NASA, n.d.).

Why is air and water management so hard? Two gravity-driven processes that we never think about on Earth disappear in orbit:

  • Buoyant convection. On Earth, air warmed by a leaf rises and is replaced; humid, CO₂-depleted air is continuously swept away. In microgravity warm air does not rise, so a stagnant boundary layer builds up around leaves and roots unless fans force the air (Porterfield, 2002; Monje et al., 2020). Every space plant chamber therefore has a ventilation system — an echo of the 0.3–0.5 m s⁻¹ air speed of plant factories (Lesson 7.2).
  • Drainage. On Earth, excess water drains downward and air enters the pores of a substrate; roots get both water and oxygen. In microgravity, surface tension controls where water goes: it spreads along surfaces, bridges pores and forms blobs, and air bubbles do not rise out of the root zone. Roots can drown in one place and dry out in another. Aeroponic mist, which relies on droplets settling onto roots, stops working (Lesson 7.1, Eq. 7.1.3 with \(g\approx0\)).

A single dimensionless number tells us when surface tension beats gravity.

Capillary length, Bond number and capillary rise(7.3.5)
\[ \ell_c = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho\,g}},\qquad \text{Bo} = \frac{\rho\,g\,L^{2}}{\sigma} = \left(\frac{L}{\ell_c}\right)^{2},\qquad h_{\text{cap}} = \frac{2\,\sigma\cos\theta}{\rho\,g\,r} \]

the capillary length is the square root of the surface tension divided by density times gravitational acceleration; the Bond number compares gravity with surface tension for a body of liquid of size L and equals the square of L divided by the capillary length; the height to which water rises in a pore of radius r is twice the surface tension times the cosine of the contact angle, divided by density, gravity and pore radius (Jurin's law).

Follow the derivation
  1. Two pressures. The hydrostatic pressure difference over a height \(L\) is \(\rho gL\). The capillary (Laplace) pressure across a curved surface of radius \(L\) scales as \(\sigma/L\).
  2. Their ratio is \(\rho gL/(\sigma/L) = \rho gL^2/\sigma\) — the Bond number. When Bo ≫ 1, gravity shapes the liquid (drainage, flat puddles); when Bo ≪ 1, surface tension does (spherical drops, films, wicking).
  3. The capillary length is the size at which Bo = 1: \(L = \sqrt{\sigma/(\rho g)}\).
  4. Jurin's law. In a tube of radius \(r\) the surface tension pulls upward with force \(2\pi r\sigma\cos\theta\) around the perimeter; the column of height \(h\) weighs \(\pi r^2h\rho g\). Setting them equal gives \(h = 2\sigma\cos\theta/(\rho gr)\).
  5. Consequence. Every length scales as \(1/g\) or \(1/\sqrt{g}\): reducing gravity a millionfold multiplies the capillary length by a thousand and the capillary rise by a million.
Assumptions and validity

Static equilibrium with clean, uniform pores. Real substrates have a distribution of pore sizes, contact-angle hysteresis and swelling. The residual acceleration on the ISS varies with location and activity; 10⁻⁶ g is an order-of-magnitude assumption.

\(\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{N m}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-3}\,\text{m s}^{-2}}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{\text{kg s}^{-2}}{\text{kg m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-2}}} = \text{m}\) ✓; \(\dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-3}\,\text{m s}^{-2}\,\text{m}^2}{\text{kg s}^{-2}} = 1\) ✓
Worked example 4Water in a root module on Earth, Mars, the Moon and the ISS

A root module is filled with a clay substrate whose pores have radii around 50 µm (contact angle ≈ 0). Calculate the capillary length, the Bond number of a 1 cm water droplet and the capillary rise in the pores on Earth (9.81 m s⁻²), Mars (3.71), the Moon (1.62) and the ISS (assume 10⁻⁶ g = 9.81 × 10⁻⁶ m s⁻²). What do the numbers mean for irrigation design?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Capillary length \(\sqrt{0.072/(1000g)}\): Earth 2.7 mm; Mars 4.4 mm; Moon 6.7 mm; ISS \(\sqrt{0.072/0.00981} = 2.7\) m.
  2. Bond number of a 1 cm drop \(1000\,g\,(0.01)^2/0.072\): Earth 13.6 (gravity wins: the drop flattens and drains); Mars 5.2; Moon 2.2; ISS \(1.4\times10^{-5}\) (surface tension wins completely: the drop is a sphere or a film).
  3. Capillary rise in 50 µm pores \(2\times0.072/(1000\,g\,50\times10^{-6})\): Earth 0.29 m; Mars 0.78 m; Moon 1.8 m; ISS ≈ 300 km — in practice, unlimited.
  4. Interpretation. On Earth a 10 cm deep substrate drains its largest pores and is aerated at the top; on Mars and the Moon water is held higher, so substrates stay wetter and less aerated; in orbit the whole substrate wicks and holds water wherever it is, and there is no "drainage". Designers therefore control water by suction through porous tubes (as in the APH), not by gravity, and must actively keep air-filled porosity for root oxygen.
ℓc: 2.7 mm (Earth), 4.4 mm (Mars), 6.7 mm (Moon), ≈ 2.7 m (ISS); capillary rise in 50 µm pores 0.29, 0.78, 1.8 m and ≈ unlimited

BIOS-3: algae and wheat in Siberia

At the Institute of Biophysics in Krasnoyarsk, the BIOS-3 facility sealed small crews for months together with algae cultivators and phytotrons of wheat and vegetables that regenerated their air and part of their food. It showed early that plants could carry the gas exchange of people for long periods — and that food and waste closure are much harder than air (Wheeler, 2017).

Biosphere 2: the missing oxygen

From September 1991 to September 1993 eight people lived sealed inside Biosphere 2, a 1.27-hectare glass-and-steel complex in the Arizona desert that contained several wild biomes and an intensive agricultural area; the crew grew most of its own food. The air did not behave as planned. The O₂ fraction fell steadily from 20.9 % to below 15 % within about a year and a half — oxygen comparable to that at about 4000 m altitude — and pure oxygen had to be injected in 1993. The oxygen had not leaked out. Severinghaus et al. (1994) showed that microbes respiring the deliberately organic-rich soils had turned O₂ into CO₂, and that much of that CO₂ had been absorbed by the exposed concrete of the structure, which carbonated to calcium carbonate. Because the concrete hid the CO₂, the loss of oxygen went unnoticed for months. The lesson is universal: in a closed system every store and every sink of carbon and oxygen matters, including those nobody put on the drawing.

Lunar Palace 1: closing the loop in Beijing

At Beihang University in Beijing, the bioregenerative facility "Lunar Palace 1" (Yuegong-1) combines plant cabins, a living cabin and waste treatment. In 2014 three volunteers lived in it for 105 days (Fu et al., 2016). In 2017–2018 eight volunteers in two groups completed a 370-day experiment: group I lived inside for 60 days, group II for 200 days and group I again for 105 days. The team reported a material closure of 98.2 %, crop production that fully met the crew's plant-food requirements, O₂ and CO₂ held in the optimal ranges, and recovery of 99.7 % of urine and 67 % of solid waste (Fu et al., 2021; this study was posted as a preprint and should be read with that in mind). Lunar Palace shows that very high closure is possible for a year — and that the last per cent of solid waste is the hardest.

EDEN ISS: fresh food in Antarctica

The EDEN ISS project, led by the German Aerospace Center (DLR), placed a container greenhouse about 400 m from the German Neumayer Station III in Antarctica. In its nominal operation from 7 February to 20 November 2018 (286 days) it produced more than 268 kg of edible biomass on 12.5 m² — 67 kg of cucumbers, 56 kg of lettuce, 49 kg of leafy greens, 50 kg of tomatoes and smaller amounts of kohlrabi, herbs and radish — for the station's ten-person winter crew. The system combined aeroponics and nutrient film technique with 42 fluid-cooled LED fixtures delivering 330–600 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ depending on the crop; its average productivity was 0.075 kg m⁻² d⁻¹ (Zabel et al., 2020). For a winter crew cut off for months, 268 kg of fresh vegetables is a large gain in diet and morale, even though it is a small fraction of the food they eat.

How quickly does a closed habitat get into trouble if its plants stop working? The answer follows from the ideal-gas law.

CO₂ build-up in a sealed habitat(7.3.6)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{\mathrm{d}p_{\text{CO}_2}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{R\,T}{V}\left(N_{\text{crew}}\,\dot n_{\text{CO}_2} - A\,\dot u_{\text{CO}_2}\right) \\[6pt] t_{\text{lim}} = \frac{(p_{\text{lim}} - p_{0})\,V}{R\,T\,\left(N_{\text{crew}}\,\dot n_{\text{CO}_2} - A\,\dot u_{\text{CO}_2}\right)} \end{gathered} \]

the partial pressure of CO₂ in a closed volume rises at a rate equal to the gas constant times the absolute temperature divided by the volume, times the net CO₂ production — what the crew exhales minus what the plants take up. If the net production is positive, the time to reach a limit is the pressure margin times the volume, divided by RT times the net production.

Worked example 5The plant chamber fails: how much time does the crew have?

Four crew members live in a habitat with 200 m³ of free volume at 101.3 kPa and 22 °C. The CO₂ partial pressure is 0.10 kPa and O₂ is 21 %. The plant chamber and the CO₂ scrubbers fail at the same time. How long until the CO₂ reaches 1.0 kPa, and how long until O₂ falls from 21 % to 18 %?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Moles of air: \(pV/(RT) = 101\,325\times200/(8.314\times295.15) = 8260\) mol.
  2. CO₂ production: \(4\times23.6 = 94.5\) mol d⁻¹ → mole fraction rises by \(94.5/8260 = 1.14\,\%\) per day, i.e. \(1.16\) kPa d⁻¹.
  3. Time to 1.0 kPa: \((1.0-0.1)/1.16 = 0.78\) d ≈ 19 h.
  4. O₂ consumption: \(4\times25.5 = 102\) mol d⁻¹ → −1.24 percentage points per day; 21 % → 18 % takes 3/1.24 = 2.4 days.
  5. Interpretation: the crew runs into CO₂ trouble in less than a day, three times sooner than into oxygen trouble. Plants are a slow buffer; fast CO₂ scrubbing must be available as a physico-chemical backup.

History

Biosphere 2 is often remembered as a failure. Scientifically, it was the opposite: an expensive, unrepeatable demonstration that an ecosystem sealed from its planet drifts in directions no model predicted — through soils, concrete, insects, microbes and people. Every later BLSS, from Lunar Palace to MELiSSA, compartmentalises the loop precisely so that each flow can be measured and controlled.

Closing the loop: MELiSSA and the recycling of wastes

Plants cannot use faeces, urine or inedible straw directly. Between the crew and the crops there must be an ecosystem of microbes that breaks wastes down into CO₂, water and mineral nutrients — the job that soil organisms do on Earth. The European Space Agency's MELiSSA (Micro-Ecological Life Support System Alternative), started in 1989, engineers this ecosystem as a chain of separate, well-controlled bioreactors, each with one main function (ESA, n.d.; Hendrickx et al., 2006). Its pilot plant at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona has operated since 2009.

Why so many compartments? Because each organism group needs different conditions (anaerobic and hot, lit, aerobic in a biofilm), and because separate compartments can be monitored and controlled — the opposite of Biosphere 2's single, opaque ecosystem. The nutrient stakes are large: projections based on NASA's hydroponic data suggest 90–100 kg of fertiliser salts per person per year for a full crop-based diet, of which more than half could be recovered from inedible biomass, and processed urine could close the loop further (Anderson et al., 2018). The inedible biomass itself is substantial: 4.3 kg (fresh weight) per crew member per day for a diet of salad and carbohydrate crops, 6.7 kg for a diet from all candidate crops (Anderson et al., 2018, Table 4-99).

Degree of closure and resupply mass(7.3.7)

the degree of closure is one minus the ratio of the consumables that must be imported to the total consumables the crew uses; the mass to be imported over a mission equals the unclosed fraction times the daily demand per person times the crew size times the mission duration in days.

Carbon is the subtlest loop. The crew exhales the carbon of the food it eats; the plants must fix that carbon plus the carbon of their own inedible parts; the waste processor returns the inedible carbon as CO₂ — but only if it oxidises it completely, and oxidising it consumes O₂. Figure 10 follows the carbon (expressed as kg of CO₂ per crew member per day) through one pass of the loop.

Why O₂ and CO₂ never balance by themselves

Photosynthesis building carbohydrate releases one O₂ per CO₂ fixed (PQ ≈ 1.0); a crew on a mixed diet uses more O₂ than it exhales CO₂ (RQ ≈ 0.93); microbes oxidising straw return about one CO₂ per O₂. The net O₂ balance therefore depends on how much organic carbon is being stored or released anywhere in the system. Biosphere 2 released carbon from its soils (negative storage) and lost O₂; a BLSS that composts too little accumulates O₂ and runs out of CO₂. Controlling the waste-oxidation step is the designer's lever.

Farming on the Moon and Mars: light, regolith, radiation and mass

Light. Mars receives only 43 % of the solar radiation that reaches Earth, and global dust storms can dim it for weeks, so NASA's baseline assumes electric lighting; yet some Martian latitudes receive up to 30 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ of photosynthetic photons for much of the year, nearly half of the brightest places on Earth, so future greenhouses might use sunlight supplemented by lamps (Anderson et al., 2018). The Moon has a different problem: at most latitudes the lunar day and night each last about two weeks, so plants would need either stored energy for a fortnight of lighting or a site near the poles.

Regolith. Lunar and Martian "soil" is crushed rock with no organic matter and no nitrogen. In 2008 NASA's Phoenix lander leached Martian soil in water and found a moderately alkaline solution (pH 7.7 ± 0.5) containing 0.4–0.6 % perchlorate (ClO₄⁻) by mass (Hecht et al., 2009) — a salt that is toxic to people, because it interferes with the thyroid, and would have to be removed before regolith could be used as a growing medium. Experiments with simulants suggest that plants can germinate and grow in Mars- and Moon-like mineral mixtures for weeks without added nutrients (Wamelink et al., 2014), but simulants lack perchlorate and many properties of real regolith. When Arabidopsis was grown in real lunar samples from Apollo 11, 12 and 17, it germinated and grew, but slowly, with severe stress symptoms and gene expression resembling responses to salt, metals and reactive oxygen (Paul et al., 2022). The likely path is hydroponics first, with regolith used as a substrate only after treatment.

Radiation. Outside Earth's magnetic field, galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events give a continuous dose. Measurements inside the Mars Science Laboratory spacecraft imply a dose equivalent of 0.66 ± 0.12 Sv for the shortest possible round trip to Mars with current propulsion and comparable shielding (Zeitlin et al., 2013), and on the Martian surface the Curiosity rover measured a dose-equivalent rate of about 0.64 mSv per day (Hassler et al., 2014). Water and plant biomass are good shielding materials. NASA's planning distinguishes shielded habitable volume from much cheaper unshielded volume, which suits equipment that is insensitive to radiation and needs little crew interaction (Anderson et al., 2018) — whether plant chambers qualify depends on how much tending they need and on the radiation tolerance of the crops and their seed stocks.

Atmosphere and pressure. Plants and people prefer different air. Crops grow best at CO₂ partial pressures of about 0.10–0.20 kPa and relative humidity near 75 %, and some, such as wheat and potato, prefer temperatures below the crew's comfort zone; productivity falls again at CO₂ levels that are still within the normal range for crews (up to about 1 kPa). At ordinary CO₂, plants even grow better at reduced O₂ partial pressure (Anderson et al., 2018). Separate plant chambers, possibly at lower total pressure — studied in Canadian hypobaric chambers (Wheeler, 2017) — can therefore make sense, and on Mars the thin atmosphere, mostly CO₂, is a potential carbon source for the crops.

Mass. Everything a greenhouse needs — structure, lamps, pumps, power, heat rejection, crew time — has to be launched or built, and the question is whether that costs more than launching food. NASA answers it with the equivalent system mass (ESM), which converts volume, power, cooling and crew time into the launch mass of the infrastructure that provides them (Levri et al., 2003; Anderson et al., 2018).

Equivalent system mass(7.3.8)
\[ \text{ESM} = M + V\,V_{\text{eq}} + P\,P_{\text{eq}} + C\,C_{\text{eq}} + CT\cdot D\cdot CT_{\text{eq}} \]

the equivalent system mass equals the hardware mass, plus the pressurised volume times the mass of structure per unit volume, plus the power times the mass of power plant per kilowatt, plus the cooling load times the mass of heat-rejection equipment per kilowatt, plus the crew time per year times the mission duration times the mass equivalent of an hour of crew time.

What closed loops teach us on Earth

Space agriculture has always been a laboratory for Earth. Several technologies that you have met in this course came out of it. The first use of light-emitting diodes for growing crops came from space-agriculture research (Wheeler, 2017): Bula et al. (1991) demonstrated that red LEDs, supplemented with a little blue light, could support plant growth — the starting point of the LED-lit plant factories of Lesson 7.2. NASA's Biomass Production Chamber was one of the first demonstrations of vertical, multi-level agriculture, and pioneered the hydroponic production of tuber crops such as potato and sweet potato (Wheeler, 2017). NASA-funded aeroponics research, including an experiment on the Mir station in 1997, fed into commercial aeroponic systems (NASA Spinoff, 2006; Lesson 7.1). The ISS water processor — now recovering 98 % of the station's water (NASA, 2023) — is a working example of the circular water systems that dry regions on Earth need.

Beyond technologies, closed ecosystems teach three habits of thought that apply to every food system:

  1. Close the balances, then look for the missing term. Biosphere 2's oxygen and the CO₂-use efficiency of a plant factory (Eq. 7.2.8) are the same accounting exercise. Whatever is not measured — soil carbon, concrete, leaks — eventually dominates.
  2. Buffers and redundancy buy time. Worked example 5 gave a crew 19 hours before CO₂ became dangerous; Lesson 7.1 gave an aeroponic crop tens of minutes before its roots dried. Resilient systems keep reserves and backups sized to the time needed to repair a failure (Lesson 15.2).
  3. Recycling costs energy, area and attention. Full closure is possible — Lunar Palace reached 98 % — but each additional per cent costs equipment, electricity and crew time. On Earth, the same trade-off governs nutrient recovery from wastewater and manure.

Extreme environments on Earth are also markets in their own right. Research and military stations in Antarctica, mines and villages in the Arctic, ships and remote islands pay a high price for fresh food, and systems like EDEN ISS show how a container-sized, LED-lit greenhouse can deliver a steady supply through a polar winter (Zabel et al., 2020). For a Nordic reader, the parallel with Kiruna in the modelling of Graamans et al. (2018) is obvious: the darker and colder the place, the smaller the energy penalty of closing the greenhouse (Lesson 7.2).

Frontier 2024–2026

Open questions for space and polar agriculture include crops bred for closed systems (dwarf plants with a high harvest index and short cycles), the management of the plant and root microbiome in sealed systems, the treatment of regolith and the use of in-situ resources such as Martian CO₂ and water ice, autonomous control that reduces crew time (the dominant long-term ESM term), and food processing that turns a few staple crops into a varied diet. Because the same questions matter for vertical farms, remote communities and circular cities on Earth, the research communities increasingly overlap.

Key terms

Bioregenerative life-support system (BLSS)
A life-support system in which organisms — plants, algae, microbes — regenerate air, water and food for a crew.
Physico-chemical life support
Life support by machines and chemistry: CO₂ scrubbers, electrolysis for O₂, distillation and filtration for water.
Crew member-day (CM-d)
The needs or outputs of one crew member for one day; the unit of NASA's baseline values.
Respiratory quotient (RQ)
Moles of CO₂ produced per mole of O₂ consumed by an organism; 0.7 for fat, 1.0 for carbohydrate, 0.93 for the nominal crew diet.
Photosynthetic quotient (PQ)
Moles of O₂ released per mole of CO₂ fixed by photosynthesis; about 1.0 for carbohydrate-rich crops.
Harvest index
Edible biomass divided by total biomass of a crop; it sets how much inedible material must be recycled.
Edible photon-use efficiency
Edible dry matter produced per mole of photons; determines the lighting power needed to feed a crew.
Capillary length
The size below which surface tension dominates gravity in a liquid, \(\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho g)}\): 2.7 mm on Earth, metres in orbit.
Bond number
The ratio of gravitational to surface-tension forces, \(\rho g L^2/\sigma\).
Degree of closure
The fraction of a crew's consumables regenerated inside the system rather than imported.
MELiSSA
ESA's Micro-Ecological Life Support System Alternative: a five-compartment loop of bioreactors, plants and crew.
Equivalent system mass (ESM)
Hardware mass plus the mass equivalents of the volume, power, cooling and crew time a system requires.
Perchlorate
The ClO₄⁻ anion, found at 0.4–0.6 % by mass in Martian soil; toxic to humans and a barrier to using regolith for crops.
