CO₂ enrichment and ventilation

Every carbon atom in a lettuce leaf or a tomato was once a molecule of carbon dioxide in the air around the plant. Outdoors that air now holds about 427 CO₂ molecules per million, but a closed greenhouse full of a healthy crop on a sunny morning can strip out most of them within an hour, and photosynthesis slows accordingly. Growers therefore add CO₂ — and then open the vents to get rid of heat, blowing much of the expensive gas back outside. In this lesson you will write down the CO₂ mass balance that governs this tug of war, find out when enrichment pays and when it is wasted, compare the sources of CO₂ from gas boilers to direct air capture, and learn the limits that protect the people who work in enriched air.

  • Compare CO₂ sources — flue gas, industrial and liquid CO₂, biogas upgrading and direct air capture — for purity, coupling with heat, cost and carbon footprint (LO7).
  • Apply occupational exposure limits (Swedish AFS 2023:14) and design CO₂ dosing that is safe for workers (LO2, LO7).

Carbon: the nutrient that arrives through the air

About 40 % of the dry matter of a plant is carbon, and all of it enters through the stomata as CO₂. Nitrogen, potassium or phosphorus can be dosed with a pump into the nutrient solution, but carbon has to be dissolved in the air, where it is present at a mole fraction of only 0.04 %. For C3 crops — tomato, cucumber, sweet pepper, lettuce, herbs, strawberry, roses and almost everything else grown in greenhouses and plant factories — this is too little: the carboxylating enzyme Rubisco is far from saturated at today's concentration, and roughly one reaction in four is wasted on oxygen instead of CO₂ (photorespiration; Lesson 2.1). Raising the CO₂ concentration therefore works like a fertiliser, which is why horticulturists call it CO₂ fertilisation or CO₂ enrichment.

In the open field the atmosphere is an infinite, well-stirred reservoir and nobody can change the CO₂ around the crop. In a greenhouse or a plant factory the situation is reversed. The air volume above each square metre is small (a few cubic metres), the crop takes up CO₂ quickly, and the only way new CO₂ can arrive is with outside air through vents and cracks — or from a dosing system. The building turns CO₂ from a fixed environmental condition into a controlled variable, with a mass balance, a set-point, a cost and a carbon footprint, exactly like the heat and water vapour of Lesson 8.1 and Lesson 8.2. The central conflict of this lesson is between two uses of ventilation: vents must open to remove heat and humidity, but every cubic metre of air that leaves carries away the CO₂ you paid for.

Big idea

CO₂ enrichment is a mass balance: supply = crop uptake + ventilation loss + change in storage. The ventilation loss grows in proportion to the air exchange rate and to how far the inside concentration is raised above the outside. When vents are closed, most of the supplied CO₂ ends up in the crop; when they are wide open, most of it is blown outside. Good dosing strategies follow from this one equation.

Counting CO₂: ppm, µmol mol⁻¹ and mg m⁻³

Greenhouse CO₂ sensors display "ppm", plant physiologists write µmol mol⁻¹, and a gas supplier invoices kilograms. To connect them we need two facts from chemistry. First, in a mixture of ideal gases the volume fraction of a component equals its mole fraction, so "parts per million by volume" (ppmv, the "ppm" of gas analysis) is the same as µmol of CO₂ per mol of air. Second, the ideal-gas law tells us how many moles of air occupy a cubic metre. Multiplying by the molar mass of CO₂ turns moles into grams.

From mole fraction to mass concentration (ideal-gas law)(8.3.1)
\[ \begin{gathered} \rho_{\text{CO}_2} = x\,\frac{M_{\text{CO}_2}\,P}{R\,T} = k\,x \\[4pt] k = \frac{M_{\text{CO}_2}\,P}{R\,T}\times10^{-6} \approx 1.83\ \frac{\text{mg m}^{-3}}{\mu\text{mol mol}^{-1}}\quad (20^{\circ}\text{C},\ 101.3\ \text{kPa}) \end{gathered} \]

the mass of CO₂ per cubic metre of air equals its mole fraction times the molar mass of CO₂ times the pressure, divided by the gas constant times the absolute temperature. At room conditions every ppm of CO₂ is 1.83 milligrams per cubic metre; 1000 ppm is 1.83 grams per cubic metre.

  3. Multiply by the molar mass: \(\rho = x\,M P/(RT)\). With \(x\) in µmol mol⁻¹ we must multiply by 10⁻⁶: \(41.57\ \text{mol m}^{-3}\times44.01\ \text{g mol}^{-1}\times10^{-6} = 1.830\times10^{-3}\) g m⁻³ per ppm = 1.83 mg m⁻³ per ppm.

How much does extra CO₂ help? Crop responses

The yield benefit of CO₂ has been measured thousands of times, and a few syntheses frame what we know. Kimball (1983) collected more than 430 observations of the yield of 37 species grown with CO₂ enrichment, mostly in greenhouses and growth chambers, and concluded that doubling the CO₂ concentration increases yield by about 33 % (99.9 % confidence interval 24–43 %). Free-air CO₂ enrichment (FACE) experiments, which fumigate crops in the open field without walls, give more modest numbers: across 186 studies of 18 C3 crops, raising the concentration by about 200 ppm increased yield by about 18 % under non-stressed conditions, but only by about 10 % under nitrogen deficiency or with about 2 °C of warming (Ainsworth & Long, 2021). For greenhouse horticulture, enrichment to 600–1000 ppm is reported to increase yields by 20–30 % (van Tuyll et al., 2022, citing Nederhoff, 1994), with 800–1000 µmol mol⁻¹ commonly regarded as the optimal range for greenhouse crops (Wang et al., 2022). For lettuce in a plant factory, Chen et al. (2021) found yield increases of 34 % at 800 µmol mol⁻¹ and 44 % at 1600 µmol mol⁻¹ compared with ambient CO₂, and a 29–41 % increase in light-use efficiency.

History: a moving baseline

Growers and researchers have enriched greenhouse air with CO₂ for about a century — Kimball's (1983) assemblage already drew on more than 70 reports published over the preceding 64 years. Meanwhile the "ambient" reference has moved. The continuous record at Mauna Loa, begun by Charles David Keeling of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography in March 1958, shows an annual mean of 316 ppm in 1959 and 427 ppm in 2025 (NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, 2026): outdoor air now contains 35 % more CO₂ than when many of the classic enrichment experiments were made. Always read an enrichment effect together with the reference concentration it was measured against.

All these data show diminishing returns: the first extra 100 ppm above ambient is worth much more than the tenth. A simple empirical curve captures this and will be useful for the engineering calculations later in the lesson.

Empirical CO₂ response of canopy photosynthesis and yield(8.3.2)
\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{Y(C)}{Y(C_{\text{ref}})} = \frac{f(C)}{f(C_{\text{ref}})}, \qquad f(C) = \frac{C-\Gamma}{C-\Gamma+K} \\[4pt] \Gamma \approx 50\ \mu\text{mol mol}^{-1}, \qquad K \approx 237\ \mu\text{mol mol}^{-1} \end{gathered} \]

the yield at concentration C, relative to the yield at a reference concentration, equals the ratio of a saturating response function evaluated at the two concentrations. The function is zero at the compensation point Γ and approaches one at very high CO₂; K is the extra concentration above Γ at which half of the maximum response is reached.

Follow the derivation (and the calibration)
  1. Biochemistry (the Farquhar model of Lesson 2.1) shows that Rubisco-limited photosynthesis rises with CO₂ in a Michaelis–Menten fashion above a compensation point. At canopy scale the details blur, and a rectangular hyperbola in the excess concentration \(C - \Gamma\) is a convenient summary: \(A = A_s\,(C-\Gamma)/(C-\Gamma+K)\), where \(A_s\) is the (light-dependent) rate at CO₂ saturation.
  2. If yield is proportional to accumulated canopy photosynthesis, the unknown \(A_s\) cancels in the ratio \(Y(C)/Y(C_{\text{ref}})\).
  3. Calibrate \(K\) with Kimball's result: \(f(660)/f(330) = 1.33\). Write it out: \(\dfrac{610\,(280+K)}{280\,(610+K)} = 1.33\) → \(170\,800 + 610K = 227\,164 + 372.4K\) → \(K = 237\) µmol mol⁻¹.
  4. Test it against independent data: from 380 to 580 ppm the curve predicts +19 % (FACE: +18 %); from 400 to 1600 ppm it predicts +46 % (lettuce, Chen et al., 2021: +44 %). From 427 to 800 and 1000 ppm it gives +24 % and +30 % — inside the 20–30 % reported for greenhouse vegetables.
Assumptions and validity

An empirical, average curve for C3 crops with enough light, water and nutrients. It ignores photosynthetic acclimation (a partial down-regulation of Rubisco during long exposure; Ainsworth & Rogers, 2007), species differences, sink limitation and quality effects, and it should not be extrapolated above about 1200–1500 ppm. C4 crops (maize, sorghum) respond little because their CO₂-concentrating mechanism already saturates Rubisco (Ainsworth & Long, 2021).

Interaction with light and temperature

Why is the response not a fixed number? The biochemistry of Lesson 2.1 gives the answer. At ambient CO₂ and high light a C3 leaf is limited by Rubisco carboxylation (\(A_c\)); extra CO₂ relieves exactly that limitation and also suppresses photorespiration. At low light the leaf is limited by electron transport (\(A_j\)); extra CO₂ still helps, because less of the light energy is wasted in photorespiration, but less. Temperature adds a second interaction: the specificity of Rubisco for CO₂ over O₂ falls as leaves warm, so photorespiration grows with temperature and the relative benefit of enrichment is largest in warm leaves. Frantz et al. (2004) found that elevated CO₂ raised the temperature optimum of lettuce growth from about 25 to 30 °C. The interactive Figure 2 lets you explore both effects with the same Farquhar–von Caemmerer–Berry model and parameters as Lesson 2.1.

Three further effects matter for the grower. First, stomata partly close at elevated CO₂: in FACE experiments stomatal conductance fell by 22 % on average while light-saturated photosynthesis rose by 31 % (Ainsworth & Rogers, 2007). Transpiration per unit leaf area therefore falls, which saves water and reduces the latent load of a plant factory (Lesson 8.2), but also slows the flow of calcium to young leaves — elevated CO₂ plus strong light is a classic recipe for tipburn in lettuce. Second, quality changes: sugars generally increase, whereas zinc, iron and protein concentrations tend to decrease in many crops (Ainsworth & Long, 2021; Wang et al., 2022). Third, the benefit shrinks when something else limits growth: Ainsworth and Long (2021) found that nitrogen deficiency almost halved the FACE response. Enrichment belongs in well-fed, well-lit, well-watered crops.

Why CO₂ and light belong together

Lesson 2.1 concluded that "CO₂ makes light more valuable, and light makes CO₂ more valuable". In engineering terms: the marginal value of a photon rises with the CO₂ concentration, and the marginal value of a ppm of CO₂ rises with the light level. That is why greenhouse climate computers are commonly set to raise the CO₂ set-point with solar radiation, and why plant factories, where every photon is paid for with electricity, almost always enrich.

The CO₂ mass balance of a greenhouse

Draw a control volume around the air of the greenhouse, as in Lesson 8.1, and count everything that crosses its boundary (Figure 3). CO₂ enters with outside air, from the dosing system and from respiration — of the crop at night, of the substrate and soil, and of people working inside. It leaves with the air that is exhausted through vents and cracks and by photosynthesis. The difference is stored in the air, changing its concentration.

Dynamic CO₂ mass balance of a ventilated building(8.3.3)
\[ \begin{gathered} \underbrace{k\,\bar h\,\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}t}}_{\text{storage}} = \underbrace{\Phi_{\text{inj}}}_{\text{supply}} + \underbrace{\Phi_{\text{resp}}}_{\text{respiration}} - \underbrace{\Phi_{\text{up}}}_{\text{photosynthesis}} - \underbrace{k\,G\,(C - C_{\text{out}})}_{\text{ventilation}} \\[4pt] G = N\,\bar h \end{gathered} \]

the rate at which the CO₂ stored in the air above each square metre of floor changes equals the supply plus the respiration minus the crop's uptake minus the net CO₂ carried out by ventilation. The ventilation term is the air flow times the difference between the inside and outside concentration, converted to mass with the factor k of Eq. 8.3.1.

Follow the derivation
  1. Conservation of mass for CO₂ in the control volume: rate of change of the mass stored = mass flows in − mass flows out + sources − sinks.
  2. Mass stored per m² of floor: volume per floor area \(\bar h\) (m³ m⁻²) times mass concentration \(kC\) (g m⁻³): \(m = k\bar hC\). Its rate of change is \(k\bar h\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t\) (with \(k\) and \(\bar h\) constant).
  3. Ventilation replaces a volume \(G = N\bar h\) of inside air per m² per hour. The air that enters brings \(kGC_{\text{out}}\) of CO₂; the air that leaves takes \(kGC\) (the leaving air has the well-mixed inside concentration). Net loss: \(kG(C - C_{\text{out}})\) — negative, i.e. a gain, when the inside is below the outside.
  4. Add the dosing system (\(\Phi_{\text{inj}}\)), respiration sources (\(\Phi_{\text{resp}}\); Kozai (2013) uses about 0.05 kg h⁻¹ per worker) and subtract the crop's net photosynthesis (\(\Phi_{\text{up}}\)). The result is Eq. 8.3.3.
  5. Kozai's (2013) expression for the CO₂ lost from a plant factory, \(C_L = k_C\,N\,V_A\,(C_{\text{in}} - C_{\text{out}})\), is exactly our ventilation term for a whole room of volume \(V_A\).
Assumptions and validity

Well-mixed air (in reality CO₂ is lowest inside a dense canopy and highest near the dosing tubes); the same volume flow enters and leaves; \(k\) constant (temperature swings of ±5 K change it by ±1.7 %); CO₂ dissolving in the nutrient solution and in condensate is neglected. Crop uptake depends on light, temperature and CO₂ itself (Eq. 8.3.2), which makes the equation non-linear; in the simulations below it is solved numerically.

When the inputs are constant for long enough, the concentration stops changing: \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = 0\). Solving Eq. 8.3.3 for this steady state gives the two relationships that growers use every day — the concentration a given supply produces, and the supply a given set-point requires.

Steady-state concentration and required supply(8.3.4)
\[ \begin{gathered} C_{\text{ss}} = C_{\text{out}} + \frac{\Phi_{\text{inj}} + \Phi_{\text{resp}} - \Phi_{\text{up}}}{k\,N\,\bar h} \\[6pt] \Phi_{\text{inj,req}} = \Phi_{\text{up}} - \Phi_{\text{resp}} + k\,N\,\bar h\,\big(C_{\text{set}} - C_{\text{out}}\big) \end{gathered} \]

in steady state the inside concentration exceeds the outside concentration by the net CO₂ source divided by the ventilation "conductance" kNh̄. Turned around: to hold a set-point, the supply must cover the crop's uptake plus the ventilation loss, which is proportional to the air exchange rate and to the enrichment above outside air.

How fast does the concentration move towards its steady state? If the inputs are constant, Eq. 8.3.3 is a linear first-order differential equation — the same mathematics as a cup of coffee cooling or a capacitor charging — and its solution is an exponential approach with a characteristic time constant.

Transient response and time constant(8.3.5)
\[ C(t) = C_{\text{ss}} + \big(C_0 - C_{\text{ss}}\big)\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{N + a/(k\bar h)} \approx \frac{1}{N} \]

after a sudden change in supply or ventilation, the concentration moves exponentially from its initial value towards the new steady state. After one time constant it has covered 63 % of the way, after three 95 %. The time constant is the reciprocal of the air exchange rate, shortened further by the crop when uptake increases with CO₂.

  2. Using the definition of \(C_{\text{ss}}\) (Eq. 8.3.4), this is \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = -N\,(C - C_{\text{ss}})\).

  4. If uptake rises with CO₂, linearise \(\Phi_{\text{up}} \approx \Phi_{\text{up},0} + a\,(C - C_0)\). The extra term \(-aC/(k\bar h)\) adds to \(-NC\), so the decay rate becomes \(N + a/(k\bar h)\). In a sealed plant factory (\(N\) = 0.02 h⁻¹, so \(1/N\) = 50 h) with eight tiers of lettuce, \(a/(k\bar h)\) ≈ 0.27 h⁻¹ and \(\tau\) shrinks to about 3.5 h: the crop, not the building, sets the pace.

At sunrise the closed greenhouse (\(N\) = 0.5 h⁻¹, \(\bar h\) = 5.5 m) is at 427 ppm. The grower switches on 150 kg ha⁻¹ h⁻¹ of pure CO₂; the crop takes up about 4.4 g m⁻² h⁻¹. How long does it take to reach 1000 ppm, and how fast does the concentration climb at first?

Reveal the reasoning

Why enrichment is wasteful when the vents are open

Worked example 2 already showed the problem in numbers. Kozai (2013) turned it into an indicator, the CO₂-use efficiency: the fraction of the supplied CO₂ that is actually fixed by the crop. It is the CO₂ counterpart of the water-use efficiency that you will meet in Lesson 8.4.

CO₂-use efficiency (Kozai, 2013)(8.3.6)
\[ \text{CUE} = \frac{\Phi_{\text{up}}}{\Phi_{\text{inj}} + \Phi_{\text{resp,w}}} \;=\; \frac{\Phi_{\text{up}}}{\Phi_{\text{up}} + k\,N\,\bar h\,\big(C - C_{\text{out}}\big)}\quad\text{(steady state)} \]

CO₂-use efficiency is the net photosynthesis of the crop divided by the CO₂ supplied (including CO₂ breathed out by workers). In steady state it equals the uptake divided by the uptake plus the ventilation loss: it falls towards zero as the air exchange rate or the enrichment above outside air increases.

Follow the derivation
  1. Kozai (2013) defines \(\text{CUE} = C_P/(C_S + C_R)\): net photosynthesis \(C_P\) over supply \(C_S\) plus worker respiration \(C_R\).
  2. In steady state with dosing (and ignoring worker respiration), Eq. 8.3.4 gives \(\Phi_{\text{inj}} = \Phi_{\text{up}} + kN\bar h(C - C_{\text{out}})\). Substitute into the definition.
  3. Divide numerator and denominator by \(\Phi_{\text{up}}\): \(\text{CUE} = 1/[1 + kN\bar h(C - C_{\text{out}})/\Phi_{\text{up}}]\). The ratio in the denominator — ventilation loss over uptake — is what the grower must keep small.
  4. Kozai reports that CUE in a plant factory rises with leaf area index up to about 3, and recommends raising the set-point as the crop grows.

Steady state, well-mixed air. Over a whole day CUE must be computed from integrals (total fixed ÷ total supplied), as Figure 4 does; during transients (e.g., filling the greenhouse after sunrise) some CO₂ is stored in the air and released later.

The mini-calculator makes the key result visible: CUE falls steeply once \(N\) exceeds about 1 h⁻¹. On a warm day a greenhouse ventilates at 20–60 air changes per hour (Lesson 8.1), and at such rates holding 1000 ppm would require several tonnes of CO₂ per hectare per hour. Three consequences shape practice:

  1. Enrich high when the vents are closed. In winter and on cool days, when the greenhouse is heated and the vents stay shut, a set-point of 800–1000 ppm costs little extra CO₂ per ppm, and most of it ends up in the crop.
  2. Lower the set-point as the vents open. A common strategy is therefore to couple the set-point to the vent opening, down to about the outdoor level when the vents are wide open. Replacing only the CO₂ the crop takes up — keeping the greenhouse at the ambient concentration rather than letting it fall below — has a CO₂-use efficiency close to 100 %, and Kläring et al. (2007) showed that such model-based dosing at ambient levels increased cucumber yield (Wang et al., 2022).
  3. Keep the vents closed longer. Because enrichment raises the temperature optimum of photosynthesis (Frantz et al., 2004), growers can accept a somewhat higher ventilation temperature and keep CO₂ in. The semi-closed and closed greenhouses of Lesson 8.1 take this to its logical end, cooling with heat exchangers instead of vents. And plant factories, with \(N\) ≈ 0.01–0.02 h⁻¹, reach CUE values of 0.87–0.89 at 1000 µmol mol⁻¹ — which is why they can afford set-points of 1000–2000 µmol mol⁻¹ (Kozai, 2013).
Common misconception

"CO₂ enrichment captures carbon — the plants lock the CO₂ away." They do not, at least not for long. The CO₂ that is not fixed leaves through the vents within minutes to hours, and the carbon that is fixed returns to the atmosphere within weeks or months when the produce is eaten and the crop residues decompose. Greenhouse horticulture is therefore not carbon sequestration, and all CO₂ supplied to greenhouses must be counted as emitted (van Tuyll et al., 2022). What matters for the climate is whether the CO₂ would have been emitted anyway — the subject of the section on sources below.

Finding the economically optimal set-point

If CUE falls as \(C\) rises, what is the best set-point? The answer comes from marginal analysis, the engineer's standard tool for trade-offs: raise the set-point as long as one more ppm earns more than it costs. The benefit is the value of the extra photosynthesis; the cost is the CO₂ that has to be bought for the extra uptake plus the extra ventilation loss.

Economically optimal CO₂ set-point(8.3.7)
\[ \begin{gathered} \Pi(C) = v\,\big[A(C) - A(C_0)\big] - p\,\big[A(C) + kN\bar h\,(C - C_{\text{out}})\big] \\[6pt] \frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi}{\mathrm{d}C} = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; C^{*} = \Gamma - K + \sqrt{\frac{A_s\,K\,(v - p)}{p\,k\,N\,\bar h}} \end{gathered} \]

the net margin of enrichment is the value of the extra CO₂ fixed minus the cost of all the CO₂ supplied. It is largest at the set-point where the marginal value of the extra photosynthesis equals the marginal cost of supplying one more ppm; for the saturating response of Eq. 8.3.2 this optimum has a closed form, which falls as the air exchange rate rises.

Where the CO₂ comes from

The Dutch greenhouse sector alone uses an estimated 2.6 million tonnes of CO₂ per year. About 60 % is produced on site by gas boilers and combined heat and power (CHP) engines; the other 40 % is delivered from industry, 80 % of it through the OCAP pipeline (van Tuyll et al., 2022). Dosing depends strongly on the crop: in 2017 Dutch vegetable growers dosed on average 36 kg CO₂ m⁻² yr⁻¹, and reported tomato dosing ranges from 23 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ with optimised strategies to 45–46 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ without, whereas the crop itself takes up only 13–15 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹ (van Tuyll et al., 2022). Figure 6 and Table 2 summarise the supply routes.

Flue gas from natural gas is the classic source. Burning methane, \(\text{CH}_4 + 2\,\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\), releases 2.74 kg of CO₂ per kg of methane, and natural gas emits 56.1 kg CO₂ per GJ of heat released (IPCC, 2006; the same factor as in Lesson 8.1). The exhaust of a CHP engine contains about 8 % CO₂ (van Tuyll et al., 2022) plus nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and unburned hydrocarbons including ethylene, which are removed catalytically before the gas is blown into the greenhouse. The fundamental drawback is the coupling: every kilogram of CO₂ comes with about 5 kWh of heat, but the crop needs CO₂ most on sunny summer days, when the greenhouse needs no heat at all.

Industrial CO₂ is captured at refineries, ammonia factories, breweries or bioethanol plants, purified and delivered either by pipeline or, liquefied, by truck. The Dutch OCAP pipeline supplies about 0.4 Mt per year to roughly a fifth of the country's greenhouse area and requires at least 98 % purity; trucks carry 20–25 t per delivery at €650–800 per trip, i.e. €26–40 per tonne for transport alone, and bottled CO₂ is usually more than 99 % pure (van Tuyll et al., 2022). Because the CO₂ arrives independently of heat, it can be dosed exactly when it is needed. Its average delivered price in the Netherlands is of the order of €0.15 per kg (Pennisi et al., 2025, citing the Dutch KWIN handbook). Where heat comes from wood chips, pellets, district heating or heat pumps, purchased CO₂ is the natural choice, because biomass flue gas is too contaminated for direct dosing without extensive cleaning (Dion et al., 2011).

Biogenic CO₂ comes from the short carbon cycle. Anaerobic digestion produces biogas of roughly 60 % methane and 40 % CO₂; upgrading the biogas to biomethane separates a concentrated CO₂ stream. Alcoholic fermentation releases 0.955 kg of CO₂ per kg of ethanol. Van Tuyll et al. (2022) estimate that by 2030 biogas upgrading and ethanol fermentation alone could supply the whole Dutch greenhouse demand of 1.8–3.0 Mt per year. The catch is seasonality: a digester produces CO₂ at a steady rate, but greenhouses need most in summer, so a buffer is required — for a regional digester producing 13 kt per year, about 3.5 kt of storage.

Direct air capture (DAC) takes CO₂ from the ambient air. In 2017 Climeworks commissioned the first commercial DAC plant in Hinwil, Switzerland; it nominally captured 900 t of CO₂ per year and delivered it to a nearby greenhouse (Beuttler et al., 2019). Its sorbent is regenerated at 80–120 °C, so about 80 % of the energy demand can be met with low-temperature heat; the company projects a long-term requirement of about 2000 kWh per tonne of CO₂, 400 kWh of it electricity (Beuttler et al., 2019). Because greenhouses need only 800–1000 ppm, not pure CO₂, low-concentration DAC is also being investigated for greenhouses (van Tuyll et al., 2022).

Clean CO₂ is not optional

Plants are far more sensitive to some combustion products than people are. Threshold levels compiled by van Tuyll et al. (2022) include about 40 ppb of NOₓ for 24 hours (16 ppb for a year), 11 ppb of ethylene for 8 hours (5 ppb for four weeks) and 100 ppb of SO₂ for 24 hours. Ethylene is a plant hormone that accelerates ageing, flower drop and ripening; a poorly adjusted burner or a failing catalyst can damage a crop within days. The lower the CO₂ content of the dosing gas, the lower the permitted contaminant concentration in it, because more gas must be blown in to deliver the same CO₂.

Research frontier

As greenhouses electrify and stop burning gas, they lose their cheapest CO₂ source. Dutch research programmes such as Kas als Energiebron are therefore investigating direct air capture for greenhouses, and biogas-upgrading hubs with seasonal CO₂ storage and lime-based systems that absorb CO₂ from biomass gas and release it on demand are being evaluated (van Tuyll et al., 2022). At the same time, tighter buildings, crop-local dosing through perforated tubes under the canopy (Wang et al., 2022) and model-based dosing aim to cut the losses that make every kilogram so expensive.

Safety: CO₂ and people

CO₂ is not toxic in the everyday sense — you exhale roughly 1 kg of it a day — but at high concentrations it causes breathlessness, headaches and dizziness, and at a few per cent it becomes dangerous. It is also 1.52 times denser than air (44.01 vs 28.97 g mol⁻¹), so leaked CO₂ flows downwards and accumulates in pits, cellars, drainage channels and low rooms. Occupational exposure limits protect workers (Table 3).

Assumptions and validity

Well-mixed air. Plant uptake reduces the rise in daylight, but not at night or when the lights fail.

Safety rules for CO₂ dosing

Never enter a room with a CO₂ alarm, a pit or a cold store where CO₂ may have accumulated without measuring first. Store liquid CO₂ outdoors or in well-ventilated spaces; keep dosing interlocked with the lights and the alarm; and remember that the 5000 ppm limit value (AFS 2023:14) applies to every workplace, including a student project container.

Putting it together: dosing strategies

The equations of this lesson translate into a small set of rules that modern climate computers implement:

  • Dose when light is high. The marginal benefit of CO₂ grows with light (Figure 2); at night the crop respires and dosing is pointless. Set-points are often made a function of solar radiation, and enriching mainly in the morning — when stomata are open and vents are still closed — can give similar benefits to all-day enrichment in some crops (Wang et al., 2022).
  • Couple the set-point to the vents. The marginal cost of a ppm is proportional to the air exchange rate (Eq. 8.3.7): high set-points with closed vents, falling to about ambient when the vents are wide open.
  • Use temperature and CO₂ together. Enrichment raises the temperature optimum of photosynthesis, so a slightly higher ventilation temperature keeps vents closed longer and CO₂ in — but it also raises the heat and humidity load, which Lessons 8.1 and 8.2 help you quantify.
  • Put the CO₂ where the leaves are. Perforated tubes under or inside the canopy deliver CO₂ where uptake is highest before it mixes into the air above (Wang et al., 2022).
  • In plant factories, grow the set-point with the crop. CUE rises with leaf area index, so the set-point can be raised as the canopy closes (Kozai, 2013); with \(N\) ≈ 0.02 h⁻¹, 1000–2000 ppm costs little.
  • Measure correctly. NDIR sensors that recalibrate themselves against an assumed "fresh air" baseline drift in permanently enriched rooms; use sensors with dual-wavelength references and compensate for pressure and temperature (Lesson 9.1).

Key terms

CO₂ enrichment (CO₂ fertilisation)
Raising the CO₂ concentration around a crop above the outdoor level to increase photosynthesis and yield.
Mole fraction (ppm, µmol mol⁻¹)
Amount of CO₂ per amount of air; for ideal gases equal to the volume fraction. 1 ppm = 1 µmol mol⁻¹.
Mass concentration
Mass of CO₂ per volume of air, \(\rho = x\,MP/(RT)\); 1.83 mg m⁻³ per ppm at 20 °C.
CO₂ compensation point
The CO₂ concentration at which photosynthetic uptake just balances respiratory release, about 40–55 ppm for C3 leaves at 25 °C.
CO₂ mass balance
Storage change = supply + respiration − crop uptake − ventilation loss, \(k\bar h\,\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = \Phi_{inj} + \Phi_{resp} - \Phi_{up} - kN\bar h(C - C_{out})\).
Air exchange rate
Number of times per hour the air volume is replaced by outside air (h⁻¹).
Time constant
Characteristic time of the exponential approach to steady state, \(\tau \approx 1/N\) when the crop's feedback is small.
CO₂-use efficiency (CUE)
Fraction of the supplied CO₂ that is fixed by the crop (Kozai, 2013): 0.87–0.89 in plant factories, about 0.4–0.6 in closed greenhouses and much lower with open vents.
Set-point
The CO₂ concentration the control system tries to maintain; often a function of light and vent opening.
Marginal analysis
Choosing the level of an input where the benefit of one more unit equals its cost; here it gives the economically optimal set-point.
Flue gas enrichment
Dosing cleaned combustion exhaust from boilers or CHP engines; couples CO₂ supply to heat production.
Biogenic CO₂
CO₂ from the short carbon cycle (biogas upgrading, fermentation, composting) rather than from fossil fuels.
Direct air capture (DAC)
Technology that extracts CO₂ from ambient air with a sorbent that is regenerated by heat.
Occupational exposure limit
Legally set maximum concentration for workers; for CO₂ in Sweden 5000 ppm over 8 h (NGV) and 10 000 ppm over 15 min (KGV), AFS 2023:14.
