Cultivated meat and cellular agriculture

On 5 August 2013 a burger was cooked and eaten in London in front of the world's press. It was made from more than 20,000 thin strands of bovine muscle grown in a Maastricht laboratory, took two years to make and cost over US$325,000. Thirteen years later cultivated meat has been approved for sale in Singapore, the United States, Israel and Australia — and banned in Italy and in several US states. Between the hype and the bans lies an engineering problem: growing fragile, slow, wall-less animal cells at the scale of a food industry. This lesson quantifies it — cell numbers, media costs, oxygen, shear, diffusion, cost per kilogram and carbon footprint — and then examines the rules and the ethics.

From a $325,000 burger to regulated food

Cultivated meat (also called cultured, cell-based or lab-grown meat) is animal tissue produced by growing animal cells outside the animal. The idea is to make the edible part of the animal — muscle, fat and connective tissue — without the rest: no feed wasted on bones, organs and body heat, no manure, no slaughter. It is part of a broader field, cellular agriculture, which also includes the precision fermentation of Lesson 12.3.

Skeletal muscle is a hierarchy. Long, multinucleated muscle fibres are packed with contractile myofibrils; between the fibres lie fat cells (adipocytes), connective tissue made of collagen, and blood capillaries that bring oxygen within a few tenths of a millimetre of every cell. In the body, muscle regenerates from satellite cells, stem cells that sit on the surface of the fibres. When activated they divide as myoblasts, then fuse into myotubes that mature into new fibres (Figure 2). Cultivated meat copies this sequence in two phases: a proliferation phase that multiplies the cells as fast as possible, and a differentiation phase in which they fuse, mature and — ideally — become organised tissue (Post, 2012; Rubio et al., 2020).

Big idea

Cultivated meat replaces the animal with a bioreactor. That removes the biological inefficiencies of the animal (maintaining a whole body) but imports the engineering limits of cell culture: slow growth, expensive nutrients, sterility, oxygen supply, heat removal and diffusion. Whether it can become a mass food depends on these limits, which is why this lesson is mostly about numbers.

Cell sources: which cells, and why it matters

The choice of cell determines how far the cells can multiply, what they can become and how they are regulated (Table 1). Primary cells taken from an animal — satellite cells for muscle, fibro-adipogenic or adipose-derived progenitors for fat — are naturally programmed to make meat tissue, but they stop dividing after a limited number of population doublings. Hayflick and Moorhead (1961) first showed that normal human diploid cells divide only about fifty times in culture; livestock cells also age, and satellite cells tend to lose their ability to form muscle as they are passaged. Pluripotent stem cells — embryonic stem cells, or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) reprogrammed from adult cells with four transcription factors (Takahashi & Yamanaka, 2006) — can self-renew indefinitely and become any cell type, but guiding them reliably to muscle or fat is complex. Immortalised lines escape the doubling limit, either through genetic engineering (for example by expressing telomerase) or spontaneously. Pasitka et al. (2023) showed that chicken fibroblasts can immortalise spontaneously, grow as single cells in serum-free medium and reach 108 × 10⁶ cells per millilitre in continuous culture — about 36 % wet cells by volume — high enough to change the economics of Section 12.4.6.

Cells multiply by binary fission, so their number follows the exponential law of Lesson 12.3 — but on a slow clock. Typical doubling times are about a day: Humbird (2021) assumed 24 h, the CE Delft design 30 h, and bovine satellite cells in the serum-free medium Beefy-9 doubled on average every 39 h (Stout et al., 2022).

Cell number and doubling time(12.4.1)
\[ N(t) = N_0\,2^{\,t/t_{\mathrm{d}}} = N_0\,e^{\mu t},\qquad \mu = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{\mathrm{d}}} \]

the number of cells equals the initial number times two raised to the number of doubling times that have elapsed; this is the same exponential growth law as for microbes, with a specific growth rate of ln 2 over the doubling time.

Follow the derivation
  1. In balanced growth each cell divides once per doubling time; after \(n\) doublings there are \(N_0 2^{n}\) cells. With \(n = t/t_{\mathrm{d}}\): \(N = N_02^{t/t_{\mathrm{d}}}\).
  2. Because \(2^{x} = e^{x\ln 2}\), this is \(N = N_0e^{(\ln 2/t_{\mathrm{d}})t} = N_0e^{\mu t}\) — Eq. 12.3.1 in cell numbers.
  3. Inverting: the number of doublings to go from \(N_0\) to \(N\) is \(n = \log_2(N/N_0)\), taking \(n\,t_{\mathrm{d}}\) hours plus the lag after each transfer.
Assumptions and validity

Constant \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\) — true only in the middle of the growth phase. Adherent cells stop dividing when the surface is full (contact inhibition), and all cells slow down at high density as nutrients run low and lactate and ammonia accumulate. Primary cells slow as they approach senescence.

\(t/t_{\mathrm{d}}\): h/h = dimensionless ✓; \(\mu\): h⁻¹ ✓

How many cells make a steak? A mammalian cell of about 3,500 µm³ weighs about 3.5 ng wet (Allan et al., 2019; Vergeer et al., 2021; Humbird, 2021, used 3.0 ng), so a kilogram of wet cells contains roughly \(3\times10^{11}\) cells.

Cells, doublings and time to a production target(12.4.2)
\[ N_{\mathrm{target}} = \frac{M}{m_{\mathrm{c}}},\qquad n = \log_2\!\frac{M}{m_{\mathrm{c}}\,N_0},\qquad t = n\,t_{\mathrm{d}} + k\,t_{\mathrm{lag}} \]

the number of cells needed is the target mass divided by the mass of one cell; the number of doublings is the base-2 logarithm of the target cell number over the starting number; the time is the number of doublings times the doubling time, plus a lag for each of the k transfers.

Follow the derivation
  1. Mass balance: \(M = N m_{\mathrm{c}}\) (cells of equal mass).
  2. From Eq. 12.4.1, \(N = N_02^{n}\), so \(n = \log_2(N/N_0) = \log_2\big(M/(m_{\mathrm{c}}N_0)\big)\).

Cells do not die or detach, and all cells are harvested. In practice viability is below 100 % and part of each vessel is used to seed the next, so real processes need a few more doublings. The doublings also count against the cells' replicative lifespan: a cell bank already made at 10–20 population doublings leaves fewer for production if primary cells are used.

\(M/m_{\mathrm{c}}\): kg/kg = number of cells ✓; the logarithm is dimensionless ✓

Cells cannot be seeded directly from a vial into a 10 m³ tank: at such a low density they would grow poorly and the medium would be wasted. Instead they pass through a seed train of vessels, each typically five to ten times larger than the last, transferred whenever the cells reach their maximum density (Figure 3).

Growth media: feeding cells without an animal

Animal cells cannot make most of what they need. A culture medium supplies glucose (energy), amino acids including glutamine, vitamins, salts, a buffer (bicarbonate/CO₂ or HEPES) and — the difficult part — the signalling proteins that tell cells to survive and divide. Traditionally these came from fetal bovine serum (FBS), harvested from the blood of calf fetuses at slaughter and added at 10–20 %. FBS is expensive, variable, a contamination risk and at odds with the purpose of animal-free meat. Serum-free formulations replace it with defined components: insulin, transferrin (iron carrier), selenium, albumin, ascorbate and growth factors such as fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β). The "Essential 8" medium for human pluripotent stem cells contains, for example, FGF2 at 100 ng mL⁻¹ and TGF-β1 at 2 ng mL⁻¹ (Chen et al., 2011); Stout et al. (2022) adapted a cheaper relative of it, B8, for bovine satellite cells by adding recombinant albumin ("Beefy-9").

Growth factors are needed in tiny concentrations but are extraordinarily expensive. In the CE Delft techno-economic study, prices quoted in December 2020 were 1.3–2.3 million US$ per gram for FGF2 and 3.7–5.0 million US$ per gram for TGF-β; recombinant proteins (albumin, insulin, transferrin) and growth factors together made up over 99 % of the medium cost (Vergeer et al., 2021).

A linear cost model at fixed prices. Bulk prices at food scale are unknown — the main uncertainty of every techno-economic study.

History — the ethics of serum

For decades, cell biology relied on fetal bovine serum, collected by cardiac puncture from calf fetuses when pregnant cows are slaughtered. A product marketed as slaughter-free could not credibly depend on it. Singapore's January 2023 approval of GOOD Meat's cultivated chicken made with serum-free medium was therefore an important milestone, and serum-free formulations such as Beefy-9 are now published openly (Stout et al., 2022).

Bioreactors for animal cells: oxygen, shear and cell density

Animal cells lack the cell wall that protects bacteria and fungi, many types grow only when attached to a surface, and they need oxygen and gentle mixing. Several reactor types are used (Table 3). In a stirred tank the cells grow either in suspension or on microcarriers — beads of about 100–300 µm on which adherent cells spread. In a perfusion reactor fresh medium flows continuously through the vessel while a filter (for example an alternating tangential-flow, ATF, filter) retains the cells, allowing much higher densities (Figure 5). Hollow-fibre and packed-bed reactors immobilise cells at tissue-like densities of 10⁸–10⁹ cells mL⁻¹, but are hard to scale (Allan et al., 2019).

Why do animal-cell reactors stay small and gently stirred? Because two constraints pull in opposite directions. Oxygen transfer needs power; power creates turbulence; turbulence damages cells. The derivation takes three steps.

Oxygen-limited cell density(12.4.4)
\[ q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,N_V = k_La\left(C^{*} - C_{\min}\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad N_{V,\max} = \frac{k_La\left(C^{*} - C_{\min}\right)}{q_{\mathrm{O_2}}} \]

at steady state the oxygen consumed by the cells — their specific uptake rate times their number per volume — equals the oxygen transferred from the gas; the largest cell density the reactor can sustain is therefore the transfer coefficient times the available driving force, divided by the uptake per cell.

Follow the derivation
  1. Oxygen balance of the medium (Eq. 12.3.7): \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}t = k_La(C^{*} - C) - q_{\mathrm{O_2}}N_V\). At steady state the two terms are equal.
  2. The dissolved O₂ may not fall below \(C_{\min}\), so the largest supportable density is \(N_{V,\max} = k_La(C^{*} - C_{\min})/q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\).
  3. Measured specific uptake rates of animal cells are about 0.2 × 10⁻¹² mol cell⁻¹ h⁻¹ (Ruffieux et al., 1998); CHO cells consumed 2.5–2.9 × 10⁻¹³ (Ducommun et al., 2000). Assumptions and validity

Well-mixed reactor and constant \(q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\). Large vessels have gradients, and the removal of CO₂ becomes limiting before oxygen when oxygen-enriched gas is used: in Humbird's (2021) analysis, CO₂ accumulation — not O₂ supply — made fed-batch reactors above about 20 m³ unattractive.

(h⁻¹ × mol m⁻³)/(mol cell⁻¹ h⁻¹) = cells m⁻³ ✓

Raising \(k_La\) means raising the power input (Eq. 12.3.8). But the power dissipated in a stirred tank cascades from large eddies to ever smaller ones until, at the Kolmogorov microscale, the eddies are so small that viscosity turns their kinetic energy into heat. Cells or microcarriers that are larger than these smallest eddies experience fluctuating velocity differences across their surface.

Kolmogorov microscale of turbulence(12.4.5)
\[ \lambda_K = \left(\frac{\nu^{3}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/4},\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{P}{\rho V} \]

the size of the smallest turbulent eddies equals the fourth root of the kinematic viscosity cubed divided by the power dissipated per unit mass of liquid. More power per kilogram means smaller eddies.

Follow the derivation (dimensional analysis)
  1. Kolmogorov (1941) argued that the smallest eddies depend only on the rate at which energy arrives from larger eddies, \(\varepsilon\) (W kg⁻¹ = m² s⁻³), and on the viscosity that dissipates it, \(\nu\) (m² s⁻¹).
  2. Seek a length \(\lambda = \nu^{a}\varepsilon^{b}\): units \(\text{m} = (\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1})^{a}(\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-3})^{b}\). Metres: \(1 = 2a + 2b\); seconds: \(0 = -a - 3b\). Solve: \(a = 3/4\), \(b = -1/4\). Hence \(\lambda_K = \nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4} = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\).
  3. In a stirred tank the mean \(\varepsilon = P/(\rho V)\); near the impeller the local value can be 10–50 times higher, so local eddies are smaller than the mean suggests.
  4. Numbers: \(\nu = 0.7\times10^{-6}\) m² s⁻¹ gives \(\lambda_K = 136\) µm at 0.001 W kg⁻¹, 77 µm at 0.01, 43 µm at 0.1 and 24 µm at 1 W kg⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

Homogeneous isotropic turbulence. Croughan et al. (1987) found that growth of cells on microcarriers suffered once the Kolmogorov scale approached the size of the microcarriers; freely suspended cells, which are smaller than the eddies, are more tolerant, and in sparged reactors most damage comes from bursting bubbles at the surface rather than from the impeller (Chisti, 2000). Nienow (2006) concluded that with protective additives such as Pluronic F-68, "shear sensitivity" of suspension cells is no longer the main problem at large scale; oxygen and CO₂ transfer and homogeneity are.

\((\text{m}^6\text{s}^{-3}/(\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-3}))^{1/4} = (\text{m}^4)^{1/4} = \text{m}\) ✓

Now combine the two. Humbird (2021) used a mass-transfer correlation for large cell-culture reactors, \(k_La = 0.075\,(P/V)^{0.47}u_s^{0.8}\) (s⁻¹, with \(P/V\) in W m⁻³ and \(u_s\) in m s⁻¹), with the superficial gas velocity capped at \(u_s = 0.006\) m s⁻¹ to protect cells from bubbles.

The oxygen–shear trade-off(12.4.6)
\[ N_{V,\max} = \frac{c\,(\rho\,\varepsilon)^{\alpha}\,u_s^{\beta}\left(C^{*} - C_{\min}\right)}{q_{\mathrm{O_2}}}\propto\varepsilon^{\alpha},\qquad \lambda_K\propto\varepsilon^{-1/4}\quad\Rightarrow\quad N_{V,\max}\propto\lambda_K^{-4\alpha}\approx\lambda_K^{-1.9} \]

inserting a power-law mass-transfer correlation into Eq. 12.4.4 shows that the supportable cell density grows with the dissipated power to the power alpha, whereas the smallest eddy size shrinks with the power to the minus one quarter; eliminating the power, the cell density scales roughly with the inverse square of the smallest eddy the cells can tolerate.

Follow the derivation
  1. Insert \(k_La = c(P/V)^{\alpha}u_s^{\beta}\) and \(P/V = \rho\varepsilon\) into Eq. 12.4.4: \(N_{V,\max} = c(\rho\varepsilon)^{\alpha}u_s^{\beta}(C^{*} - C_{\min})/q_{\mathrm{O_2}} \propto \varepsilon^{\alpha}\).
  2. From Eq. 12.4.5, \(\varepsilon = \nu^{3}/\lambda_K^{4}\). Substitute: \(N_{V,\max} \propto (\nu^3\lambda_K^{-4})^{\alpha} \propto \lambda_K^{-4\alpha}\).

Assumptions and validity

A single empirical correlation, constant \(u_s\), and a damage threshold expressed through the mean \(\varepsilon\). Real reactors are heterogeneous; the scaling law shows the direction and approximate strength of the trade-off, not an exact limit.

Scaffolds, tissue and the diffusion limit

A suspension of cells is not a steak. To create texture, cells must be organised: aligned muscle fibres, interspersed fat, connective tissue. Scaffolds give cells something to attach to and a shape to follow. Candidates include edible microcarriers that stay in the product, porous plant-protein structures such as textured soy protein (Ben-Arye et al., 2020), decellularised plant tissues, hydrogels and fibres spun or printed in parallel. Scaffolds must be food-safe, cheap, permissive to cell attachment and differentiation, and — the key physical constraint — permeable to oxygen and nutrients.

In an animal, no cell is more than a few tenths of a millimetre from a capillary. In a culture without blood vessels, oxygen reaches the inside of a tissue only by diffusion, while the cells along the way consume it.

Oxygen penetration depth in a cell-dense tissue(12.4.7)
\[ D\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^2C}{\mathrm{d}x^2} = Q = q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad L = \sqrt{\frac{2\,D\,C_s}{q_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,\rho_{\mathrm{c}}}} \]

in a tissue where cells consume oxygen at a constant volumetric rate, the diffusion flux changes at the rate of consumption; solving this balance, oxygen reaches a depth equal to the square root of two times the diffusion coefficient times the surface concentration, divided by the volumetric consumption rate. Deeper cells starve.

Follow the derivation
  1. Steady-state mass balance on a thin slice of tissue at depth \(x\): diffusion in − diffusion out = consumption → \(D\,\mathrm{d}^2C/\mathrm{d}x^2 = Q\) (zero-order uptake, since cells consume at their maximum rate until O₂ is nearly exhausted).
  2. Boundary conditions: \(C = C_s\) at the surface \(x = 0\); at the penetration depth \(x = L\) both \(C = 0\) and the flux \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}x = 0\) (no oxygen passes beyond).
  3. Integrate twice: \(C(x) = C_s - (Q/D)(Lx - x^2/2)\) satisfies \(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}x = 0\) at \(x = L\). Setting \(C(L) = 0\): \(C_s = QL^2/(2D)\) → \(L = \sqrt{2DC_s/Q}\).
  4. At 3 × 10⁸ cells mL⁻¹, \(L\) ≈ 0.22 mm — the classic 100–200 µm range between capillaries.
Assumptions and validity

Zero-order consumption and a uniform cell density; real cells slow their respiration at low O₂ (Michaelis–Menten kinetics), which extends \(L\) slightly, and nutrients other than O₂ (glucose) and waste removal (lactate, CO₂) impose similar limits. Oxygen-carrying media or pure-oxygen surfaces raise \(C_s\) and hence \(L\) by \(\sqrt{C_s}\) only.

\(\sqrt{(\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-1}\times\text{mol m}^{-3})/(\text{mol m}^{-3}\,\text{s}^{-1})} = \sqrt{\text{m}^2} = \text{m}\) ✓

The diffusion limit is why most products approved so far are minced, emulsified or hybrid: cultivated cells or fat blended with plant protein, which provides structure while the animal cells contribute flavour and aroma. Pasitka et al. (2023), for example, combined cultured chicken fat-like cells with extruded soy protein in chicken strips, and the cultivated pork fat that Mission Barns had cleared by the FDA in 2025 is sold in products that combine plant proteins with small amounts of the fat. Whole-cut, fully cultivated steaks remain a research goal.

Techno-economics: what would a kilogram cost?

Two techno-economic analyses (TEA) frame the debate. Humbird (2021), an independent chemical engineer commissioned by Open Philanthropy, designed plants as if animal cells were grown like pharmaceuticals but at food scale. With 3,000 pg cells doubling every 24 h and metabolically enhanced cells, a fed-batch plant of 24 × 20 m³ reactors would make 6.8 kt of wet cells per year at about US$37 kg⁻¹ (macronutrients 19, micronutrients 3, other costs including capital 15); a perfusion plant of 96 × 2 m³ reactors, 6.9 kt a⁻¹ at about US$51 kg⁻¹. He proposed about US$25 kg⁻¹ of wet cells as a threshold for competing with meat and concluded that metabolic efficiency and cheap plant-hydrolysate media are "necessary but insufficient" for cultivated meat to displace conventional meat. The CE Delft study for the Good Food Institute (Vergeer et al., 2021) modelled a 10 kt a⁻¹ plant with 130 stirred tanks of 10 m³ and 430 perfusion reactors of 2 m³ and an investment of about US$450 million. With current medium prices, costs were US$1,708–22,421 per kg; a series of increasingly optimistic assumptions brought them down to about US$6 per kg (Figure 8 lets you explore the same levers).

Annual output of a batch cell-culture plant(12.4.8)

the mass of cells produced per year equals the total reactor volume times the cell mass concentration at harvest, times the number of production cycles per year: the available operating hours divided by the cycle time, which is the growth time in the production vessel plus the other time the vessel is occupied.

Capital is paid once but must be recovered every year. The standard tool is the capital recovery factor, the fraction of the investment that must be earned each year to repay it with interest over its life.

\[ c = \frac{\mathrm{CRF}\cdot I}{\dot M} + c_{\mathrm{med}} + c_{\mathrm{el}} + c_{\mathrm{o}},\qquad \mathrm{CRF} = \frac{i\,(1+i)^{n}}{(1+i)^{n} - 1} \]

the cost per kilogram equals the annualised investment — the investment times the capital recovery factor, divided by the annual output — plus the medium, electricity and other operating costs per kilogram. Life-cycle assessment: energy, media and the purified-medium debate

Is cultivated meat better for the climate? The answer depends almost entirely on energy and on the medium. The most detailed ex-ante LCA to date, by Sinke et al. (2023), combined data from more than 15 companies for commercial-scale production in 2030. Cultivated meat turned crops into meat almost three times more efficiently than chicken, the most efficient animal, so its agricultural land use and nitrogen and air-pollution emissions were low. But production is energy-intensive: facility energy was about 22.3 kWh per kg of cells in the baseline, around 75 % of it for cooling the reactors, compared with 2.8–9.6 kWh kg⁻¹ in a hollow-fibre design by Tuomisto et al. (2022). The carbon footprint ranged from 2.8 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ with renewable energy throughout the supply chain, through 4.0 with renewable energy at the facility only, to 14.3 with global-average energy (Figure 9).

Where the numbers come from
  1. Sinke et al. (2023) report 4.0 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ when the facility runs on renewables and upstream processes use average energy — so the upstream part is about 4.0 kg CO₂e kg⁻¹.
  2. With global-average energy the total was 14.3: \(22.3\ \text{kWh}\times0.46 + 4.0 = 14.3\) — the simple model reproduces the reported value.
  3. Because nearly all of the footprint is CO₂ from energy, it falls as grids decarbonise; for livestock most of it is methane and nitrous oxide from biology, which is harder to abate.
Assumptions and validity

Linear in energy; the upstream term itself depends on the energy mix of the ingredient suppliers (Sinke's "ambitious benchmark" with renewable energy in the supply chain gave 2.8). It assumes food-grade medium ingredients — which is exactly what the purified-medium debate is about.

kWh kg⁻¹ × kg CO₂e kWh⁻¹ + kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ = kg CO₂e kg⁻¹ ✓

The purified-medium debate. Risner et al. (2024), at the University of California, Davis, argued that if near-term production relied on highly refined growth media — pharmaceutical-grade ingredients purified to remove bacterial endotoxins, to which animal cells are very sensitive — the environmental impact would be "orders of magnitude higher than median beef production". To represent purification they multiplied the energy of ingredient production by a factor of 20, based on pharmaceutical industry data; in their preprint the purified scenarios reached roughly 250–1,500 kg CO₂e per kg, 4–25 times retail beef. Swartz, Nahmias and Tuomisto (2025) replied that food-grade inputs, not pharmaceutical ones, are used at scale, and Risner et al. (2025) defended their assumptions for the near term. Both sides agree on the crucial point: the grade and production route of the medium decide whether cultivated meat is a climate solution or a problem.

CO₂ versus methane. Carbon dioxide equivalents compare gases over a fixed horizon (usually 100 years). Lynch and Pierrehumbert (2019) modelled the warming itself and showed that because methane from cattle is short-lived while CO₂ accumulates, cultured meat produced with fossil energy could, in some scenarios, cause more long-term warming than cattle. Their conclusion — cultured meat "is not prima facie climatically superior to cattle" — makes decarbonised energy a precondition, not a detail.

Why ban a food that is not yet on sale?

The bans in Italy and US states are defended as protecting farmers, food culture and consumers from an unproven technology; critics see protectionism and a restriction of consumer choice, and point out that safety is already assessed by regulators. Ethics, acceptance and the future of meat

The ethical case for cultivated meat rests on animal welfare — tens of billions of land animals are slaughtered each year — and on reducing land use, deforestation and zoonotic risks. The case against, or at least for caution, includes the use of animal inputs (serum, biopsies) in early processes, the energy footprint, the concentration of food production in capital-intensive, patent-protected companies, the livelihoods of livestock farmers and rural regions, and cultural and religious meanings of meat. Some ask whether resources would be better spent on plant-based diets that already exist.

Consumers are divided. A systematic review found moderate but conditional willingness to try cultured meat, with unnaturalness, safety and price as the main concerns and animal welfare and the environment as the main perceived benefits (Bryant & Barnett, 2018). Across novel food technologies, perceived naturalness, trust in producers and regulators, and disgust shape acceptance more than technical information does (Siegrist & Hartmann, 2020; Lessons 13.1 and 13.2). Company fortunes mirror the uncertainty: UPSIDE Foods stopped building a large plant in 2024 and cut staff again in 2025, and Aleph Farms laid off staff in 2026 while switching to contract manufacturing — even as new approvals arrived.

Frontier 2024–2026

Research is moving towards spontaneously immortalised, suspension-adapted cell lines that reach tens of millions of cells per millilitre in serum-free media; growth factors made by precision fermentation or replaced by small molecules; cultivated fat as a flavour ingredient in hybrid products; and edible scaffolds with internal perfusion. Whether cultivated meat becomes a niche ingredient or a staple will be decided by media costs, the price of capital and clean energy — and by regulators and voters.

Key terms

Cultivated meat
Animal tissue produced by growing animal cells outside the animal; also called cultured, cell-based or cell-cultivated meat.
Cellular agriculture
Production of agricultural products from cell cultures instead of whole organisms, including cultivated meat and precision fermentation.
Satellite cell
Muscle stem cell located on muscle fibres that proliferates as myoblasts and fuses into new fibres.
Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)
An adult cell reprogrammed, typically with four transcription factors, to an embryonic-like state that can self-renew and form any cell type.
Immortalised cell line
Cells that divide indefinitely, spontaneously or after genetic engineering, escaping the replicative limit of primary cells.
Hayflick limit
The finite number of divisions (about fifty for normal human diploid cells) that primary cells undergo in culture before senescence.
Serum-free medium
Culture medium without animal serum, in which defined proteins (insulin, transferrin, albumin, growth factors) replace fetal bovine serum.
Growth factor
Signalling protein, such as FGF2 or TGF-β, that stimulates cell survival and division; the most expensive medium component per gram.
Seed train
Sequence of progressively larger culture vessels used to expand cells from a vial to the production bioreactor.
Microcarrier
Bead of about 100–300 µm on which adherent cells grow in a stirred bioreactor.
Perfusion culture
Continuous exchange of medium while the cells are retained in the reactor, allowing high cell densities.
Kolmogorov microscale
Size of the smallest turbulent eddies, (ν³/ε)^¼; eddies comparable with cells or microcarriers can damage them.
Diffusion limit
Maximum depth (≈ 0.2–0.4 mm) to which oxygen can diffuse into a cell-dense tissue before it is consumed.
Techno-economic analysis (TEA)
Model combining process design, mass and energy balances and costs to estimate the production cost of a technology.

Fraction of an investment that must be earned each year to repay it with interest over its life, i(1+i)ⁿ/((1+i)ⁿ − 1).
