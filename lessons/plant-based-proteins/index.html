Plant-based proteins and texturisation

A field pea contains plenty of protein, but nobody mistakes a bowl of pea soup for a chicken breast. Turning plant protein into something that bites, chews and holds juice like muscle is an engineering problem: separate the protein from starch and fibre, keep it functional, then push it through heat and shear until it forms aligned fibres. This lesson follows that chain from Nordic protein crops to the cooling die of a high-moisture extruder — with the mass balances, fluid mechanics and heat transfer that decide whether the product works.

What you will be able to do

  • Compare protein crops, including Nordic ones (pea, faba bean, oats, rapeseed, hemp), by protein content, protein yield per hectare and amino-acid profile (LO1, LO3).
  • Calculate mass balances, protein purity and recovery for dry fractionation and wet (pH-shift) extraction, and explain the physics of air classification and isoelectric precipitation (LO3).
  • Explain the functional properties of plant proteins — solubility, gelation, emulsification, foaming — and the origin of anti-nutritional factors and off-flavours (LO1).
  • Distinguish low- and high-moisture extrusion, and calculate specific mechanical energy, shear rates and cooling times that govern fibre formation in the cooling die (LO3).
  • Evaluate shear-cell technology, 3D printing and the market development of plant-based products, including Swedish companies, as of 2026 (LO6, LO7).

From field to fibre: why texture is the bottleneck

Most of the protein humans eat directly from plants comes in the form it grows: bread, porridge, beans, lentils. A much smaller but fast-changing share is eaten as ingredients — protein concentrates and isolates that are extracted from crops and then reassembled into foods that imitate meat, milk or eggs. The appeal is obvious from Lesson 12.1: 100 g of protein from peas causes about 0.44 kg CO₂e and occupies 3.4 m² of land for a year, versus about 50 kg CO₂e and 164 m² for beef from beef herds (Poore & Nemecek, 2018). The difficulty is equally obvious to anyone who has cooked with peas: plant proteins are globular storage proteins, packed into protein bodies in the seed together with starch, fibre, oil and a range of bitter or beany-tasting compounds. Muscle, by contrast, is a hierarchy of aligned fibres.

The process chain therefore has two halves (Figure 1). Fractionation separates protein from the rest of the seed, trading purity against yield, water and energy. Structuring — extrusion, shear cells, 3D printing — converts the protein into an anisotropic, fibrous material. Each step has a measurable physics: particle aerodynamics in an air classifier, acid–base chemistry in wet extraction, rheology and heat transfer in the extruder.

Dry fractionation: milling and air classification

In a pea cotyledon, protein is stored in small protein bodies of about 1–3 µm, embedded between much larger starch granules of roughly 20–40 µm. If the seed is milled finely enough to detach the protein bodies from the starch without breaking the starch granules, the flour contains two populations of particles that differ by an order of magnitude in size. An air classifier separates them: a rotating classifier wheel throws coarse particles outward by centrifugal force, while an air stream drags fine particles inward through the wheel to a cyclone. The fine fraction is protein-enriched, the coarse fraction starch-enriched (Figure 2). Because no water is added, no drying is needed and the proteins stay native and functional (Schutyser et al., 2015).

Why does the wheel separate by size? A particle in the classifier zone is pushed outward by the centrifugal force and dragged inward by the air flowing radially through the wheel. Both forces depend on particle diameter — but differently.

Cut size of a centrifugal air classifier(12.2.2)
\[ d_{\text{c}} = \sqrt{\frac{18\,\mu\,v_{\text{r}}}{(\rho_{\text{p}}-\rho_{\text{a}})\,\omega^{2}\,r}} \]

the cut size — the particle diameter that is equally likely to go either way — is the square root of eighteen times the air viscosity times the radial air velocity, divided by the density difference between particle and air, times the square of the angular velocity, times the radius of the wheel.

Follow the derivation
  1. Centrifugal force (outward) on a sphere of diameter \(d\), corrected for buoyancy: \(F_{\text{c}} = \tfrac{\pi}{6}d^{3}(\rho_{\text{p}}-\rho_{\text{a}})\,\omega^{2}r\).
  2. Drag force (inward): particles of a few micrometres move slowly relative to the air, so the Reynolds number is ≪ 1 and Stokes' law applies: \(F_{\text{d}} = 3\pi\mu d\,v_{\text{r}}\).
  3. A particle that is exactly balanced satisfies \(F_{\text{c}} = F_{\text{d}}\): \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}d^{3}(\rho_{\text{p}}-\rho_{\text{a}})\omega^{2}r = 3\pi\mu d v_{\text{r}}\).
  4. Divide by \(\pi d/6\): \(d^{2}(\rho_{\text{p}}-\rho_{\text{a}})\omega^{2}r = 18\mu v_{\text{r}}\), and take the square root.
  5. Interpretation: \(F_{\text{c}}\propto d^{3}\) but \(F_{\text{d}}\propto d\). Larger particles are dominated by the centrifugal force and are rejected; smaller ones follow the air. Faster wheels (larger ω) give a finer cut.
Assumptions and validity

Spherical, non-interacting particles in Stokes flow (valid for \(Re = \rho_a v d/\mu \ll 1\)). Real flours contain agglomerates, and particles collide, so the real separation curve is not a sharp step but an S-shaped "grade efficiency" curve around \(d_{\text{c}}\). Operators tune wheel speed and airflow empirically; the equation explains the direction and magnitude of the effect.

\(\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{Pa s}\cdot\text{m s}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-3}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}\cdot\text{m}}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}\cdot\text{m s}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-2}}} = \sqrt{\text{m}^{2}} = \text{m}\) ✓

The performance of a fractionation step is judged by two numbers that pull in opposite directions: the purity (protein content) of the enriched fraction and the recovery (the share of the feed protein that ends up in it). A finer cut raises purity but sends more protein to the coarse fraction. For pea, air classification typically roughly doubles the protein content, to about 45–60 %, at a protein recovery that depends strongly on the cut size (Pelgrom et al., 2013; Schutyser et al., 2015).

Mass balance and protein recovery of a two-way split(12.2.3)
\[ m_{\text{F}} = m_{\text{P}} + m_{\text{C}},\qquad m_{\text{F}}x_{\text{F}} = m_{\text{P}}x_{\text{P}} + m_{\text{C}}x_{\text{C}},\qquad R = \frac{m_{\text{P}}\,x_{\text{P}}}{m_{\text{F}}\,x_{\text{F}}} = y_{\text{P}}\frac{x_{\text{P}}}{x_{\text{F}}} \]

total mass in equals the mass of the protein-rich and the coarse fraction; protein in equals protein out in both fractions; the protein recovery is the protein in the enriched fraction divided by the protein in the feed, which is the mass yield of the enriched fraction times its enrichment factor.

Worked example 2Air-classifying 10 t of pea flour

10 t of dehulled, pin-milled pea flour with 23 % protein (dry basis) is air-classified. The fine fraction is 25 % of the mass and contains 55 % protein. (a) What is the protein recovery? (b) What is the protein content of the coarse fraction? (c) If the classifier is re-set to a finer cut, the fines drop to 18 % of the mass at 60 % protein. Which setting is better?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) Eq. 12.2.3: \(R = 0.25\times55/23 = 0.598\) → 60 % of the protein goes to the fines (1.375 t of protein out of 2.30 t).
  2. (b) Coarse fraction: 7.5 t containing \(2.30 - 1.375 = 0.925\) t of protein → \(x_{\text{C}} = 12.3\) %. The "starch fraction" still holds 40 % of the protein.
  3. (c) Finer cut: \(R = 0.18\times60/23 = 0.47\). Purity rises by 5 percentage points but recovery drops by 13 points.
  4. Judgement. There is no universal "better": it depends on the prices of the two fractions. If the coarse fraction is sold as feed, the loss of protein to it is not lost to the food system. Industrial plants often mill and classify twice to raise recovery.

Wet extraction: pH-shift solubilisation and isoelectric precipitation

To reach purities above about 80 %, the protein must be dissolved and separated from insoluble starch and fibre. The dominant industrial route for pea, faba and soy is the pH-shift (alkaline extraction – isoelectric precipitation) process shown in Figure 3. It exploits the fact that a protein's solubility depends on its net electric charge, which depends on pH.

Each amino acid with an ionisable side chain carries a charge that depends on pH. Carboxyl groups (aspartate, glutamate, C-terminus) are negative above their pKa of about 4; amino and guanidino groups (lysine, arginine, N-terminus) are positive below their pKa of about 8–12.5; histidine switches near pH 6. The net charge of the whole protein is the sum.

Net charge of a protein (Henderson–Hasselbalch)(12.2.4)
\[ Z(\text{pH}) = \sum_{j\,\in\,\text{basic}} \frac{n_j}{1+10^{\,\text{pH}-\text{p}K_{a,j}}} \;-\; \sum_{k\,\in\,\text{acidic}} \frac{n_k}{1+10^{\,\text{p}K_{a,k}-\text{pH}}} \]

the net charge is the number of positively charged groups — each basic group counted by the fraction of time it is protonated — minus the number of negatively charged groups — each acidic group counted by the fraction of time it is deprotonated. At the isoelectric point pI the two sums are equal and the net charge is zero.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical pKa
\(n_j\)number of basic groups: Lys, Arg, His, N-terminus–10.5, 12.5, 6.0, 8.0
\(n_k\)number of acidic groups: Asp + Glu (C-terminus), Cys, Tyr–≈ 4.0, 8.3, 10.1
\(Z\)net charge (elementary charges per molecule, or per 1000 residues)––
Follow the derivation
  1. For an acid HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, \(K_a = [\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}]\). Taking −log₁₀: \(\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log_{10}([\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}])\) (Henderson–Hasselbalch).
  2. The deprotonated fraction is \(\alpha = [\text{A}^-]/([\text{A}^-]+[\text{HA}]) = 1/(1+10^{\text{p}K_a-\text{pH}})\). For an acidic side chain this is the fraction carrying charge −1.
  3. For a basic group BH⁺ ⇌ B + H⁺, the protonated (charged +1) fraction is \(1/(1+10^{\text{pH}-\text{p}K_a})\).
  4. Sum over all groups with their counts: Eq. 12.2.4. Solving \(Z(\text{pI}) = 0\) numerically gives the isoelectric point; for pea and soy globulins it is around pH 4.5.
Why charge controls solubility

Near the pI, molecules carry little net charge, so electrostatic repulsion between them is weak; attractive hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions dominate and the proteins aggregate and precipitate. Far from the pI, like charges repel and the proteins stay dispersed. The result is the U-shaped solubility curve of Figure 4. Ionic strength modulates the effect: salt screens charges (salting-in at low concentration, salting-out at high).

Counts × dimensionless fractions = dimensionless charge number ✓

Worked example 3Water and energy for 1 t of pea flour

1000 kg (dry basis) of pea flour at 23 % protein is extracted at a liquid-to-solid ratio of 9:1 with step yields as in the mini-calculator (0.85, 0.95, 0.90, 0.97); the isolate contains 85 % protein. (a) How much isolate is produced and what is the overall protein recovery? (b) The washed curd leaving the centrifuge contains 20 % dry matter. Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(R_{\text{tot}} = 0.85\times0.95\times0.90\times0.97 = 0.705\). Protein recovered: \(230\times0.705 = 162\) kg. Isolate: \(162/0.85 = 191\) kg dry matter (≈ 201 kg at 5 % moisture).
  2. Water in extraction: 9 m³ per tonne of flour, plus washing water — several cubic metres of process water per 200 kg of isolate, which ends up as effluent rich in sugars and albumins.
  3. (b) Water to evaporate. Curd at 20 % DM carrying 191 kg DM has total mass \(191/0.20 = 955\) kg, i.e. 764 kg water. Product at 5 % moisture: \(191/0.95 = 201\) kg, containing 10 kg water. Evaporate \(764-10 = 754\) kg.
  4. Heat. \(754\times4.5 = 3.4\) GJ, about 17 MJ per kg of isolate — before counting the energy for pumping, centrifuges and wastewater treatment.
  5. Sanity check. The latent heat of water is 2.26 MJ kg⁻¹; spray dryers typically need about twice that, so 4.5 MJ kg⁻¹ is plausible. The comparison explains why less refined, dry-fractionated ingredients have lower environmental impacts (Lie-Piang et al., 2021).
≈ 190 kg isolate (R ≈ 70 %); ≈ 750 kg water evaporated, ≈ 3.4 GJ of heat
Key idea — purity costs

Every step towards higher purity costs protein (yield), water and energy, and produces side streams. Life-cycle assessments of protein ingredients show that less refined ingredients have lower environmental impacts (Lie-Piang et al., 2021). The engineering question is therefore not "how pure can we make it?" but "how pure does this product need to be?" — a question that shear-cell and extrusion research increasingly answers with "less than we thought".

Functional properties, anti-nutrients and off-flavours

An ingredient is bought for what it does in a food. The techno-functional properties of plant proteins follow from their molecular structure: large, compact globular proteins (the 11S legumins and 7S vicilins of legumes, glycinin and β-conglycinin of soy, cruciferin of rapeseed) that unfold when heated, expose hydrophobic groups and form networks.

Emulsion stability shows how much of food texture is plain fluid mechanics. An oil droplet in a plant drink rises because it is lighter than water; the only thing that slows it is viscous drag.

\[ v_{\text{cream}} = \frac{2\,a^{2}\,(\rho_{\text{c}}-\rho_{\text{d}})\,g}{9\,\eta_{\text{c}}} \]

Allergens

Soy and wheat (gluten) are among the major food allergens that must be declared in the EU; pea and other legumes can also cause allergies, and cross-reactivity (e.g., peanut–lupin) exists. Switching a product from soy to pea protein changes, but does not remove, its allergen profile.

Extrusion: from powder to fibres

An extruder is a continuous heat-and-shear reactor. In a co-rotating twin-screw extruder two intermeshing screws turn inside a heated barrel. Protein powder and water are fed at one end, conveyed, compressed, kneaded and heated until the proteins denature and form a viscous melt, which is forced through a die at the other end (Figure 5). Two families of processes exist:

Two energy inputs transform the material: thermal energy from the heated barrel and mechanical energy dissipated by viscous friction when the screws shear the melt. The mechanical input, normalised by throughput, is the specific mechanical energy (SME) — one of the most important process indicators in extrusion, because it correlates with protein unfolding, cross-linking and final texture.

Specific mechanical energy(12.2.7)
\[ \text{SME} = \frac{P_{\text{mech}}}{\dot m} = \frac{P_{\text{max}}\;\dfrac{\tau}{\tau_{\max}}\;\dfrac{N}{N_{\max}}\;\eta_{\text{gb}}}{\dot m} = \frac{2\pi\,N_{\text{s}}\,T_{\text{q}}}{\dot m} \]

the specific mechanical energy is the mechanical power delivered to the material divided by the mass flow rate. With machine data it is the rated motor power times the fraction of maximum torque used times the fraction of maximum screw speed (times the gearbox efficiency), divided by the mass flow; equivalently two pi times screw speed times torque, divided by the mass flow.

  3. Energy per unit mass = power / mass flow. With power in kW and flow in kg s⁻¹, SME comes out in kJ kg⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

SME is an average over the whole screw: it does not say where the energy was dissipated. Water acts as a plasticiser and lubricant, so wetter melts have lower viscosity and lower SME at the same screw speed. Compare SME values only between runs with similar screw configuration and moisture.

kW / (kg s⁻¹) = kJ s⁻¹ / (kg s⁻¹) = kJ kg⁻¹ ✓

How do fibres form? The cooling die

High-moisture extrusion was developed empirically, and the mechanism of fibre formation is still debated. The leading hypotheses (Cornet et al., 2022; van der Sman & van der Goot, 2023) combine three elements:

  1. Phase separation. The hot melt is not a single homogeneous phase: protein-rich and water-rich (or protein-rich and polysaccharide-rich) domains coexist, especially when less refined ingredients containing fibre and starch are used.
  2. Deformation and alignment in laminar flow. In the die the melt flows in laminar shear flow. Dispersed domains are stretched along the flow direction; the velocity profile is parabolic, so layers near the wall are sheared strongly while the core moves almost as a plug.
  3. Solidification from the wall inwards. The cold die wall cools the melt below the temperature at which the protein network sets; the stretched structure is frozen in, layer by layer, before it can relax. Cooling below 100 °C also prevents water from flashing to steam at the exit, which would destroy the fibres (and is exactly what TVP exploits).

The first two elements are described by viscous flow in a slit; the third by transient heat conduction. Both can be calculated.

Laminar flow in a slit die: velocity profile and wall shear rate(12.2.8)
\[ u(y) = \tfrac{3}{2}\,\bar u\left[1-\left(\frac{2y}{H}\right)^{2}\right],\qquad \bar u = \frac{\dot V}{W H},\qquad \dot\gamma_{\text{w}} = \frac{6\,\bar u}{H} = \frac{6\,\dot V}{W H^{2}} \]

in a narrow slit of gap H and width W the velocity varies parabolically across the gap, from zero at the walls to one and a half times the mean velocity at the centre. The mean velocity is the volumetric flow divided by the cross-section, and the shear rate at the wall is six times the mean velocity divided by the gap.

Follow the derivation
  1. Steady, fully developed laminar flow between parallel plates (W ≫ H): the momentum balance reduces to \(\mathrm{d}\tau/\mathrm{d}y = \mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}x = -G\) (constant pressure gradient).
  2. For a Newtonian fluid \(\tau = \eta\,\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}y\), so \(\eta\,\mathrm{d}^{2}u/\mathrm{d}y^{2} = -G\).
  3. Integrate twice with no slip at the walls (\(u = 0\) at \(y = \pm H/2\)): \(u(y) = \frac{G}{2\eta}\left(\frac{H^{2}}{4}-y^{2}\right)\).
  4. The mean velocity is \(\bar u = \frac{1}{H}\int_{-H/2}^{H/2}u\,\mathrm{d}y = \frac{GH^{2}}{12\eta}\); eliminating \(G\) gives the profile in terms of \(\bar u\), with \(u_{\max} = \tfrac32\bar u\).
  5. Shear rate: \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}y = -12\bar u\,y/H^{2}\); at the wall \(|y| = H/2\): \(\dot\gamma_{\text{w}} = 6\bar u/H\). The shear rate is zero on the centre plane — which is why fibres often align best near the walls and a "core" can remain less structured.
  6. Protein melts are shear-thinning (power-law index \(n<1\)); the wall shear rate is then larger by the factor \((2n+1)/(3n)\) and the profile is flatter.
\(\dot\gamma_{\text{w}}\): (m s⁻¹)/m = s⁻¹ ✓; \(\bar u\): (m³ s⁻¹)/(m × m) = m s⁻¹ ✓
Cooling of the melt in the die (transient conduction in a slab)(12.2.9)
\[ \frac{T(y,t)-T_{\text{w}}}{T_0-T_{\text{w}}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{4(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)\pi}\cos\!\left(\frac{(2n+1)\pi y}{H}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}\alpha t}{H^{2}}\right),\qquad t_{\text{c}}\approx\frac{H^{2}}{\pi^{2}\alpha}\ln\!\left(\frac{4}{\pi\,\theta_{\text{c}}}\right) \]

the dimensionless temperature at position y across the gap after a time t in the die is a sum of cosine modes that decay exponentially; the higher modes die out quickly, so the time for the core to cool to a target dimensionless temperature θc is approximately the gap squared divided by pi squared times the thermal diffusivity, times the natural logarithm of four over pi times θc.

Follow the derivation
  1. Neglect conduction along the flow direction and viscous heating in the die; then each slice of melt cools like a slab of thickness \(H\) whose two faces are suddenly held at \(T_{\text{w}}\): \(\partial T/\partial t = \alpha\,\partial^{2}T/\partial y^{2}\).
  2. Separation of variables with \(T = T_{\text{w}}\) at \(y = \pm H/2\) and uniform \(T_0\) at \(t = 0\) gives cosine modes \(\cos(k\pi y/H)\) with odd \(k = 2n+1\) and decay rates \(k^{2}\pi^{2}\alpha/H^{2}\); the Fourier coefficients of a uniform initial profile are \(4(-1)^{n}/(k\pi)\).
  3. After a short time only the first mode (\(n = 0\)) matters. At the centre \(y = 0\): \(\theta_{\text{c}} \approx (4/\pi)\,e^{-\pi^{2}\alpha t/H^{2}}\). Solve for \(t\): the expression on the right.
  4. The key scaling is \(t_{\text{c}}\propto H^{2}/\alpha\): halving the gap cools the core four times faster. The required die length is \(L \approx \bar u\,t_{\text{c}}\) — or longer, because the core actually moves at up to \(1.5\bar u\).
Assumptions and validity

Constant properties; perfect contact with a wall at coolant temperature (in reality there is a wall resistance and the melt may slip); no latent heat (no water evaporation, which is precisely what the die is designed to prevent). The one-term approximation is good for \(\theta_{\text{c}} \lesssim 0.8\).

\(H^{2}/\alpha\): m²/(m² s⁻¹) = s; the logarithm is dimensionless ✓
Worked example 4Sizing a cooling die

A pilot HME line produces 30 kg h⁻¹ of melt (ρ = 1100 kg m⁻³) at 140 °C. The cooling die has a rectangular channel 40 mm wide and 8 mm high, with coolant at 40 °C. The melt core must be below 90 °C at the exit. Estimate (a) the mean velocity and wall shear rate, (b) the cooling time, (c) the minimum die length. (d) What happens if the gap is doubled to 16 mm at the same throughput?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(\dot V = 30/3600/1100 = 7.58\times10^{-6}\) m³ s⁻¹. \(\bar u = 7.58\times10^{-6}/(0.040\times0.008) = 0.0237\) m s⁻¹. \(\dot\gamma_{\text{w}} = 6\times0.0237/0.008 = 17.8\) s⁻¹.
  2. (b) \(\theta_{\text{c}} = (90-40)/(140-40) = 0.50\). \(H^{2}/(\pi^{2}\alpha) = (0.008)^{2}/(9.87\times1.35\times10^{-7}) = 48.0\) s. \(\ln(4/(\pi\times0.5)) = \ln 2.546 = 0.935\). \(t_{\text{c}} = 44.9\) s.
  3. (c) \(L \ge \bar u\,t_{\text{c}} = 0.0237\times44.9 = 1.06\) m. Because the core moves faster than the mean (up to \(1.5\bar u\)), a conservative design would be ≈ 1.6 m.
  4. (d) Doubling H halves \(\bar u\) (0.0118 m s⁻¹) and quarters the wall shear rate (4.4 s⁻¹), but the cooling time quadruples (180 s): \(L = 0.0118\times180 = 2.1\) m. The die must be twice as long, and the lower shear may weaken fibre alignment.
  5. Sanity check. Units: m s⁻¹ × s = m ✓. The metre scale explains why HME cooling dies are so much longer than the short dies used for expanded (TVP) products, and why die design is a trade-off between gap (cooling time ∝ H²) and length.
ū ≈ 2.4 cm s⁻¹, γ̇w ≈ 18 s⁻¹, tc ≈ 45 s, L ≳ 1.1 m (≈ 2.1 m for a 16 mm gap)

Beyond the extruder: shear cells and 3D printing

An extruder mixes, heats, shears and cools in one continuous machine, which makes it productive but hard to understand: the shear history of each fluid element is different and poorly known. Researchers at Wageningen University developed the Couette shear cell as a model system with a well-defined flow. The protein mixture is placed in the gap between two concentric cylinders; the inner cylinder rotates while both are heated, applying simple shear at a controlled rate and temperature, and the cell is then cooled. Krintiras et al. (2015) showed that fibrous, meat-like structures could be produced from soy protein in this way, and the concept has been scaled up and compared systematically with high-moisture extrusion (Cornet et al., 2022).

Shear rate in a narrow-gap Couette cell(12.2.10)
\[ \dot\gamma \approx \frac{\omega R_{\text{i}}}{R_{\text{o}}-R_{\text{i}}} = \frac{2\pi N\,R_{\text{i}}}{60\,(R_{\text{o}}-R_{\text{i}})} \]

the shear rate in the gap is the speed of the moving inner wall divided by the gap width; with the rotation speed N in revolutions per minute, the angular velocity is two pi N over sixty.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\omega,\ N\)angular velocity; rotation speedrad s⁻¹; rpm
\(R_{\text{i}},\ R_{\text{o}}\)radii of the inner and outer cylinderm
Follow the derivation
  1. No slip: the material touching the inner cylinder moves with it at \(u = \omega R_{\text{i}}\); at the outer wall \(u = 0\).
  2. If the gap is narrow compared with the radius, curvature can be neglected and the flow is plane Couette flow with a linear profile.
  3. Shear rate = velocity difference / gap = \(\omega R_{\text{i}}/(R_{\text{o}}-R_{\text{i}})\). For wider gaps the exact Newtonian result at the inner wall is \(2\omega R_{\text{o}}^{2}/(R_{\text{o}}^{2}-R_{\text{i}}^{2})\).
(rad s⁻¹ × m)/m = s⁻¹ ✓

3D printing of meat analogues deposits protein pastes or gels layer by layer through a nozzle, allowing separate "muscle" and "fat" phases to be placed where they belong in a whole cut, and producing aligned structures along the print path (Dick et al., 2019). Its strengths are design freedom and personalisation; its weakness is throughput, which is orders of magnitude below that of an extruder. Hybrid approaches — extruded fibrous strands assembled by printing or lamination — combine both.

Frontier 2024–2026

Research is moving towards less refined ingredients (dry-fractionated concentrates, whole-pulse flours) for extrusion, towards predictive models of fibre formation that link ingredient phase behaviour to die design, and towards hybrid products that combine plant protein with small amounts of fermentation-derived or cultivated ingredients (Lessons 12.3 and 12.4) for flavour and fat.

Markets, companies and the plateau

Plant-based milk and meat alternatives grew rapidly in the 2010s. Sweden has a notable place in this history. Oatly was founded in 1994 by the food scientist Rickard Öste and his brother Björn, building on research at Lund University into enzymatic breakdown of oat starch — the enzymes convert the starch into sugars, giving a drinkable oat base without added sugar. The company is headquartered in Malmö with production and R&D in Landskrona, listed on the Nasdaq exchange in New York in May 2021 at a valuation of over US$10 billion, and reported revenue of US$722 million in 2022 together with a net loss of US$393 million. Swedish supermarkets also carry a wide range of plant-based meat alternatives from domestic and Nordic brands, many produced with extruded pea or soy protein.

After the rapid growth of 2019–2021, however, the plant-based meat segment stalled. The clearest public indicator is Beyond Meat, the US company whose 2019 stock-market debut was the best-performing IPO of a major US company since 2000: its revenue fell for several years in a row, to US$326 million in 2024 with a net loss of about US$160 million, and by the end of 2024 its total equity was negative; the company cut staff repeatedly and in 2025 rebranded itself as "Beyond" (Beyond Meat, 2025). Explanations offered by analysts and researchers include price premiums over meat, taste and texture that did not win repeat purchases, and consumer concern about long ingredient lists and "ultra-processing" (Lesson 12.1). Plant-based milks, where oat drinks are a Nordic success story, have been more resilient than meat analogues.

Reading market data critically

Market figures are published mostly by industry organisations, market-research firms and companies themselves, with different product definitions (retail only or including food service; value or volume; which countries). When you cite a number — "sales fell by x %" — check whether it is dollar sales (affected by inflation and price cuts) or volume, and for which segment.

Key terms

Protein concentrate / isolate
Protein ingredients with, conventionally, about 50–70 % (concentrate) or ≥ 80–90 % (isolate) protein on a dry basis.
Dry fractionation
Separation of flour into protein- and starch-rich fractions by fine milling and air classification, without water.
Air classifier
A device in which a rotating wheel and an air stream separate particles by size (and density) around a cut size.
Protein recovery
Fraction of the protein in the feed that ends up in the product stream of a separation.
pH-shift extraction
Alkaline solubilisation of protein followed by isoelectric precipitation, the main wet route to protein isolates.
Isoelectric point (pI)
The pH at which a protein carries zero net charge; solubility is minimal near it.
Techno-functional properties
Solubility, water and oil holding, gelation, emulsifying and foaming behaviour of an ingredient.
Anti-nutritional factor
A compound in food that reduces nutrient availability or has adverse effects, e.g. trypsin inhibitors, phytate, vicine.
Textured vegetable protein (TVP)
Dry, expanded product of low-moisture extrusion, rehydrated before use.
High-moisture extrusion (HME)
Extrusion at about 40–80 % moisture with a cooling die, producing dense, fibrous meat analogues.
Specific mechanical energy (SME)
Mechanical energy input per unit mass of extrudate (kJ kg⁻¹).
Cooling die
A long, cooled channel after the extruder in which the melt flows laminarly and solidifies into an anisotropic structure.
Couette shear cell
A device with concentric cylinders that applies well-defined simple shear and heat to a protein mixture.
