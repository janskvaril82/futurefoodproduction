Radiation physics: photons, PAR and PPFD

A lettuce plant does not care how many watts your lamp consumes, nor how bright it looks to you. It counts photons. This lesson builds, from first principles, the physics you need to measure light the way a plant experiences it — and to convert between the units that engineers, lamp manufacturers and plant physiologists use.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain light as electromagnetic radiation that is emitted and absorbed in discrete photons, and calculate photon energy from wavelength (LO2, LO3).
  • Distinguish radiometric (W m⁻²), quantum (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) and photometric (lux) units, and justify why quantum units are used for plants (LO2).
  • Define photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), photosynthetic photon flux (PPF) and photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD), and relate them to the McCree action spectrum (LO2).
  • Convert between energy and photon units for monochromatic and broadband light, including sunlight (LO3).
  • Describe how a quantum sensor measures PPFD and what limits its accuracy (LO4, LO5).

Why engineers must count photons

Every calorie you have ever eaten began as a photon captured by a chlorophyll molecule. In the field this photon supply is free, but in a controlled environment it is the single largest operating cost. In plant factories with artificial lighting, electric lighting typically accounts for roughly half of all electricity used, and almost all of the rest is spent removing the heat that the lamps produce (Kozai et al., 2020; Graamans et al., 2018). Understanding light quantitatively is therefore not a physiological nicety — it is the core of the energy balance of indoor food production.

To design lighting you must answer three questions precisely: How many photons does the crop need? How many photons does a lamp produce per joule of electricity? and How many of those photons reach the leaves? All three are questions about photons, not about watts or lux. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the equations; the next lessons apply them to daily light integrals, LED design and spectral quality.

Big idea

Photosynthesis is a photochemical process: each photon absorbed drives (at most) one electron-transfer event, regardless of the photon's energy within the photosynthetic range. That is why plant scientists measure light as a number of photons (in moles) rather than as energy.

Light as electromagnetic radiation

Light is an electromagnetic wave: coupled oscillating electric and magnetic fields travelling at \(c = 2.998\times10^{8}\) m s⁻¹ in vacuum. A wave is characterised by its wavelength \(\lambda\) (the distance between successive crests) and its frequency \(\nu\) (crests passing per second). Because every wave travels at the same speed in vacuum, wavelength and frequency are locked together:

Wave relation(4.1.1)
\[ c = \lambda\,\nu \]

the speed of light equals the wavelength multiplied by the frequency.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(c\)speed of light in vacuum, 2.998 × 10⁸m s⁻¹
\(\lambda\)wavelengthm (usually quoted in nm = 10⁻⁹ m)
\(\nu\)frequencys⁻¹ = Hz
Where does this come from?

A wave crest moves one wavelength in one period \(T\). Speed is distance over time: \(c=\lambda/T\). Frequency is the number of periods per second, \(\nu = 1/T\), so \(c=\lambda\nu\). In air, light travels about 0.03 % slower than in vacuum; in water about 25 % slower. The frequency stays the same, so the wavelength shortens. Horticultural wavelengths are conventionally quoted in air, but the difference is negligible for our purposes.

m × s⁻¹ = m s⁻¹ ✓

The electromagnetic spectrum runs from radio waves (kilometres) to gamma rays (picometres). Plants use a narrow window, roughly 400–700 nm, that almost coincides with human vision. This is no coincidence: it is where the Sun emits most of its energy that also penetrates the atmosphere and water, and where photons carry enough energy to excite electrons in pigments but not so much that they break chemical bonds.

Photons: light in packets

At the beginning of the twentieth century Planck and Einstein showed that light is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets, photons. The energy of one photon depends only on its frequency — and therefore on its wavelength:

Energy of a photon (Planck–Einstein relation)(4.1.2)
\[ E_{\text{ph}} = h\,\nu = \frac{h\,c}{\lambda} \]

the energy of one photon equals Planck's constant times its frequency, or equivalently Planck's constant times the speed of light divided by the wavelength. Shorter wavelength means more energy per photon.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(E_{\text{ph}}\)energy of a single photonJ≈ 3–5 × 10⁻¹⁹ J in PAR
\(h\)Planck constantJ s6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ (exact)
\(c\)speed of lightm s⁻¹2.998 × 10⁸ (exact)
\(\lambda\)wavelengthm400–700 × 10⁻⁹ in PAR
Follow the derivation
  1. Planck (1900) found that the spectrum of thermal radiation could only be explained if oscillators exchange energy in multiples of \(h\nu\).
  2. Einstein (1905) explained the photoelectric effect by proposing that light itself arrives in quanta of energy \(E=h\nu\): the energy of ejected electrons depends on the light's frequency, not on its intensity.
  3. Substituting the wave relation \(\nu = c/\lambda\) (Eq. 4.1.1) gives \(E = hc/\lambda\).
  4. A useful shortcut: \(hc = 1.986\times10^{-25}\) J m. With \(\lambda\) in nanometres, \(E_{\text{ph}} = 1.986\times10^{-16}/\lambda_{\text{nm}}\) J, or in electronvolts \(E_{\text{ph}} \approx 1240/\lambda_{\text{nm}}\) eV.
Assumptions and validity

Exact for a single photon in any medium (frequency does not change between media). "Wavelength" here is the vacuum wavelength.

\(\dfrac{\text{J s}\cdot \text{m s}^{-1}}{\text{m}} = \text{J}\) ✓

A blue photon at 450 nm carries 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; a red photon at 660 nm only 3.01 × 10⁻¹⁹ J — about 32 % less. Yet both can drive one electron through photosystem II. The extra energy of the blue photon is lost as heat within picoseconds as the excited chlorophyll relaxes to its lowest excited state. This single fact explains why red LEDs are the most efficient way to make photosynthetic photons from electricity (you will see this in Lesson 4.3).

Counting photons in moles

Photons are so numerous that we count them in moles, exactly as chemists count molecules. One mole of photons (historically called an einstein) is Avogadro's number of photons, \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\). Plant light levels are conveniently expressed in micromoles (µmol = 10⁻⁶ mol) per square metre per second.

Photons per joule at one wavelength(4.1.3)
\[ \frac{n_{\text{ph}}}{E} = \frac{1}{E_{\text{ph}}\,N_A}=\frac{\lambda}{h\,c\,N_A}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\mu\text{mol}}{\text{J}} \approx 0.008359\,\lambda_{\text{nm}} \]

the number of moles of photons contained in one joule of monochromatic light is the wavelength divided by the product of Planck's constant, the speed of light and Avogadro's number. In practical units: multiply the wavelength in nanometres by 0.008359 to get micromoles per joule.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(n_{\text{ph}}/E\)photons (in moles) per joule of radiant energymol J⁻¹
\(N_A\)Avogadro constant, 6.022 × 10²³mol⁻¹
\(\lambda_{\text{nm}}\)wavelength expressed in nanometresnm
Follow the derivation
  1. One joule of light at wavelength \(\lambda\) contains \(1/E_{\text{ph}} = \lambda/(hc)\) photons.
  2. Divide by Avogadro's number to convert a count of photons to moles: \(\lambda/(hcN_A)\) mol J⁻¹.
  3. Evaluate the constant: \(hcN_A = 6.626\times10^{-34}\times2.998\times10^{8}\times6.022\times10^{23} = 0.11963\) J m mol⁻¹.
  4. With \(\lambda\) in nm (× 10⁻⁹ m) and the result in µmol (× 10⁶): \(\dfrac{\lambda_{\text{nm}}\times10^{-9}}{0.11963}\times10^{6} = 0.008359\,\lambda_{\text{nm}}\) µmol J⁻¹.
  5. Check: at 550 nm, \(0.008359\times550 = 4.60\) µmol J⁻¹ — the origin of the often-quoted "4.6".
\(\dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{J s}\cdot\text{m s}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}} = \dfrac{\text{mol}}{\text{J}}\) ✓
Worked example 1Watts to micromoles for a red LED

A monochromatic 660 nm LED array delivers a radiant irradiance of 50 W m⁻² at the canopy. What is the PPFD?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Identify the conversion. For monochromatic light, Eq. 4.1.3 gives photons per joule: \(0.008359 \times 660 = 5.517\) µmol J⁻¹.
  2. Interpret irradiance. 50 W m⁻² means 50 J of radiant energy arrives on each square metre each second.
  3. Multiply. \(\text{PPFD} = 50\ \text{J m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\times5.517\ \mu\text{mol J}^{-1} = 275.9\ \mu\text{mol m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\).
  4. Sanity check. 276 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ is a typical PPFD for leafy greens in a vertical farm, so the answer is plausible.
PPFD ≈ 276 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹

Had the LED been blue (450 nm) at the same 50 W m⁻², the PPFD would be only \(50\times0.008359\times450 = 188\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — the same energy, 32 % fewer photons.

Three families of light units

Confusion about light units is the most common source of error in controlled-environment agriculture. There are three families, each answering a different question:

Table 1. Radiometric, quantum and photometric quantities.
QuestionFamilyFlux (whole source)Flux density (per area)Weighting
How much energy?Radiometricradiant flux, Wirradiance, W m⁻²none (every joule counts equally)
How many photons?Quantum (photon)photon flux, µmol s⁻¹ (PPF for 400–700 nm)photon flux density, µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (PPFD)every photon in the band counts equally
How bright to a human?Photometricluminous flux, lumen (lm)illuminance, lux (lx = lm m⁻²)human eye sensitivity \(V(\lambda)\), peak at 555 nm

The photometric family is designed for human vision. The eye's photopic sensitivity \(V(\lambda)\) peaks sharply in the green (555 nm) and is only about 4 % of the peak at 450 nm and 6 % at 660 nm. A red-and-blue LED that is superb for photosynthesis therefore looks dim to a lux meter. Conversely, a green-rich lamp looks bright but may be mediocre for plants. Lux must never be used to specify plant lighting.

Common misconception

"My lamp gives 30 000 lux, so it is as strong as the plant needs." The conversion from lux to PPFD depends entirely on the spectrum: about 54 lux per µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for sunlight, 70–80 for a cool-white LED, about 82 for high-pressure sodium — and as low as 8–20 for red/blue LEDs. Without the spectrum, a lux value tells you almost nothing about photosynthesis (Both et al., 2017).

Photosynthetically active radiation

In 1972 Keith McCree measured the photosynthetic rate of leaves from 22 crop species under narrow-band light from 350 to 750 nm. Plotted per incident photon, the response — the relative quantum efficiency (RQE) — is surprisingly flat across 400–700 nm: blue photons are about 70–75 % as effective as red ones, and green photons, often thought to be useless, are 75–90 % as effective (McCree, 1972a). Below 400 nm and above 700 nm efficiency falls steeply.

This led to the standard definition: photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) is radiation between 400 and 700 nm, and it is quantified as a photon flux — every photon in the band counted equally. The weighting by the McCree curve is deliberately not applied in standard PAR, because the curve varies between species and with measurement conditions; a flat photon count is robust and easy to measure.

Photosynthetic photon flux density(4.1.4)
\[ \text{PPFD} = \int_{400\,\text{nm}}^{700\,\text{nm}} \frac{E_\lambda(\lambda)\,\lambda}{h\,c\,N_A}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda \]

PPFD is the sum over all wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm of the spectral irradiance at each wavelength converted to photons by multiplying by the wavelength and dividing by \(hcN_A\).

SymbolMeaningUnit
PPFDphotosynthetic photon flux densitymol m⁻² s⁻¹ (quoted as µmol m⁻² s⁻¹)
\(E_\lambda(\lambda)\)spectral irradiance: irradiance per unit wavelengthW m⁻² nm⁻¹
\(\mathrm{d}\lambda\)small wavelength intervalnm
Follow the derivation
  1. Split the spectrum into narrow bands of width \(\Delta\lambda\). Each band carries irradiance \(E_\lambda\Delta\lambda\) (W m⁻²).
  2. Within a narrow band the light is effectively monochromatic, so Eq. 4.1.3 converts its energy to photons: \(E_\lambda\Delta\lambda\cdot\lambda/(hcN_A)\).
  3. Add all bands in 400–700 nm and let \(\Delta\lambda\to0\): the sum becomes the integral.
  4. In practice a spectroradiometer reports \(E_\lambda\) every 1 nm and the integral is a sum: \(\text{PPFD}\approx\sum_i E_{\lambda,i}\,\lambda_i\cdot 0.008359\,\Delta\lambda\) (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ with \(E_\lambda\) in W m⁻² nm⁻¹ and \(\lambda\) in nm).
Related quantities: YPFD, ePAR and PFD

YPFD (yield photon flux density) weights each photon by the McCree RQE, giving a closer estimate of expected photosynthesis for a given spectrum. ePAR (extended PAR, 400–750 nm) adds far-red photons, which Zhen and Bugbee (2020) showed are photosynthetically as effective as shorter wavelengths when combined with them (the Emerson enhancement). PFD (photon flux density) without a qualifier often means 300–800 nm or the whole measured range — always state the band.

\(\text{W m}^{-2}\text{nm}^{-1}\cdot\text{nm}\cdot\dfrac{\text{nm}}{\text{J nm mol}^{-1}}\): W = J s⁻¹, so the result is mol m⁻² s⁻¹ ✓

Converting broadband light: sunlight and lamps

For real light sources the conversion factor between energy and photons is a spectrum-weighted average of Eq. 4.1.3. Two numbers are worth memorising for sunlight:

  • Within PAR: 1 J of sunlight's 400–700 nm energy contains about 4.57 µmol of photons (McCree, 1972b).
  • Relative to total solar radiation (global shortwave, 300–3000 nm, what a pyranometer measures): only about 45 % of the energy lies in PAR, so 1 J of total solar radiation contains about \(0.45\times4.57\approx 2.0\)–2.1 µmol of PAR photons.
Sunlight: global radiation to PPFD(4.1.5)
\[ \text{PPFD} \approx f_{\text{PAR}}\;\kappa_{\text{PAR}}\;R_g \approx 0.45\times4.57\times R_g \approx 2.0\,R_g \]

the PPFD of sunlight in micromoles per square metre per second is about twice the global solar irradiance in watts per square metre.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(R_g\)global (total shortwave) solar irradianceW m⁻²0–1000
\(f_{\text{PAR}}\)fraction of solar energy in 400–700 nm–0.43–0.50
\(\kappa_{\text{PAR}}\)photons per joule within PAR for sunlightµmol J⁻¹4.57
Assumptions and validity

\(f_{\text{PAR}}\) is higher under overcast skies (diffuse light is bluer and water vapour absorbs more infrared) and lower at low sun angles. Uncertainty in the 2.0 factor is ±5–10 %. For greenhouse design, remember that glass transmits only 60–85 % of PAR, so inside PPFD ≈ 1.3–1.7 × outside \(R_g\).

(–) × (µmol J⁻¹) × (J m⁻² s⁻¹) = µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 2A sunny day in Västerås

At solar noon on a clear day in mid-June, a pyranometer at Västerås (59.6° N) reads 820 W m⁻². Estimate the PPFD outdoors and inside a glass greenhouse with 70 % PAR transmission. How does this compare with the 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ typically used for lettuce in a vertical farm?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Outdoor PPFD from Eq. 4.1.5: \(0.45\times4.57\times820 = 1686\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. (Full summer sunlight is usually quoted as ≈ 2000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at lower latitudes.)
  2. Inside the greenhouse: \(0.70\times1686 = 1180\) µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  3. Ratio to a vertical farm: \(1180/250 \approx 4.7\). At noon in June the greenhouse crop receives almost five times the photon flux of an indoor farm — for free.
  4. But the sun does not shine at noon all day, nor all year. What matters for growth is the integral over the day — the daily light integral — which is the subject of Lesson 4.2.
≈ 1690 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ outdoors; ≈ 1180 inside the greenhouse
Worked example 3How much energy is in a mole of photons?

A lettuce crop receives a daily light integral of 17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ from red LEDs at 660 nm. How much radiant energy (MJ m⁻² d⁻¹) is that? If the LEDs convert 50 % of their electrical input to radiation, how many kWh of electricity per square metre per day are needed?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Energy of one mole of 660 nm photons: \(N_A\,hc/\lambda = 0.11963/(660\times10^{-9}) = 1.813\times10^{5}\) J mol⁻¹ = 0.181 MJ mol⁻¹.
  2. Radiant energy per day: \(17\times0.181 = 3.08\) MJ m⁻² d⁻¹.
  3. Electrical energy at 50 % wall-plug efficiency: \(3.08/0.50 = 6.16\) MJ m⁻² d⁻¹.
  4. Convert to kWh (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ): \(6.16/3.6 = 1.71\) kWh m⁻² d⁻¹.
  5. Cross-check with photon efficacy: 50 % efficiency at 660 nm means \(0.5\times5.52 = 2.76\) µmol J⁻¹, and \(17\times10^6/2.76/(3.6\times10^6) = 1.71\) kWh ✓.
3.1 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹ of light; ≈ 1.7 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ of electricity

Measuring PAR: the quantum sensor

A quantum sensor (PAR meter) is a silicon photodiode behind optical filters and a diffuser. A photodiode produces a current proportional to the number of absorbed photons, weighted by its own spectral responsivity. Filters shape that responsivity to be as flat as possible from 400 to 700 nm and zero outside, mimicking the definition of PAR. The diffuser gives a cosine response: light arriving at angle \(\theta\) is weighted by \(\cos\theta\), as it is for a horizontal leaf.

Sources of error you must consider when you measure PPFD in your project:

  • Spectral error — mismatch between the sensor response and the ideal box, largest for narrow-band LEDs.
  • Cosine error — imperfect diffusers under-read light at low angles; important in greenhouses with diffuse light.
  • Calibration drift — sensors age; typical recalibration interval is two years; stated accuracy ±5 %.
  • Positioning — PPFD varies strongly over short distances under LEDs (see the lighting lab). Always measure on a grid at canopy height and report the mean and the uniformity.

From instantaneous PPFD to the daily light integral

PPFD is an instantaneous rate. Plants respond to the total number of photons received over a day. Integrating PPFD over time gives the daily light integral:

Daily light integral (constant PPFD)(4.1.6)
\[ \text{DLI} = \frac{\text{PPFD}\times t_{\text{photo}}\times 3600}{10^{6}} \]

the daily light integral in moles per square metre per day equals the PPFD in micromoles per square metre per second, times the photoperiod in hours, times 3600 seconds per hour, divided by one million micromoles per mole.

SymbolMeaningUnit
DLIdaily light integralmol m⁻² d⁻¹
\(t_{\text{photo}}\)photoperiod (hours of light per day)h d⁻¹
µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ × h d⁻¹ × s h⁻¹ ÷ µmol mol⁻¹ = mol m⁻² d⁻¹ ✓

Lettuce typically needs a DLI of 12–17 mol m⁻² d⁻¹; tomatoes 20–30. In Lesson 4.2 you will extend this to variable sunlight and calculate supplementary lighting needs for a Swedish greenhouse in winter.

Key terms

Photon
The smallest discrete amount (quantum) of electromagnetic radiation; energy \(E=hc/\lambda\).
PAR
Photosynthetically active radiation: radiation in the 400–700 nm band, quantified as photon flux.
PPF
Photosynthetic photon flux: total PAR photons emitted by a source per second (µmol s⁻¹).
PPFD
Photosynthetic photon flux density: PAR photons arriving per unit area per second (µmol m⁻² s⁻¹).
Irradiance
Radiant power per unit area (W m⁻²); a radiometric (energy) quantity.
Illuminance
Luminous flux per unit area (lux), weighted by human eye sensitivity; not suitable for plants.
Relative quantum efficiency
Photosynthesis per incident photon at a wavelength, relative to the maximum (McCree curve).
ePAR
Extended PAR, 400–750 nm, including far-red photons that contribute to photosynthesis in combination with shorter wavelengths.
Quantum sensor
A filtered, cosine-corrected silicon photodiode calibrated to read PPFD.
DLI
Daily light integral: PAR photons received per unit area per day (mol m⁻² d⁻¹).
