Biomass and precision fermentation

In two 150-cubic-metre fermenters at Billingham in north-east England, a soil fungus doubles its mass roughly every four hours and delivers about 300 kilograms of biomass per hour — the raw material of Quorn. No field and no animal are involved. Microbes are the fastest protein producers we know, but they do not grow on good intentions: every kilogram of cells consumes sugar or gas, oxygen and electricity, and releases heat that must be pumped away. This lesson builds the engineering toolkit of fermentation — growth kinetics, yields, reactor operation, oxygen and heat transfer, scale-up and purification — and then uses it to judge mycoprotein, gas-fermented "air protein" and precision-fermented dairy and egg proteins as they stand in 2026.

Three kinds of fermentation

Humans have used microbes to make food for millennia — bread, beer, wine, cheese, yoghurt, soy sauce, miso and tempeh are all products of microbial metabolism. In everyday biochemistry "fermentation" means energy metabolism without oxygen, as when yeast turns sugar into ethanol. In bioprocess engineering the word has a much wider meaning: any controlled cultivation of microorganisms in a vessel, and most of the fermentations in this lesson are, in fact, intensely aerobic. For the protein transition it helps to distinguish three kinds (Figure 1, Table 1).

  • Traditional fermentation — the microbe is the cook. It transforms a food raw material (soybeans, milk, cereals) into a different food with new texture, flavour, shelf life or digestibility. The microbial biomass is a small part of the product.
  • Biomass fermentation — the microbe is the food. Fast-growing bacteria, yeasts, filamentous fungi or microalgae are grown to high density and the cells themselves are harvested. Historically this was called single-cell protein (SCP); today's examples include mycoprotein (Quorn), fungal proteins grown on industrial side streams and bacterial protein grown on hydrogen and CO₂.
  • Precision fermentation — the microbe is the factory. A host organism (typically a yeast such as Komagataella phaffii or a filamentous fungus such as Trichoderma reesei) carries a gene for one specific molecule — an enzyme, a milk or egg protein, a fat or a flavour — secretes it into the broth, and the molecule is purified. The cells are removed, so the ingredient contains no living genetically modified organisms.

Big idea

A microbe can be the cook (traditional fermentation), the food (biomass fermentation) or the factory (precision fermentation). In all three cases the biology is fast and land-light, and the limits are set by engineering: how quickly we can supply substrate and oxygen, remove heat, and separate the product.

Common misconception

"Fermentation happens without oxygen." That is true of the ethanol and lactic-acid fermentations of biochemistry textbooks. Industrial protein fermentations are almost all aerobic, and supplying oxygen fast enough is usually the hardest part of the process (Section 12.3.5).

Growth kinetics: from exponential growth to Monod

Put a few cells into a sterile, well-mixed medium and measure the biomass concentration \(X\) (grams of dry cells per litre) over time. On a logarithmic axis the result has a characteristic shape (Figure 2): a lag phase while the cells adapt; an exponential phase in which \(\ln X\) rises along a straight line; a deceleration phase as the growth-limiting substrate runs out; a stationary phase; and eventually death or lysis.

In the exponential phase every cell divides at the same average rate, so the rate at which biomass is formed is proportional to the biomass already present. The proportionality constant is the specific growth rate \(\mu\) — the growth rate per unit of biomass.

Exponential growth and doubling time(12.3.1)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mu X \quad\Rightarrow\quad X(t) = X_0\,e^{\mu t},\qquad t_{\mathrm{d}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\mu} \]

the rate of increase of biomass equals the specific growth rate times the biomass; integrated, the biomass grows exponentially from its starting value, and the time needed to double it is the natural logarithm of two divided by the specific growth rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. Define \(\mu = \dfrac{1}{X}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t}\): the biomass formed per unit of existing biomass per unit time.
  2. If \(\mu\) is constant (all nutrients in excess), separate the variables: \(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}X}{X} = \mu\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. Integrate from \((0, X_0)\) to \((t, X)\): \(\ln X - \ln X_0 = \mu t\), i.e. \(X = X_0 e^{\mu t}\). A plot of \(\ln X\) against \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(\mu\) — the way \(\mu\) is measured.
  4. Doubling: set \(X = 2X_0\): \(\ln 2 = \mu t_{\mathrm{d}}\), so \(t_{\mathrm{d}} = \ln 2/\mu \approx 0.693/\mu\).
  5. The number of doublings needed to multiply the biomass by a factor \(R\) is \(n = \log_2 R = \ln R/\ln 2\), taking \(n\,t_{\mathrm{d}}\) hours.
Assumptions and validity

Valid only while \(\mu\) is constant: every nutrient in excess, no inhibition, constant temperature and pH. It breaks down in the lag phase and as soon as a substrate becomes limiting (Eq. 12.3.2) or oxygen transfer cannot keep up (Eq. 12.3.7). Cell death and maintenance are neglected.

\(\mu\): (g L⁻¹ h⁻¹)/(g L⁻¹) = h⁻¹ ✓ · \(\mu t\) is dimensionless, as the argument of an exponential must be ✓ · \(t_{\mathrm{d}}\): 1/h⁻¹ = h ✓

Exponential growth cannot last. As the limiting substrate \(S\) — usually the carbon and energy source — runs low, the cells cannot take it up fast enough and \(\mu\) falls. In 1949 Jacques Monod showed that the specific growth rate of bacteria depends on the concentration of the limiting substrate in the same saturating way that the rate of an enzyme reaction depends on substrate concentration (Monod, 1949).

Monod equation(12.3.2)
\[ \mu = \mu_{\max}\,\frac{S}{K_S + S} \]

the specific growth rate equals its maximum value times the substrate concentration divided by the sum of the half-saturation constant and the substrate concentration. When the substrate concentration equals the half-saturation constant, the cells grow at half their maximum rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. A cell takes up the limiting substrate through membrane transporters. A transporter behaves like an enzyme: it binds the substrate, carries it across and releases it. With a finite number of transporters, the specific uptake rate saturates: \(q_S = q_{S,\max}\,S/(K + S)\) (the Michaelis–Menten form met in Lesson 2.3 for root nutrient uptake).
  2. If each gram of substrate taken up yields a fixed mass of new cells, \(Y_{X/S}\) (Eq. 12.3.3), the growth rate is proportional to the uptake rate: \(\mu = Y_{X/S}\,q_S\).
  3. Combining: \(\mu = Y_{X/S}q_{S,\max}\,S/(K+S)\). Define \(\mu_{\max} = Y_{X/S}q_{S,\max}\) and \(K_S = K\) to obtain Eq. 12.3.2.
  4. Limits: for \(S \gg K_S\), \(\mu \to \mu_{\max}\) (zero-order in \(S\): all transporters busy). For \(S \ll K_S\), \(\mu \approx (\mu_{\max}/K_S)\,S\) (first order). At \(S = K_S\), \(\mu = \mu_{\max}/2\) — the meaning of the half-saturation constant.
  5. To estimate the parameters, measure \(\mu\) at several \(S\) (for example in chemostats, Section 12.3.4) and fit Eq. 12.3.2 by non-linear least squares (Lesson 10.1), or plot \(1/\mu\) against \(1/S\): the intercept is \(1/\mu_{\max}\) and the slope \(K_S/\mu_{\max}\) (the Lineweaver–Burk linearisation, which is simple but magnifies errors at low \(S\)).
Assumptions and validity

Monod's equation is empirical: the derivation above shows why its shape is plausible, not that it is exact. It assumes one limiting substrate, balanced growth and no inhibition. Real cultures show substrate inhibition at high concentrations, product inhibition (e.g., ethanol), dual limitation (sugar and oxygen — used in the simulator of Figure 3, where \(\mu\) is multiplied by \(C/(K_O+C)\)) and a lag after sudden changes. Because \(K_S\) for sugars is so small, a batch culture grows at practically \(\mu_{\max}\) until the sugar is almost exhausted — which is why the growth curve of Figure 2 bends so abruptly.

Yields, oxygen demand and productivity

Cells are made from their substrates, so biomass formation and substrate consumption are tied by stoichiometry. The engineer's shorthand for this is the yield coefficient: grams of biomass formed per gram of substrate consumed. For aerobic growth on glucose it is typically about 0.4–0.5 g g⁻¹ (Doran, 2013): about half of the sugar's carbon ends up in cells, the rest is respired to CO₂ to provide the energy for biosynthesis.

Yield coefficient, batch mass balance and productivity(12.3.3)
\[ Y_{X/S} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}S},\qquad X_{\mathrm{f}} = X_0 + Y_{X/S}\left(S_0 - S_{\mathrm{f}}\right),\qquad Q_X = \frac{X_{\mathrm{f}} - X_0}{t_{\mathrm{b}} + t_{\mathrm{ta}}} \]

the biomass yield is the biomass formed per unit of substrate consumed. In a batch the final biomass equals the initial biomass plus the yield times the substrate consumed, and the volumetric productivity equals the biomass produced divided by the batch time plus the turnaround time between batches.

Follow the derivation
  1. Over a small time interval the culture consumes \(-\mathrm{d}S\) grams of substrate per litre and forms \(\mathrm{d}X\) grams of cells. Their ratio defines \(Y_{X/S}\) (the minus sign makes it positive, because \(S\) decreases).
  2. If \(Y_{X/S}\) is constant, integrate from the start to the end of the batch: \(X_{\mathrm{f}} - X_0 = Y_{X/S}(S_0 - S_{\mathrm{f}})\). Since \(S_{\mathrm{f}}\approx 0\), \(X_{\mathrm{f}}\approx X_0 + Y_{X/S}S_0\): the substrate you put in decides how much biomass you get out.
  3. With Eq. 12.3.1 (growth at \(\mu_{\max}\) almost to the end), the batch time is \(t_{\mathrm{b}} \approx \ln(X_{\mathrm{f}}/X_0)/\mu_{\max}\) (plus the lag).
  4. A plant earns money per cycle, so productivity is the product formed divided by the whole cycle time, turnaround included.
Assumptions and validity

The yield is not a constant of nature: it falls at low growth rates because part of the substrate is burned for maintenance (keeping gradients, repairing macromolecules), and it falls dramatically under overflow metabolism — for example when yeast at high sugar concentration makes ethanol even in the presence of oxygen (the Crabtree effect). The yields quoted here are "observed" yields for aerobic, respiring growth.

\(Y_{X/S}\): (g L⁻¹)/(g L⁻¹) = g g⁻¹ ✓ · \(Q_X\): (g L⁻¹)/h = g L⁻¹ h⁻¹ ✓

Aerobic organisms also consume oxygen, and here stoichiometry gives us something powerful. Every oxidation–reduction is a transfer of electrons. The electrons available in the substrate either end up in new biomass or are passed to oxygen. Counting electrons — with the degree of reduction \(\gamma\), the number of available electrons per carbon atom — lets us calculate the oxygen demand from the biomass yield alone (Villadsen et al., 2011; Doran, 2013).

Oxygen demand from an electron balance(12.3.4)
\[ Y_{O/X} = \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\gamma_S}{Y'_{X/S}} - \gamma_X\right),\qquad Y_{X/O} = \frac{M_X}{M_{\mathrm{O_2}}\,Y_{O/X}} \]

the moles of oxygen consumed per C-mole of biomass formed equal one quarter of the electrons supplied by the substrate per C-mole of biomass, minus the electrons locked up in the biomass itself. The biomass yield on oxygen, in grams per gram, follows by converting moles to grams.

  2. Electron balance per C-mole of biomass. To make one C-mole of cells the culture consumes \(1/Y'_{X/S}\) C-moles of glucose, supplying \(\gamma_S/Y'_{X/S}\) moles of electrons.   3. Numbers. \(Y_{X/S} = 0.5\) g g⁻¹ gives \(Y' = 0.5\times30.0/24.6 = 0.610\). Then \(Y_{O/X} = (4/0.610 - 4.2)/4 = (6.56 - 4.20)/4 = 0.590\) mol O₂ per C-mol.

  5. Check the limit. If all the substrate electrons ended in biomass, \(Y' = \gamma_S/\gamma_X = 0.952\) and no oxygen would be needed — the thermodynamic ceiling on the yield.

Batch, fed-batch and continuous culture

A fermenter can be run in three basic ways (Figure 4). In a batch everything is added at the start and nothing is removed until harvest. In a fed-batch a concentrated feed is added during the run, so that the growth rate — and with it oxygen demand and heat release — can be controlled; the volume increases until the vessel is full. In a continuous culture (a chemostat) fresh medium flows in and broth flows out at the same rate, so the volume stays constant and the culture can, in principle, run indefinitely at steady state.

The chemostat is where Monod kinetics become a design tool. Because the outflow removes cells, the culture must grow exactly as fast as it is diluted to keep a constant population. The operator does not set the growth rate directly — the cells find it themselves, by lowering the substrate concentration until Monod's equation gives \(\mu = D\).

Chemostat steady state and washout(12.3.5)
\[ \mu = D,\qquad \bar S = \frac{K_S\,D}{\mu_{\max} - D},\qquad \bar X = Y_{X/S}\left(S_R - \bar S\right),\qquad D_{\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{\mu_{\max}\,S_R}{K_S + S_R} \]

at steady state the specific growth rate equals the dilution rate; the residual substrate is the half-saturation constant times the dilution rate divided by the difference between the maximum growth rate and the dilution rate; the biomass is the yield times the substrate consumed; and if the dilution rate exceeds a critical value the cells are washed out.

Follow the derivation
  1. Biomass balance (accumulation = growth − outflow; sterile feed): \(V\,\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = \mu XV - FX\). Divide by \(V\): \(\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = (\mu - D)X\).
  2. Steady state with cells present (\(X \ne 0\)): \(\mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}t = 0 \Rightarrow \mu = D\). The dilution rate sets the growth rate.
  3. Residual substrate. Insert \(\mu = D\) in Monod (Eq. 12.3.2): \(D = \mu_{\max}\bar S/(K_S + \bar S)\). Solve: \(\bar S = K_SD/(\mu_{\max} - D)\). Remarkably, \(\bar S\) does not depend on the feed concentration.
  4. Substrate balance: \(V\,\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = F(S_R - S) - \mu XV/Y_{X/S}\). At steady state, with \(\mu = D\): \(D(S_R - \bar S) = D\bar X/Y_{X/S}\), so \(\bar X = Y_{X/S}(S_R - \bar S)\).
  5. Washout. \(\bar X > 0\) requires \(\bar S < S_R\), i.e. \(K_SD/(\mu_{\max} - D) < S_R\), which gives \(D < D_{\mathrm{crit}} = \mu_{\max}S_R/(K_S + S_R)\). Above it, cells leave faster than they can grow, whatever the operator does.
  6. Maximum productivity. The biomass output is \(D\bar X = DY_{X/S}\left(S_R - K_SD/(\mu_{\max} - D)\right)\). Setting its derivative to zero gives \(D_{\mathrm{opt}} = \mu_{\max}\left(1 - \sqrt{K_S/(K_S + S_R)}\right)\) — typically just below \(D_{\mathrm{crit}}\), which is why productive chemostats run uncomfortably close to washout.
Assumptions and validity

Ideal mixing, a sterile feed, a constant yield and no maintenance, no wall growth and no mutation. Long continuous runs select for mutants that grow faster under the imposed conditions — the reason why Quorn production runs typically last about 1,000 h: highly branched ("colonial") mutants appear after roughly 250–340 generations and spoil the texture (Wiebe, 2002).

Oxygen transfer and heat removal: the physical limits

Oxygen is the most awkward nutrient of all. It dissolves poorly — air-saturated water at 30 °C holds only 7.6 mg L⁻¹, or 0.24 mmol L⁻¹ — while an actively growing culture consumes tens of millimoles per litre per hour. The dissolved oxygen in the broth would be exhausted within seconds if it were not replenished continuously from gas bubbles. That is the job of the stirred-tank bioreactor (Figure 6): a sparger releases gas bubbles at the bottom; impellers break them up and circulate the broth; baffles prevent the liquid from simply swirling; a jacket and internal coils carry away heat.

Oxygen transfer rate and the oxygen-limited biomass concentration(12.3.7)
\[ \text{OTR} = k_La\left(C^{*} - C\right),\qquad \text{OUR} = \frac{\mu X}{Y_{X/O}},\qquad X_{\max} = \frac{k_La\left(C^{*} - C_{\mathrm{crit}}\right)Y_{X/O}}{\mu} \]

the oxygen transfer rate equals the volumetric mass-transfer coefficient times the difference between the saturation and the actual dissolved-oxygen concentration. The oxygen uptake rate equals the growth rate times the biomass, divided by the yield on oxygen. Setting the two equal, with the dissolved oxygen at its lowest acceptable value, gives the largest biomass concentration the reactor can keep growing at rate μ.

Hydrogen-oxidising ("knallgas") bacteria grow on H₂, CO₂ and O₂ — three gases, two of them sparingly soluble, and a mixture that is explosive over a wide range of compositions. Gas fermentation therefore doubles the mass-transfer problem of Eq. 12.3.7 and adds a safety problem; reactors are designed to keep the gas composition outside the flammability limits and to maximise gas–liquid contact, for example in tall bubble columns or loop reactors, or under elevated pressure (which raises \(C^{*}\)).

Scale-up: why a 100 m³ tank is not a big shake flask

A process developed in a 10 L laboratory fermenter must eventually run at 10–500 m³. Scaling up with geometric similarity — all dimensions multiplied by the same factor — keeps the shape but not the physics: surface-to-volume ratios fall, circulation paths lengthen and the energy dissipated per volume changes in ways that depend on how the stirrer speed is chosen. It is impossible to keep all relevant quantities constant at once, so engineers choose the criterion that matters most for their process (Garcia-Ochoa & Gomez, 2009; Doran, 2013).

Impeller power and scale-up relations(12.3.10)
\[ P = N_p\,\rho\,N^{3}D_i^{5},\qquad \frac{P}{V}\propto N^{3}D_i^{2},\qquad v_{\mathrm{tip}} = \pi N D_i,\qquad \mathit{Re} = \frac{\rho N D_i^{2}}{\eta},\qquad t_{\mathrm{m}}\propto\frac{1}{N} \]

in turbulent flow the power drawn by an impeller equals the power number times the liquid density times the stirrer speed cubed times the impeller diameter to the fifth power; for geometrically similar vessels the power per volume therefore scales with speed cubed times diameter squared. The tip speed is pi times speed times diameter, the Reynolds number is density times speed times diameter squared over viscosity, and the mixing time is inversely proportional to the speed.

Downstream processing: from broth to food

A fermenter delivers a dilute suspension: 1–2 % solids for mycoprotein, a few grams per litre of secreted protein for precision fermentation. Downstream processing (DSP) turns this into a safe, stable food ingredient (Figure 8). The two routes differ fundamentally: in biomass fermentation the cells are the product, so DSP must make them edible; in precision fermentation the cells are the waste, so DSP must remove them and purify one molecule.

Nucleic acids. Fast-growing cells are full of ribosomes, and ribosomes are made of RNA: nucleic acids make up about 7–10 % of fungal and 8–12 % of bacterial dry matter, compared with 3–8 % in microalgae (Ritala et al., 2017). Humans break purines down to uric acid, and a high intake can raise plasma uric acid enough to cause gout and kidney stones; microbial biomass intended as a staple food therefore needs RNA reduction. In the mycoprotein process the biomass is heated to above 68 °C (optimum 72–74 °C) for 30–45 minutes, which activates endogenous ribonucleases; RNA falls from about 10 % to below 2 % of the dry mass, at the cost of losing 30–33 % of the biomass (Wiebe, 2002). If the RNA is degraded in a first-order process, \(w(t) = w_0e^{-k_dt}\), the reported reduction by a factor of five in 30–45 min implies \(k_d = \ln 5/(30\text{--}45\ \text{min}) \approx 0.04\)–0.05 min⁻¹ — a useful rule for sizing the holding tank.

Membranes. For secreted proteins, the workhorse is ultrafiltration: a membrane retains the protein and lets water, salts and sugars pass. To wash out small molecules without diluting the product, water is added at the same rate as permeate is removed — constant-volume diafiltration. A solute balance on the retentate gives

\[ \frac{c}{c_0} = \exp\!\left[-N_D\,(1-\sigma)\right] \qquad (12.3.11) \]

where \(N_D\) is the number of "diavolumes" (volume of wash water divided by retentate volume) and \(\sigma\) the rejection coefficient of the membrane for that solute (0 for a freely passing salt, ≈ 1 for the protein). Five diavolumes remove \(1 - e^{-5} = 99.3\) % of a small molecule while keeping 95 % of a protein with \(\sigma = 0.99\). (Derivation: \(V\,\mathrm{d}c = -(1-\sigma)c\,\mathrm{d}V_{\mathrm{p}}\) with \(\mathrm{d}N_D = \mathrm{d}V_{\mathrm{p}}/V\); integrate.) Every extra purification step costs yield, water and energy — the same trade-off between purity and recovery met for plant proteins in Lesson 12.2.

GMO or not?

A protein made by a genetically modified yeast is regulated in the EU as a novel food, not as a genetically modified food, provided the final product contains no viable GM cells and no recombinant DNA. Biomass from a GM organism would be a GM food. See Lessons 14.1 and 14.2.

Case studies: mycoprotein, air protein and precision proteins (as of 2026)

Mycoprotein: a forty-year overnight success

In the 1960s, fears of a world protein shortage led the British company Rank Hovis McDougall to screen about 3,000 fungi for a palatable protein source that could be grown on starch-derived glucose. Fusarium venenatum A3/5 (first identified as F. graminearum) was chosen, tested for twelve years and approved for sale by the UK Ministry of Agriculture, Fisheries and Food in 1984; the first product, a savoury pie, appeared in 1985 (Wiebe, 2002; Trinci, 1992). Its secret is texture: the fungal hyphae, when aligned with a binder (egg albumin in most products), resemble muscle fibres. Mycoprotein contains about 44 % protein on a dry basis, with all indispensable amino acids and a relatively low sulfur-amino-acid content, plus dietary fibre from chitin and glucans in the cell walls (Wiebe, 2002; Finnigan et al., 2019).

Air protein: what does it cost in electricity and land?

Gas fermentation replaces the sugar crop with electricity: water is split into H₂ and O₂, CO₂ is captured from air or industrial exhaust, and bacteria assemble protein from the gases. Leger et al. (2021) followed the energy through this chain and found that, per unit of land, photovoltaic-driven microbial protein could yield more than ten times the protein of soybean (≈ 115 g protein m⁻² a⁻¹), because solar panels capture sunlight more efficiently than photosynthesis and bacteria contain more protein than seeds. Life-cycle studies of hydrogen-oxidising bacteria in Finland agree that the electricity source dominates the footprint: using hydropower instead of the average Finnish mix lowered the carbon footprint by 87.5 % (Järviö et al., 2021; see also Sillman et al., 2019).

What would such land efficiency mean globally? Humpenöder et al. (2022) — a team from the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research and the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences — used the global land-use model MAgPIE to simulate microbial protein made in bioreactors from sugar (like mycoprotein) replacing ruminant meat on a protein basis. Replacing 20 % of per-capita ruminant meat consumption by 2050 offset the projected growth in pasture area and cut annual deforestation and the associated CO₂ emissions roughly in half. Methane and nitrous-oxide emissions, agricultural water use and nitrogen fixation fell roughly linearly with further substitution, but the land-saving benefit saturated: at 50 % and 80 % substitution, each additional percentage point saved less forest. The result is a warning against extrapolating single-product LCAs linearly to whole food systems.

Precision fermentation: proteins without the animal

Precision fermentation is older than it sounds. In 1990 the US FDA affirmed fermentation-produced chymosin — the milk-clotting enzyme traditionally extracted from calf stomachs — as generally recognised as safe; it was the first food ingredient made with recombinant DNA technology to be approved, and it is now used for much of the world's cheese. The new wave targets the animal proteins themselves. The FDA issued a "no questions" letter in 2020 for Perfect Day's β-lactoglobulin (the main whey protein) produced by Trichoderma reesei, and in 2025 for Onego Bio's ovalbumin. Europe is slower: as of 2026 no animal protein produced by precision fermentation has been authorised as a novel food in the EU. Perfect Day's 2022 dossier was declared not valid in April 2024 before any risk assessment began, and Remilk withdrew its β-lactoglobulin application; human-milk oligosaccharides produced by fermentation, by contrast, have been authorised since the late 2010s (Lesson 14.1).

Careful with company news

Fermentation start-ups announce factories, approvals and partnerships faster than any textbook can follow, and several — Mycorena among them — have failed between the announcement and the factory. Treat company press releases as claims, check the regulator's own register (EFSA, FDA's GRAS inventory, the Singapore Food Agency) and note the date: the statements in this section are "as of 2026".

Frontier 2024–2026

Three developments to watch: biomass fermentation on side streams (forest sugars, brewers' spent grain) rather than refined glucose, which lowers land use but raises safety questions about the feedstock; gas and electricity-based routes that decouple protein from photosynthesis altogether; and hybrid products that combine plant protein with small amounts of fermentation-derived proteins or fats for taste and function (Lesson 12.2). Whether any of them becomes a staple food will depend less on biology than on energy prices, capital costs and regulators.

Key terms

Biomass fermentation
Cultivation of microorganisms whose cells are themselves the food product, e.g. mycoprotein or bacterial single-cell protein.
Precision fermentation
Use of a (usually genetically engineered) microbial host to produce and secrete one specific molecule, which is then purified from the broth.
Specific growth rate (μ)
Rate of biomass formation per unit of biomass present (h⁻¹); the doubling time is ln 2/μ.
Monod equation
Empirical saturating relation between specific growth rate and the concentration of the limiting substrate: μ = μmaxS/(KS + S).
Half-saturation constant (KS)
Substrate concentration at which the specific growth rate is half its maximum.
Yield coefficient
Mass of product (e.g., biomass) formed per mass of substrate (sugar, oxygen) consumed, e.g. YX/S in g g⁻¹.
Degree of reduction
Number of available electrons per carbon atom of a compound, used in electron balances to calculate oxygen demand.
Chemostat
Continuous culture with equal inflow and outflow, in which the steady-state specific growth rate equals the dilution rate.
Washout
Loss of the culture from a continuous reactor when the dilution rate exceeds the critical value, slightly below μmax.
Fed-batch culture
Culture in which a concentrated feed is added during the run, without outflow, to control growth rate, oxygen demand and heat release.
kLa
Volumetric mass-transfer coefficient: product of the liquid-film coefficient and the gas–liquid interfacial area per volume; OTR = kLa(C* − C).
Heat of fermentation
Metabolic heat released by a culture, about 460 kJ per mole of O₂ consumed in aerobic processes.
Scale-up criterion
The quantity (power per volume, tip speed, kLa, mixing time) kept constant when a process is transferred to a larger vessel.
Mycoprotein
Food made from the RNA-reduced mycelium of the fungus Fusarium venenatum, sold as Quorn since 1985.
Diafiltration
Membrane washing in which water is added at the rate permeate is removed, to wash small solutes out of a retained product.
