Psychrometrics: humidity, VPD and dehumidification

A hectare of tomatoes pumps thirty tonnes of water into the air on a bright summer day. That water is invisible, but it carries as much energy as a large gas boiler, it decides whether leaves stay dry or grow grey mould, and in a plant factory it can be collected again as pure water from the cooling coil. Psychrometrics — the thermodynamics of moist air — is the language in which all of this is calculated. In this lesson you will derive its equations, learn to read the psychrometric chart like a map, and use it to design humidity control for greenhouses and plant factories.

What you will be able to do

  • Apply Dalton's law and the ideal-gas law to moist air, and calculate vapour pressure, relative humidity, absolute humidity, humidity ratio, dew point, wet-bulb temperature, enthalpy and specific volume (LO2, LO3).
  • Derive the Magnus–Tetens equation for saturation vapour pressure from the Clausius–Clapeyron relation and explain its fitted constants (LO3).
  • Read and construct a psychrometric chart and trace sensible heating, cooling with dehumidification, evaporative cooling and mixing on it (LO3).
  • Calculate the vapour pressure deficit at air and leaf temperature and relate it to target ranges for crops (LO2).
  • Quantify transpiration as a latent load and compare the energy cost of dehumidification by ventilation, condensation and heat pumps (LO3, LO7).
  • Predict condensation on covers and leaves, explain its link to Botrytis, and estimate water recovery from condensate in plant factories (LO2, LO7).

The invisible half of the climate

Ask a grower what the climate in her greenhouse is and she will give you two numbers: temperature and humidity. Temperature is easy to feel; humidity is not, yet it is at least as important. It controls how fast the crop transpires, and therefore how fast it pulls water and calcium from the roots. It controls whether water condenses on the leaves at dawn, and therefore the risk of fungal disease. It controls how much energy leaves the greenhouse through the vents, because humid air carries latent heat. And in a sealed plant factory, where the crop's transpiration is the main source of humidity, the air-conditioner must remove almost as much latent heat as sensible heat.

The branch of thermodynamics that deals with mixtures of dry air and water vapour is called psychrometrics (from the Greek psychros, cold — the classic humidity instrument, the psychrometer, measures the cooling of a wet thermometer). It rests on three pieces of physics you already know: the ideal-gas law, Dalton's law of partial pressures and the latent heat of vaporisation. From them follow a handful of equations and one extraordinarily useful graph, the psychrometric chart.

This lesson builds on the energy balance of Lesson 8.1, where transpiration appeared as the term λE. Here we open that term up, and on the way we meet the vapour pressure deficit (VPD) that you know from plant physiology (Lesson 2.2) as the driving force of transpiration.

Big idea

Moist air has two "temperatures of water": how much vapour it contains (vapour pressure, humidity ratio, dew point) and how much it could contain at its temperature (saturation vapour pressure). Relative humidity is their ratio; VPD is their difference. Heating air leaves the first unchanged but raises the second — that single fact explains most of greenhouse humidity control.

Moist air as a mixture of ideal gases

Air at the pressures and temperatures of a greenhouse behaves almost exactly as an ideal gas, and so does the water vapour in it. John Dalton observed in 1801 that in a mixture of gases each component exerts the pressure it would exert if it alone filled the volume, and the total pressure is the sum of these partial pressures. For moist air that gives the first equation of psychrometrics.

Dalton's law and the vapour density (absolute humidity)(8.2.1)
\[ P = p_a + e, \qquad \rho_v = \frac{e}{R_v\,T} = \frac{e\,M_w}{R\,T} \;\approx\; 2165\,\frac{e_{\,\text{kPa}}}{T_{\,\text{K}}}\ \ \text{g m}^{-3} \]

the total pressure of moist air is the sum of the partial pressure of dry air and the partial pressure of water vapour. The mass of vapour per cubic metre (vapour density or absolute humidity) follows from the ideal-gas law: vapour pressure divided by the specific gas constant of water vapour times the absolute temperature.

Follow the derivation
  1. Ideal-gas law for the vapour alone: \(e V = n_v R T\), where \(n_v\) is the number of moles of water.
  2. Mass of vapour \(m_v = n_v M_w\), so \(\rho_v = m_v/V = e M_w/(RT)\).
  3. With \(e\) in kPa (× 1000 Pa) and \(\rho_v\) in g m⁻³ (× 1000 g kg⁻¹): \(\rho_v = e\cdot 10^{3}\times0.018015\times10^{3}/(8.314\,T) = 2167\,e/T\). The coefficient 2165 used in the FAO/physics.js formula differs by 0.1 % because of rounding of the constants.
  4. Dalton's law itself follows from kinetic theory: pressure is the momentum flux of molecules hitting a wall, and molecules of different species hit it independently.
Assumptions and validity

Ideal-gas behaviour of both components (error well below 1 % at greenhouse conditions); the small "enhancement factor" that raises the saturation vapour pressure of moist air relative to pure vapour (about 0.4 %) is ignored, as in all agricultural calculations (ASHRAE, 2021).

\(\dfrac{\text{Pa}}{\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}} = \dfrac{\text{N m}^{-2}}{\text{N m kg}^{-1}} = \text{kg m}^{-3}\) ✓

Absolute humidity has one practical flaw: when air is heated or cooled it expands or contracts, so its vapour content per cubic metre changes even though no water is added or removed. Engineers therefore prefer a quantity that is conserved when air is heated — the mass of vapour per mass of dry air.

Humidity ratio (mixing ratio)(8.2.2)
\[ W = \frac{m_v}{m_a} = \frac{M_w}{M_a}\,\frac{e}{P - e} = 0.622\,\frac{e}{P - e} \]

the humidity ratio is the mass of water vapour per kilogram of dry air. It equals the ratio of the molar masses of water and dry air, 0.622, times the vapour pressure divided by the partial pressure of the dry air.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(W\)humidity ratiokg kg⁻¹ (often quoted in g kg⁻¹)2–20 g kg⁻¹
\(M_w/M_a\)ratio of molar masses, 18.015/28.965–0.622

Follow the derivation
  1. Both gases occupy the same volume at the same temperature. Ideal-gas law: \(m_v = e V M_w/(RT)\) and \(m_a = p_a V M_a/(RT)\).
  2. Divide: \(W = m_v/m_a = (M_w/M_a)(e/p_a)\); \(V\), \(R\) and \(T\) cancel.
  3. Use Dalton: \(p_a = P - e\). With \(M_w = 18.015\) and \(M_a = 28.965\) g mol⁻¹, \(M_w/M_a = 0.62196\) (physics.js uses 0.62198).
  4. Because \(W\) is a ratio of masses, it does not change when the air is heated, cooled (above the dew point) or compressed — only adding or removing water changes it. On the psychrometric chart, heating is therefore a horizontal line.
Related quantity: specific humidity

Meteorologists often use the specific humidity \(q = m_v/(m_v + m_a) = W/(1+W)\), vapour mass per mass of moist air. At greenhouse humidities the two differ by about 1 %.

(kg mol⁻¹ ÷ kg mol⁻¹) × (kPa ÷ kPa) = kg kg⁻¹ (dimensionless) ✓

Saturation vapour pressure: from Clausius–Clapeyron to Magnus–Tetens

Put a dish of water in a closed box. Molecules escape from the liquid surface and some return; eventually escape and return balance and the vapour pressure stops rising. That equilibrium value is the saturation vapour pressure, \(e_s\). It depends only on temperature — not on the presence of air — and it rises steeply, roughly doubling for every 10–11 K. The physics behind the curve is one of the classic results of thermodynamics.

Saturation vapour pressure (Clausius–Clapeyron → Magnus–Tetens)(8.2.3)
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}e_s}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\lambda\,e_s}{R_v\,T^{2}} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; e_s(t) = 0.6108\,\exp\!\left(\frac{17.27\,t}{t + 237.3}\right)\ \text{kPa} \]

the rate at which the saturation vapour pressure rises with temperature is proportional to the latent heat and to the vapour pressure itself, and inversely proportional to the square of the absolute temperature. Integrated and fitted to measurements, this gives the Magnus–Tetens formula, with the temperature \(t\) in degrees Celsius.

SymbolMeaningUnitValue
\(e_s\)saturation vapour pressure over liquid waterkPa0.611 at 0 °C; 2.338 at 20 °C; 4.243 at 30 °C
\(\lambda\)latent heat of vaporisationJ kg⁻¹2.501 × 10⁶ at 0 °C
\(R_v\)gas constant of water vapourJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹461.5
\(t\), \(T\)temperature in °C and in K°C, K\(T = t + 273.15\)
0.6108, 17.27, 237.3Tetens constants (FAO-56)kPa, –, °CAlduchov & Eskridge (1996): 0.61094, 17.625, 243.04
Follow the derivation (Clausius–Clapeyron)
  1. At equilibrium between liquid and vapour the Gibbs energies per unit mass are equal. Moving along the equilibrium curve, both change equally: \(-s_l\,\mathrm{d}T + v_l\,\mathrm{d}p = -s_v\,\mathrm{d}T + v_v\,\mathrm{d}p\).
  2. Rearrange: \(\mathrm{d}p/\mathrm{d}T = (s_v - s_l)/(v_v - v_l)\). The entropy change of evaporation is \(s_v - s_l = \lambda/T\). This is the Clapeyron equation.
  3. The specific volume of liquid water (0.001 m³ kg⁻¹) is negligible beside that of vapour (≈ 58 m³ kg⁻¹ at 20 °C), and the vapour is ideal: \(v_v = R_v T/e_s\). Substituting gives the Clausius–Clapeyron equation above.
  4. Separate variables and integrate from \(T_0 = 273.15\) K (where \(e_s = e_0 = 0.6112\) kPa) with constant \(\lambda\): \(\ln(e_s/e_0) = (\lambda/R_v)(1/T_0 - 1/T)\).
  5. Write \(1/T_0 - 1/T = (T - T_0)/(T\,T_0) = t/\big(T_0(t + 273.15)\big)\). Then \(e_s = e_0\exp\!\big(a\,t/(t+b)\big)\) with \(a = \lambda/(R_vT_0) = 2.501\times10^{6}/(461.5\times273.15) = 19.84\) and \(b = 273.15\) °C.
  6. But \(\lambda\) is not constant: it falls by ≈ 0.1 % per kelvin. Fitting \(a\) and \(b\) to measured data compensates: Tetens (1930) found \(a = 17.27\), \(b = 237.3\) °C. Notice that \(a/b\) is almost the same in both (0.0726 and 0.0728): the fit keeps the correct slope at 0 °C and adjusts the curvature. This "Magnus form" is accurate to within about 0.1–0.3 % between 0 and 50 °C (Alduchov & Eskridge, 1996).
Assumptions and validity

Over liquid water. Below 0 °C, the saturation vapour pressure over ice is lower (at −10 °C: 0.260 against 0.287 kPa over supercooled water); frost formation on a cold cover is governed by the ice curve. The FAO-56 form (Allen et al., 1998) is used throughout this course and in physics.js, so all lessons and labs agree.

\(\dfrac{\text{J kg}^{-1}\cdot\text{kPa}}{\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{2}} = \text{kPa K}^{-1}\) ✓  ·  exponent: °C ÷ °C = dimensionless ✓

With \(e_s\) known, the most familiar humidity quantity follows at once.

Relative humidity(8.2.4)
\[ \text{RH} = 100\,\frac{e}{e_s(T)}\ \ \% \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad e = e_s(T)\,\frac{\text{RH}}{100} \]

relative humidity is the actual vapour pressure as a percentage of the saturation vapour pressure at the air temperature.

Why RH is a slippery quantity
  1. RH depends on two things: how much water the air contains (\(e\)) and its temperature (through \(e_s\)).
  2. Heat greenhouse air at 85 % RH from 18 to 22 °C without adding water: \(e\) stays at 1.754 kPa but \(e_s\) rises from 2.064 to 2.644 kPa, so RH falls to 66 %. Nothing was removed — heating "dries" the air only in the relative sense.
  3. This is why growers heat to dehumidify, and why RH measured next to a heating pipe or a cold window is meaningless for the crop.
kPa ÷ kPa × 100 = % ✓
Common misconception

"Warm air can hold more water, like a bigger sponge." The air does not hold the water at all — water vapour would reach the same saturation pressure in a vacuum. What rises with temperature is the equilibrium vapour pressure of water, because more molecules have enough energy to escape the liquid. The sponge picture predicts the right direction but leads to wrong ideas, for instance that dry air "absorbs" vapour faster than it really does.

Dew point, wet-bulb temperature and condensation

Cool air without changing its water content and \(e\) stays constant while \(e_s(T)\) falls. At some temperature they become equal: RH reaches 100 % and any further cooling makes water condense. That temperature is the dew point. Any surface colder than the dew point of the surrounding air — a greenhouse roof at night, a cold water pipe, a leaf radiating to a clear sky, the cooling coil of an air-conditioner — collects water.

Dew-point temperature(8.2.5)
\[ T_d = \frac{237.3\;\alpha}{17.27 - \alpha}, \qquad \alpha = \ln\!\left(\frac{e}{0.6108}\right) = \ln\!\left(\frac{\text{RH}}{100}\right) + \frac{17.27\,T}{T + 237.3} \]

the dew point is found by inverting the Magnus–Tetens equation: the temperature at which the saturation vapour pressure equals the actual vapour pressure.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(T_d\)dew-point temperature°C
\(\alpha\)auxiliary variable–
\(T\), RHair temperature and relative humidity°C, %
Follow the derivation
  1. Condition: \(e_s(T_d) = e\), i.e. \(0.6108\exp\!\big(17.27T_d/(T_d+237.3)\big) = e\).
  2. Take logarithms: \(17.27\,T_d/(T_d+237.3) = \ln(e/0.6108) = \alpha\).
  3. Solve the linear equation for \(T_d\): \(17.27T_d = \alpha T_d + 237.3\alpha\) → \(T_d = 237.3\alpha/(17.27-\alpha)\).
  4. Substituting \(e = e_s(T)\,\text{RH}/100\) gives the second form of \(\alpha\). A handy rule for moist air (RH > 50 %) is \(T_d \approx T - (100-\text{RH})/5\) (Lawrence, 2005).
°C × (–) ÷ (–) = °C ✓

The wet-bulb temperature is the temperature a wetted thermometer reaches when air blows over it: evaporation cools the wick until the heat it receives from the air exactly supplies the latent heat of the evaporating water. The drier the air, the larger the wet-bulb depression. The wet/dry-bulb psychrometer that gave the field its name uses this to measure humidity, and evaporative coolers (pad-and-fan systems, fogging) cannot cool air below it.

The psychrometer equation (wet-bulb temperature)(8.2.6)
\[ e = e_s(T_w) - \gamma_{\text{psy}}\,(T - T_w), \qquad \gamma_{\text{psy}} = A_{\text{psy}}\,P \]

the actual vapour pressure equals the saturation vapour pressure at the wet-bulb temperature minus a psychrometer constant times the wet-bulb depression, the difference between dry- and wet-bulb temperatures.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(T_w\)wet-bulb temperature°C—

\(A_{\text{psy}}\)psychrometer coefficientK⁻¹0.000662 for ventilated (aspirated) psychrometers (Allen et al., 1998)
Follow the derivation
  1. Steady state of the wet wick: sensible heat gained from the air = latent heat used for evaporation: \(h_c(T - T_w) = \lambda\,g_v\,\rho_a\,(W_s(T_w) - W)\), where \(g_v\) is a mass-transfer conductance.
  2. For a well-ventilated wick heat and mass transfer are analogous, so \(h_c/(\rho_a c_p g_v)\) is close to 1 (the Lewis relation).
  3. Convert humidity ratios to vapour pressures with \(W \approx 0.622\,e/P\): \(e_s(T_w) - e = (c_p P/(0.622\lambda))(T - T_w)\).
  4. The factor \(c_pP/(0.622\lambda) = 1.013\times10^{-3}\times101.3/(0.622\times2.45) = 0.067\) kPa K⁻¹ is the same psychrometric constant γ that appears in the Penman–Monteith equation (FAO-56). Solving for \(T_w\) needs iteration, or an empirical fit such as Stull (2011), which physics.js uses (mean absolute error below 0.3 K; individual errors between −1 and +0.65 K).

Worked example 2Dripping glass and wet leaves on a winter night

On a clear January night the greenhouse air is 18 °C and 85 % RH; outside it is −5 °C. The single glass has U = 6.0 W m⁻² K⁻¹ and the inside surface coefficient is 7.5 W m⁻² K⁻¹. (a) What is the dew point? (b) Estimate the temperature of the inner glass surface. (c) Leaves radiating to the cold roof can be 2–3 K below air temperature. Is there a risk of water on the leaves?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Dew point: \(e_s(18) = 2.064\) kPa → \(e = 0.85\times2.064 = 1.754\) kPa; \(\alpha = \ln(1.754/0.6108) = 1.055\); \(T_d = 237.3\times1.055/(17.27-1.055) = 15.4\) °C.
  2. Heat flux through the glass (Lesson 8.1, Eq. 8.1.5): \(q = U\Delta T = 6.0\times(18-(-5)) = 138\) W m⁻².
  3. Inner surface temperature: the same flux crosses the inside air film: \(T_s = T_i - q/h_i = 18 - 138/7.5 = -0.4\) °C.
  4. Glass: −0.4 °C is 16 K below the dew point: heavy condensation (even frost). The glass is a very effective — and free — dehumidifier, but the released latent heat is largely lost through the roof, and drops falling from the glass wet the crop.
  5. Leaves: at 18 − 2.5 = 15.5 °C a leaf is only 0.1 K above the dew point. Any further cooling or a slight rise in RH puts a water film on the leaf — ideal conditions for fungal spores to germinate.
  6. What the grower does: close the energy screen (it keeps leaves from "seeing" the cold roof), run a minimum pipe temperature to warm leaves slightly, or lower the humidity so that the dew point drops (for example to 80 % RH → \(T_d\) = 14.5 °C).
Td ≈ 15.4 °C; glass ≈ −0.4 °C (condensation certain); leaves at ≈ 15.5 °C are on the edge of wetting
Condensation, grey mould and the dew point margin

Grey mould (Botrytis cinerea) attacks tomato, cucumber, strawberry, lettuce, ornamentals and many more crops. Its spores germinate and infect mainly when plant surfaces carry free water or the air is close to saturation (Elad et al., 2016). Greenhouse climate computers therefore control not just RH but the margin between leaf (or crop) temperature and dew point, especially around sunrise, when leaf temperature lags behind rising air humidity. Relative humidities above about 85–90 % for long periods are treated as a disease risk.

Enthalpy: sensible and latent energy in the same air

The energy content of moist air is its enthalpy. Because only differences matter, we set the zero at dry air and liquid water at 0 °C. Warming a kilogram of dry air by 1 K takes about 1.006 kJ; the water vapour it carries contains the latent heat of evaporation at 0 °C (2501 kJ kg⁻¹) plus the sensible heat of warming the vapour (1.86 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).

Specific enthalpy and specific volume of moist air(8.2.7)
\[ h = \underbrace{1.006\,t}_{\text{dry air}} + W\,\big(\underbrace{2501}_{\text{latent}} + \underbrace{1.86\,t}_{\text{vapour}}\big)\ \ \text{kJ kg}_{a}^{-1}, \qquad v = \frac{R_a\,T\,(1 + 1.608\,W)}{P} \]

the enthalpy per kilogram of dry air is the sensible heat of the dry air plus, for each kilogram of vapour it carries, the latent heat of vaporisation at 0 °C and the sensible heat of the vapour. The specific volume is the volume occupied by moist air per kilogram of dry air.

Follow the derivation
  1. Enthalpy is additive: \(h = h_a + W h_v\) per kg of dry air.
  2. Dry air (ideal gas, constant \(c_{p,a}\)): \(h_a = c_{p,a}t = 1.006t\) kJ kg⁻¹ relative to 0 °C.
  3. Vapour: take liquid water at 0 °C, evaporate it at 0 °C (\(\lambda_0\) = 2501 kJ kg⁻¹), then warm the vapour to \(t\) (\(c_{p,v}\) = 1.86 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹): \(h_v = 2501 + 1.86t\).
  4. Because enthalpy is a state function, the path does not matter: evaporating at 20 °C (2454 kJ kg⁻¹) and warming the liquid first gives the same result within 0.1 %. This is the ASHRAE (2021) formulation used in physics.js.
  5. Specific volume: ideal-gas law for the mixture, \(v = (R_a T/P)(1 + W R_v/R_a) = (R_aT/P)(1 + 1.608W)\).
Sensible versus latent

The first and last terms change with temperature (sensible heat — you can sense it with a thermometer); the middle term changes only when water is evaporated or condensed (latent heat). At 20 °C and 70 % RH, \(h\) = 46.0 kJ kg⁻¹, of which 20.1 is sensible (dry air) and 25.9 is carried by the vapour. More than half the energy of warm humid air is latent.

Worked example 3What does one air change cost in winter?

A greenhouse at 18 °C and 85 % RH is ventilated with outside air at −5 °C and 90 % RH at one air change per hour (mean height 5.5 m, air density 1.2 kg m⁻³). Calculate the enthalpy of both air streams, split the difference into sensible and latent parts, and express both as W per m² of floor.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Inside: \(e = 1.754\) kPa, \(W_i = 0.622\times1.754/99.57 = 10.96\) g kg⁻¹; \(h_i = 1.006\times18 + 0.01096\times(2501+1.86\times18) = 18.11 + 27.78 = 45.9\) kJ kg⁻¹.
  2. Outside: \(e_s(-5) = 0.421\) kPa, \(e = 0.379\) kPa, \(W_o = 2.34\) g kg⁻¹; \(h_o = -5.03 + 0.00234\times2491.7 = 0.79\) kJ kg⁻¹.
  3. Difference: \(\Delta h = 45.1\) kJ kg⁻¹. Sensible part \(\approx (1.006 + 1.86W_o)\times23 = 23.2\) kJ kg⁻¹; latent part \(\approx (W_i - W_o)(2501 + 1.86\times18) = 0.00862\times2534.5 = 21.9\) kJ kg⁻¹.
  4. Air mass exchanged per m² of floor: \(\rho N\bar h/3600 = 1.2\times1\times5.5/3600 = 1.83\times10^{-3}\) kg m⁻² s⁻¹.
  5. Heat flows: sensible \(1.83\times10^{-3}\times23.2\times1000 = 42.6\) W m⁻²; latent \(1.83\times10^{-3}\times21.9\times1000 = 40.1\) W m⁻², corresponding to \(1.83\times10^{-3}\times0.00862\times3600 = 0.057\) kg of water removed per m² per hour.

The psychrometric chart

All the relations above can be drawn on one graph. The modern (Mollier–Carrier) psychrometric chart plots dry-bulb temperature on the horizontal axis and humidity ratio on the vertical axis. Curves of constant relative humidity rise exponentially towards the upper right, the 100 % curve (saturation) being the upper boundary. Straight, gently sloping lines of constant enthalpy run from upper left to lower right; lines of constant wet-bulb temperature are almost parallel to them. Steep lines of constant specific volume complete the grid. Two properties fix a state; every other property can then be read off.

Air-handling processes become lines on the chart:

  • Sensible heating or cooling (no water added or removed): horizontal line, constant \(W\) and dew point. RH falls on heating.
  • Cooling with dehumidification at a coil colder than the dew point: the air moves towards the coil's apparatus dew point on the saturation curve; the humidity ratio falls. Part of the air bypasses the coil, so the leaving state lies on the straight line between entering air and coil point.
  • Evaporative cooling (pad-and-fan, fog): water evaporates using the air's own sensible heat, so enthalpy is (nearly) constant; the state slides up-left along a line of constant \(h\) and \(T_w\) towards saturation. Transpiration in a closed space without other energy input does the same.
  • Mixing of two air streams: the mixture lies on the straight line connecting them, at the mass-weighted position (Eq. 8.2.8).
Adiabatic mixing of two air streams(8.2.8)
\[ W_m = \frac{\dot m_1 W_1 + \dot m_2 W_2}{\dot m_1 + \dot m_2}, \qquad h_m = \frac{\dot m_1 h_1 + \dot m_2 h_2}{\dot m_1 + \dot m_2}, \qquad t_m = \frac{h_m - 2501\,W_m}{1.006 + 1.86\,W_m} \]

when two air streams mix without heat exchange with the surroundings, the humidity ratio and the enthalpy of the mixture are the averages of the two streams weighted by their dry-air mass flows. The mixture temperature follows by inverting the enthalpy equation.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\dot m_1, \dot m_2\)dry-air mass flows of the two streamskg s⁻¹
\(W_1, W_2, W_m\)humidity ratios of streams and mixturekg kg⁻¹
\(h_1, h_2, h_m\)specific enthalpieskJ kg⁻¹
\(t_m\)mixture temperature°C
Follow the derivation
  1. Conservation of dry air: \(\dot m_m = \dot m_1 + \dot m_2\).
  2. Conservation of water: \(\dot m_m W_m = \dot m_1W_1 + \dot m_2W_2\).
  3. Conservation of energy (adiabatic, no work): \(\dot m_m h_m = \dot m_1h_1 + \dot m_2h_2\).
  4. Both \(W\) and \(h\) are linear averages, and on the chart \(h\) is almost linear in \(t\) at fixed \(W\), so the mixture lies (very nearly) on the straight line joining the two states. Because the saturation curve bends upward, a straight line between two nearly saturated states can pass above it: the excess vapour condenses as fog. This is why a plume forms above a greenhouse vent on a cold day, and why cold air entering a humid greenhouse can mist.
(kg s⁻¹ × kg kg⁻¹) ÷ kg s⁻¹ = kg kg⁻¹ ✓; likewise kJ kg⁻¹ ✓
Worked example 4Fog at the vent

Inside air (18 °C, 85 % RH; \(W\) = 10.96 g kg⁻¹, \(h\) = 45.9 kJ kg⁻¹) mixes with outside air (−5 °C, 90 % RH; \(W\) = 2.34 g kg⁻¹, \(h\) = 0.79 kJ kg⁻¹). Find the mixture state for 20 % and for 30 % outside air (by dry-air mass). Does fog form?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. 20 % outside air: \(W_m = 0.2\times2.34 + 0.8\times10.96 = 9.23\) g kg⁻¹; \(h_m = 0.2\times0.79 + 0.8\times45.9 = 36.9\) kJ kg⁻¹.
  2. Temperature: \(t_m = (36.86 - 2501\times0.00923)/(1.006 + 1.86\times0.00923) = (36.86 - 23.09)/1.023 = 13.5\) °C.
  3. Check saturation: \(e = W P/(0.622 + W) = 0.00923\times101.325/0.631 = 1.482\) kPa; \(e_s(13.5) = 1.543\) kPa; RH = 96 %: no fog, but close.
  4. 30 % outside air: \(W_m = 8.37\) g kg⁻¹, \(h_m = 32.4\) kJ kg⁻¹, \(t_m = 11.2\) °C; \(e = 1.346\) kPa but \(e_s(11.2) = 1.328\) kPa → RH would be 101 %: the mixture is supersaturated and about 0.05 g kg⁻¹ condenses as fog (less than the 0.1 g kg⁻¹ excess, because condensation releases latent heat that warms the mixture slightly).
  5. Interpretation: cold outside air entering through the vents mixes locally with humid inside air and can form mist — and drips on the crop below the vent. Growers open vents gradually and on the lee side, and mix incoming air with warm air above the heating pipes.
20 %: 13.5 °C, 96 % RH (no fog); 30 %: 11.2 °C, supersaturated → fog

Vapour pressure deficit: what the leaf feels

Transpiration is diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf through the stomata. Inside the leaf the air spaces are saturated at leaf temperature; outside is the greenhouse air. The driving force is therefore the difference in vapour pressure between the leaf interior and the air — the vapour pressure deficit. Plant physiologists have long argued that VPD is a better indicator of the "drying power" of the air than RH, because the same RH means very different VPD at different temperatures (Lesson 2.2; Grossiord et al., 2020).

Vapour pressure deficit (air-based and leaf-to-air)(8.2.9)
\[ \text{VPD}_{\text{air}} = e_s(T_a)\left(1 - \frac{\text{RH}}{100}\right), \qquad \text{VPD}_{\text{leaf}} = e_s(T_{\ell}) - e_s(T_a)\,\frac{\text{RH}}{100} \]

the air vapour pressure deficit is the saturation vapour pressure at air temperature minus the actual vapour pressure. The leaf-to-air deficit, which actually drives transpiration, uses the saturation vapour pressure at leaf temperature instead.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
VPDvapour pressure deficitkPa0.3–1.5 in protected cultivation
\(T_a, T_\ell\)air and leaf temperature°Cleaf 1–3 K below air when transpiring in low light; above air under strong radiation or closed stomata
Follow the derivation
  1. The sub-stomatal cavity is in equilibrium with liquid water in the cell walls, so its vapour pressure is \(e_s(T_\ell)\) (the small effect of leaf water potential on it is negligible).
  2. The air outside the leaf boundary layer has \(e = e_s(T_a)\,\text{RH}/100\).
  3. Transpiration flux \(E = g_{\text{tot}}\,(e_s(T_\ell) - e)/P\) (mol m⁻² s⁻¹ with conductance in mol m⁻² s⁻¹): proportional to the leaf-to-air deficit.
  4. When leaf and air temperatures are equal the two definitions coincide; with a leaf 2 K cooler than air at 24 °C and 70 % RH, VPD falls from 0.90 to 0.56 kPa — a 38 % reduction in the driving force.
kPa − kPa = kPa ✓

How much VPD is right? There is no single optimum; it depends on crop, growth stage and light. In a classic review, Grange and Hand (1987) concluded that humidities between about 0.2 and 1.0 kPa VPD (roughly 55–90 % RH at 20 °C) have little direct effect on the physiology and development of horticultural crops. The problems lie at the extremes. Very low VPD (very humid air) slows transpiration and with it the mass flow of calcium to young, fast-growing tissues — a cause of calcium-related disorders such as tipburn in lettuce — and increases disease risk. High VPD makes stomata close to prevent excessive water loss, which cuts CO₂ uptake and photosynthesis (Grossiord et al., 2020); in extreme cases plants wilt even with ample water at the roots. Grower guides therefore often work with rule-of-thumb zones, shown in Figure 4, while reviews compile crop-specific ranges (for tomato, e.g., Shamshiri et al., 2018).

Transpiration as a latent load, and how to remove it

Every litre the crop transpires has to leave the building, or humidity rises until transpiration stops. How much is it? A mature greenhouse crop typically converts roughly half of the solar radiation it absorbs into latent heat. On a bright summer day in central Sweden, global radiation can reach 20 MJ m⁻² d⁻¹ (Table 2 of Lesson 8.1 gives a June average of 5.5 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ = 19.8 MJ). With 70 % transmission, 14 MJ m⁻² enters, and if 60 % of that drives transpiration, \(E = 0.6\times14\ \text{MJ}/2.45\ \text{MJ kg}^{-1} = 3.4\) kg m⁻² d⁻¹. On such days a tall crop transpires 2–4 L m⁻²; in winter well under 1 L. A hectare of tomatoes on a bright day therefore releases some 30 tonnes of water vapour.

The vapour balance of the greenhouse air (the water counterpart of the energy balance of Lesson 8.1) tells us how it leaves.

Vapour balance and the heat cost of dehumidifying by ventilation(8.2.10)
\[ E_{\text{crop}} = \rho_a\,g\,(W_i - W_o) + M_{\text{cond}} + M_{\text{dehum}}, \qquad \frac{Q_{\text{vent}}}{E_{\text{vent}}} = \frac{c_p\,(T_i - T_o)}{W_i - W_o} \]

in steady state, the water transpired by the crop leaves the greenhouse air in three ways: carried out by ventilation (air flow times the difference in humidity ratio between inside and outside), condensed on the cover, or removed by a dehumidifier. When ventilation does the job, each kilogram of water removed costs the sensible heat needed to warm the replacement air, which equals the specific heat times the temperature difference divided by the humidity-ratio difference.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(E_{\text{crop}}\)transpiration (and evaporation) per floor areakg m⁻² s⁻¹0–1.5 × 10⁻⁴
\(\rho_a g\)dry-air mass flow of ventilation per floor areakg m⁻² s⁻¹see Lesson 8.1, Eq. 8.1.7
\(W_i, W_o\)inside and outside humidity ratiokg kg⁻¹—
\(M_{\text{cond}}\)condensation on the coverkg m⁻² s⁻¹large on cold nights
\(M_{\text{dehum}}\)water removed by a mechanical dehumidifierkg m⁻² s⁻¹—
\(Q_{\text{vent}}/E_{\text{vent}}\)sensible heat lost per kg of water ventedJ kg⁻¹≈ 2–3 MJ kg⁻¹ in a Swedish winter
Follow the derivation
  1. Water mass conservation for the greenhouse air in steady state: sources (transpiration, evaporation from soil and wet surfaces) = sinks (vapour exported by air exchange, condensation, dehumidifiers).
  2. Exported vapour: each kilogram of dry air leaving carries \(W_i\) and is replaced by air carrying \(W_o\): net \(\rho_a g (W_i - W_o)\).
  3. If ventilation alone removes \(E\), the dry-air flow needed is \(\rho_a g = E/(W_i - W_o)\), and warming it costs \(\rho_a g\, c_p (T_i - T_o)\). Divide by \(E\) to obtain the heat per kilogram of water.
  4. Example: 18 °C/85 % inside, −2 °C/90 % outside: \(W_i - W_o = 10.96 - 2.93 = 8.03\) g kg⁻¹; \(1006\times20/0.00803 = 2.5\) MJ kg⁻¹ — about the latent heat of the water itself. In winter, removing a litre of transpired water by ventilation costs roughly as much heat again as it took to evaporate it.
Assumptions and validity

Steady state, well-mixed air; the latent energy exported (which was supplied earlier, when the crop transpired) is not counted again. The cost rises steeply when outside air is humid and warm (small \(W_i - W_o\)): on a mild, humid autumn night ventilation hardly dehumidifies at all. That is the situation in which mechanical dehumidification pays off (Campen et al., 2003; Amani et al., 2020).

\(\dfrac{\text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}\times\text{K}}{\text{kg kg}^{-1}} = \text{J per kg of water}\) ✓
Worked example 5Venting away the night's transpiration

On a February night (outside −2 °C, 90 % RH) a greenhouse crop and wet surfaces release 0.05 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ of water. The grower wants to hold 18 °C and 85 % RH by ventilation alone (ignore condensation on the cover). Mean height 5.5 m. What air exchange rate and what extra heating are needed?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Humidity ratios: inside 10.96 g kg⁻¹ (Worked example 3); outside \(e_s(-2) = 0.527\) kPa, \(e = 0.9\times0.527 = 0.475\) kPa, \(W_o = 0.622\times0.475/100.85 = 2.93\) g kg⁻¹. Difference 8.03 g kg⁻¹.
  2. Dry-air flow: \(0.05/0.00803 = 6.23\) kg m⁻² h⁻¹ ≈ 5.2 m³ m⁻² h⁻¹ (at 1.2 kg m⁻³).
  3. Air exchange rate: \(N = 5.2/5.5 = 0.94\) h⁻¹ — the leakage of a tight greenhouse is not enough; a vent gap is needed.
  4. Heat to warm it: \(6.23/3600\times1006\times20 = 34.8\) W m⁻², i.e. ≈ 350 kW for a hectare, on top of the transmission losses.
  5. Per kilogram of water: \(34.8\ \text{W}\times3600\ \text{s}/0.05\ \text{kg} = 2.5\) MJ kg⁻¹, consistent with Eq. 8.2.10.
  6. Sanity check: in practice the cold cover condenses a large part of the night-time vapour for free (Worked example 2), which is why closing a screen — which keeps vapour away from the cold roof — often makes humidity control harder and requires a screen gap.
N ≈ 0.9 h⁻¹; ≈ 35 W m⁻² of extra heating (≈ 2.5 MJ per kg of water)

Dehumidification methods and their energy costs

Four physical routes remove vapour from greenhouse or plant-factory air (Amani et al., 2020):

In a plant factory there is no ventilation to speak of (Kozai, 2013: 0.01–0.02 h⁻¹), so dehumidification happens at the cooling coil of the air-conditioner: air is cooled below its dew point, the transpired water condenses, and the cold, saturated air is mixed with room air or reheated. This has a wonderful side effect — the condensate is nearly pure water that can be returned to the nutrient tank. Kozai (2013) reported water-use efficiencies (water fixed or held in plants per water supplied) of 0.95–0.98 in closed plant production systems against 0.02–0.03 in greenhouses, precisely because transpired water is recovered rather than vented.

Worked example 6A heat-pump dehumidifier and plant-factory condensate

(a) A heat-pump dehumidifier draws greenhouse air at 18 °C and 85 % RH (\(W\) = 10.96 g kg⁻¹) through an evaporator coil that leaves it saturated at 8 °C. The cooling COP is 3. How much electricity does it need per kilogram of water removed? (b) A plant-factory tier transpires 0.39 kWh m⁻² d⁻¹ of latent heat (Lesson 8.1, Figure 7, default settings). How much condensate can be recovered at 22 °C per m² per day, and per year from 1000 m² of cultivation area?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Coil outlet humidity: \(e_s(8) = 1.073\) kPa → \(W = 0.622\times1.073/100.25 = 6.66\) g kg⁻¹. Water removed per kg of air: \(10.96 - 6.66 = 4.30\) g.
  2. Air per kg of water: \(1/0.00430 = 232\) kg of dry air.
  3. Coil load per kg of water: sensible \(232\times1.006\times(18-8) = 2340\) kJ plus latent ≈ 2482 kJ (λ at 8 °C) → ≈ 4820 kJ.
  4. Electricity: \(4820/3 = 1610\) kJ = 0.45 kWh per kg, i.e. a specific moisture extraction rate of about 2.2 kg kWh⁻¹. All 4820 + 1610 kJ reappear at the condenser and reheat the air: in winter the dehumidifier doubles as a heater.
  5. Condensate (b): \(0.39\ \text{kWh} = 1.40\) MJ; \(1.40/2.448 = 0.57\) kg m⁻² d⁻¹ ≈ 0.57 L m⁻² d⁻¹.
  6. Per year, 1000 m²: \(0.57\times1000\times365 = 2.1\times10^{5}\) L ≈ 210 m³ of clean water returned to the nutrient solution instead of being vented.
(a) ≈ 0.45 kWh per kg of water (≈ 2.2 kg kWh⁻¹); (b) ≈ 0.57 L m⁻² d⁻¹ → ≈ 210 m³ yr⁻¹ per 1000 m²
Research frontier

Humidity control is where greenhouse energy savings now concentrate. Dutch "next generation growing" practice (2010s–2020s) replaced fixed RH set-points by control of the absolute humidity deficit and VPD, admitted drier outside air through forced ventilation with heat exchangers, and used screens more hours per year. Plant factories go one step further and treat the crop's transpiration as a water source. Sensor-driven control of VPD at leaf level (with thermal cameras measuring leaf temperature) is an active area of research in 2024–2026.

Key terms

Psychrometrics
The thermodynamics of mixtures of dry air and water vapour.
Vapour pressure
The partial pressure of water vapour in air, \(e\) (kPa).
Saturation vapour pressure
The equilibrium vapour pressure over liquid water at a given temperature, \(e_s(T)\); approximated by the Magnus–Tetens equation.
Clausius–Clapeyron equation
\(\mathrm{d}e_s/\mathrm{d}T = \lambda e_s/(R_vT^2)\): the thermodynamic law behind the steep rise of \(e_s\) with temperature.
Relative humidity
Actual vapour pressure as a percentage of the saturation vapour pressure at air temperature.
Absolute humidity
Mass of water vapour per volume of air (g m⁻³).
Humidity ratio
Mass of water vapour per mass of dry air, \(W = 0.622e/(P-e)\); unchanged by heating or cooling above the dew point.
Dew point
The temperature to which air must be cooled at constant pressure and water content to become saturated.
Wet-bulb temperature
The temperature of a ventilated wet thermometer, where evaporative cooling balances heat gain from the air; the lower limit of evaporative cooling.
Enthalpy of moist air
Energy content per kg of dry air, \(h = 1.006t + W(2501 + 1.86t)\) kJ kg⁻¹, comprising sensible and latent parts.
Psychrometric chart
A graph of humidity ratio against dry-bulb temperature with lines of RH, enthalpy, wet-bulb temperature and specific volume, on which air processes are straight or simple lines.
Vapour pressure deficit (VPD)
The difference between saturation vapour pressure (at air or leaf temperature) and actual vapour pressure; the driving force of transpiration.
Latent load
The rate at which water vapour (and its latent heat) must be removed from a space to hold its humidity.
Specific moisture extraction rate
Kilograms of water removed by a dehumidifier per kWh of electricity.
