Resilience of food systems

In the summer of 2018 Swedish farmers harvested 45 % less grain than the year before. In early 2022, within weeks of Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, world prices of staple foods and fertilisers reached all-time highs. In spring 2026, war in the Persian Gulf blocked a strait through which nearly a third of the world's fertiliser trade normally passes. Swedish shops stayed full through all of it — but not by accident, and not by guarantee. This lesson turns resilience from a buzzword into something an engineer can define, measure and design for: how deep a shock cuts, how fast a system recovers, how diverse its suppliers really are, how long its buffers last — and whether high-tech farms make a food system more or less secure.

Four shocks in eight years

A food system is a network of flows: of seeds, fertiliser, fuel, feed, water, electricity, labour, money and information into farms, and of food out of them through processors, wholesalers and shops to households. Most of the time these flows are so reliable that we notice them only when they fail. Engineers distinguish two kinds of disturbance. A stress is slow and persistent — soil degradation, rising temperatures, an ageing farm workforce. A shock is sudden — a drought, a pandemic, a war, a blocked canal. Stresses erode the capacity to cope with shocks; shocks reveal how much capacity is left. The years since 2018 have delivered an unusually dense sequence of shocks, and they are the empirical material of this lesson.

Summer 2018

Nordic drought and heat. Sweden harvested 3.25 million tonnes of cereals, 45 % less than in 2017; the roughage harvest from temporary grassland fell by a fifth, and more cereal fields than usual were cut as silage to feed livestock (SCB, 2018). Measured in energy, Swedish food production fell to about 2,710 kcal per person and day against an average of 3,530 kcal for 2013–2023 (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025). Across Europe, above-normal yields in the south nearly offset the northern losses (Beillouin et al., 2020).

2020

COVID-19. Lockdowns disrupted labour, logistics and restaurants, but the main impact on food security came from lost income and purchasing power, especially among the poor (Béné, 2020; Laborde et al., 2020).

23–29 March 2021

Suez Canal blocked. The container ship Ever Given ran aground and closed a route that carries about 12 % of world trade for six days.

2022–2023

War in Ukraine. In 2021 Russia had shipped 18 % and Ukraine 10 % of the world's wheat exports; together they supplied close to 63 % of sunflower-oil exports, and Russia was the largest exporter of nitrogen fertiliser (FAO, 2022). Food prices hit all-time highs in early 2022 and urea prices had risen two and a half times in twelve months. The Black Sea Grain Initiative (22 July 2022 – 17 July 2023) let nearly 33 million tonnes of grain and other food leave Ukrainian ports (United Nations, 2023). Global food-price inflation peaked at 13.6 % in January 2023 and at 30 % in low-income countries in May 2023 (FAO et al., 2025).

2026

Strait of Hormuz. From 28 February 2026, war largely blocked shipping through the strait, which normally handles nearly a third of global fertiliser trade — about 30 % of world urea trade was held up at the start of the northern planting season (PBS NewsHour, 2026). In Sweden the first state emergency grain stocks were in place in the four northernmost counties by June 2026, and a new law on emergency stocks in the food chain took effect on 1 July 2026 (Jordbruksverket, 2026; Riksdagen, 2026).

Look at the list as an engineer. The 2018 drought was a production shock in one region; COVID-19 was a labour, logistics and income shock everywhere at once; Suez and Hormuz were chokepoint shocks in the transport network; the war in Ukraine combined a production shock, a trade shock and an input shock (fertiliser, energy). Each hit a different part of the system, and each was absorbed — or not — by different mechanisms: trade, stocks, substitution, public support. Table 1 summarises the pattern.

Key idea

Resilience is about the stability of a function — people having access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food — not about the stability of any particular component. A system can keep its function while individual farms, suppliers or technologies come and go; and a system in which every component looks efficient can still fail as a whole.

What resilience means

"Resilience" entered science from two directions, and the difference between them matters for how you assess a food technology.

Engineering resilience is the speed with which a system returns to its equilibrium after a disturbance — a bridge that springs back after a load, a thermostat that restores the set-point. It presumes a single, known equilibrium and measures efficiency of recovery. Ecological resilience, introduced by C. S. Holling in 1973, asks a different question: how large a disturbance can a system absorb before it flips into a different state altogether? In Holling's words, resilience "determines the persistence of relationships within a system and is a measure of the ability of these systems to absorb changes of state variables, driving variables, and parameters, and still persist" (Holling, 1973, p. 17). Holling (1996) later named the two ideas engineering and ecological resilience and argued that systems optimised for fast return to one equilibrium often lose the capacity to persist when conditions change.

Walker et al. (2004) extended the idea to social–ecological systems — farms, markets, institutions and ecosystems together — and distinguished three properties: resilience (the capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganise while keeping essentially the same function, structure and feedbacks), adaptability (the capacity of the actors to manage resilience) and transformability (the capacity to create a fundamentally new system when the existing one becomes untenable). Development researchers turned these into three capacities that are now widely used in food-security work (Béné, 2020): absorptive capacity (coping without changing — buffers, stocks, insurance), adaptive capacity (incremental adjustment — switching suppliers, crops or diets) and transformative capacity (changing the system itself — a different energy base, new institutions). Which capacity is needed depends on the intensity and duration of the shock (Figure 2).

Engineers who study infrastructure after earthquakes gave us a fourth, very practical vocabulary: the four Rs of Bruneau et al. (2003) — robustness, redundancy, resourcefulness and rapidity. They map directly onto food systems (Table 2) and onto the resilience triangle of the next section, which is why this lesson uses them as its organising frame.

For food systems, Tendall et al. (2015) proposed a definition that combines these strands: food-system resilience is the capacity, over time, of a food system and its units at multiple levels to provide sufficient, appropriate and accessible food to all in the face of various, even unforeseen, disturbances. Note the three qualifiers — sufficient (quantity), appropriate (nutritious, culturally acceptable) and accessible (affordable, physically reachable) — and the phrase even unforeseen: resilience is precisely the capacity to cope with shocks nobody planned for.

Common misconception

"A resilient system bounces back to exactly where it was." Sometimes the old state is the problem. After the 2022 fertiliser shock, a region that simply waits for gas prices to fall has engineering resilience; a region that builds nutrient recycling and domestic production from residual streams has transformative capacity. Resilience thinking asks which state is worth returning to.

History

C. S. "Buzz" Holling (1930–2019) was an ecologist who studied predator–prey dynamics and managed forests in Canada. His 1973 paper, written to explain why managed ecosystems such as spruce forests plagued by budworm could suddenly collapse, became one of the most cited papers in ecology. Three decades later, the earthquake engineer Michel Bruneau and colleagues (2003) gave the concept its first widely used quantitative measure — the resilience triangle — for the recovery of hospitals, power networks and water systems after earthquakes.

Measuring resilience: the resilience triangle

To measure resilience you need a functionality indicator \(Q(t)\): a quantity that is 100 % when the system works normally. For a food system it could be the share of normal calorie supply that reaches consumers, the fraction of shops with a full assortment, the regional production relative to its long-term mean, or the share of households that can afford a healthy diet. When a shock strikes at time \(t_0\), \(Q\) drops and later recovers. The area between the normal level and the actual curve is the loss of resilience.

Resilience loss and resilience index(15.2.1)
\[ \text{(a)}\;\; L = \int_{t_0}^{t_0+T} \big[100 - Q(t)\big]\,\mathrm{d}t \qquad\qquad \text{(b)}\;\; \Phi = \frac{1}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}\frac{Q(t)}{100}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1 - \frac{L}{100\,T} \]

(a) the resilience loss is the time integral of the functionality shortfall below 100 % over a control period T after the shock; (b) the resilience index is the average functionality over the control period as a fraction of normal — one minus the loss divided by the largest possible loss, 100 % times T.

Follow the derivation
  1. Bruneau et al. (2003) measured the loss of resilience of a community after an earthquake as the area \(\int[100-Q(t)]\,\mathrm{d}t\): every day on which a hospital works at 60 % adds 40 %·day to the loss. The integral combines depth and duration in one number.
  2. The loss depends on how long you look: a longer \(T\) cannot reduce it. To compare shocks of different length, Cimellaro et al. (2010) normalised the functionality by the control period: \(\Phi=\frac1T\int Q/100\,\mathrm{d}t\).
  3. Write \(Q = 100-(100-Q)\) inside the integral: \(\frac1T\int\big(1-\frac{100-Q}{100}\big)\mathrm{d}t = 1-\frac{1}{100T}\int(100-Q)\,\mathrm{d}t = 1-\frac{L}{100T}\).
  4. For an exponential recovery \(Q(t)=100-\Delta Q\,e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau}\) the integral is analytic: \(L=\Delta Q\,\tau\,(1-e^{-T/\tau})\), which tends to \(\Delta Q\,\tau\) when \(T\gg\tau\). The mini-calculator uses this form.
Assumptions and validity

The result depends on the choice of \(Q\) (production? supply to consumers? affordability?), on the control period \(T\) and on the baseline (is "normal" last year's level or a trend?). \(\Phi\) treats a short deep dip and a long shallow one with the same area as equivalent — but a 90 % loss of food supply for three days is not ethically equivalent to a 3 % loss for three months. Report the depth and the duration alongside the area. The index also says nothing about who bears the loss: an average \(Q\) of 95 % can hide households at 0 % (Lesson 15.3).

Diversity and concentration

The oldest rule of risk management is not to put all your eggs in one basket. For a food system the baskets are suppliers, crops, regions, transport routes and technologies. Two families of indicators measure how many baskets there are and how evenly the eggs are spread. The first comes from information theory.

Shannon diversity, evenness and effective number(15.2.3)
\[ H = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i \ln p_i, \qquad J = \frac{H}{\ln n}, \qquad D_1 = e^{H} \]

the Shannon index H is minus the sum over all suppliers of each supplier's share times the natural logarithm of that share; the evenness J is H divided by its maximum possible value, the logarithm of the number of suppliers; and the effective number of suppliers is e raised to the power H.

\(J\)Pielou evenness–0–1
\(D_1\)effective number of equally important suppliers (Hill number of order 1)suppliers1 to \(n\)
Follow the derivation
  1. Shannon (1948) asked how much "surprise" a message carries. An event of probability \(p\) carries information \(-\ln p\): rare events surprise more. The average surprise over all events is \(H=-\sum p_i\ln p_i\).
  2. Read \(p_i\) as the probability that a randomly chosen tonne of supply came from supplier \(i\). If one supplier delivers everything (\(p_1=1\)), there is no uncertainty: \(H=0\).
  3. For fixed \(n\), \(H\) is maximal when all shares are equal. With \(p_i=1/n\): \(H=-n\cdot\frac1n\ln\frac1n=\ln n\). Dividing by this maximum gives the evenness \(J\) (Pielou).
  4. \(H\) itself is hard to interpret (is 1.2 a lot?). Hill (1973) and Jost (2006) recommend converting it into an effective number: the number of equally sized suppliers that would give the same \(H\). Solve \(\ln D_1 = H\): \(D_1=e^{H}\).
  5. Example: shares 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 give \(H = -(0.5\ln0.5+0.3\ln0.3+0.2\ln0.2)=0.347+0.361+0.322=1.030\); \(J=1.030/\ln3=0.937\); \(D_1=e^{1.030}=2.80\) effective suppliers.
Assumptions and validity

The index counts shares, not risks. Two suppliers in the same war zone, the same river basin or the same monoculture are two names but one risk (Worked example 2). Kummu et al. (2020) used Shannon diversity of supply sources to show that between 1987 and 2013 most people gained supply diversity through trade — at the cost of higher import dependency and, often, fewer import partners.

The second family comes from competition economics. The Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI) is the sum of squared shares. It is used by competition authorities to judge whether a market is concentrated, and it is now also used for supply risk: the Swedish Food Agency and Board of Agriculture classify the concentration of the EU's food imports with it (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025).

\[ \text{HHI} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{2}, \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{1}{\text{HHI}} \]

the Herfindahl–Hirschman index is the sum of the squared supply shares; its reciprocal is the effective number of equally sized suppliers.

  2. For \(n\) equal suppliers: \(\text{HHI}=n\cdot(1/n)^2=1/n\), so \(1/\text{HHI}=n\). For unequal shares, \(1/\text{HHI}\) is the number of equal suppliers with the same concentration.

What do real numbers look like? In 2024, 37 % of the tonnage of food imported into the EU had a high concentration index. Ukraine supplied 26 % of the 121 million tonnes imported, Brazil 9 % and the USA 8 %; for maize and wheat about 60 % came from Ukraine alone — partly an artefact of transit trade through the EU's "solidarity lanes" (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025). The EU as a whole is nevertheless a net exporter of cereals, which is why this concentration is a risk for particular flows rather than for total supply.

Diversity matters within countries too. Using five decades of yields of 176 crops in 91 nations, Renard and Tilman (2019) found that nations with a greater effective diversity of crops have more stable total harvests, with fewer years of sharp losses — a stabilising effect similar in size to the destabilising effect of rainfall variability. The mechanism is the portfolio effect of Section 15.2.8: different crops fail in different years.

Self-sufficiency and hidden dependencies

"How self-sufficient are we?" is the first question politicians ask after a shock. FAO food balance sheets give it a precise meaning by comparing domestic production with the domestic supply available for use (FAO, 2001):

Self-sufficiency ratio and import dependency ratio(15.2.5)
\[ \text{SSR} = \frac{P}{P + M - X}\times100\,\%, \qquad \text{IDR} = \frac{M}{P + M - X}\times100\,\% \]

the self-sufficiency ratio is domestic production divided by domestic supply — production plus imports minus exports; the import dependency ratio is imports divided by the same domestic supply.

Follow the derivation
  1. The commodity balance of a food balance sheet: supply = production + imports − exports − stock increase; supply = food + feed + seed + processing + losses + other uses (FAO, 2001).
  2. Neglecting stock changes, domestic supply is \(U = P + M - X\). The shares of this supply that come from domestic production and from imports are \(P/U\) and \(M/U\).
  3. They add up to more than 100 % when the country also exports: \(\text{SSR}+\text{IDR}-X/U = 100\ \%\). A country can therefore have SSR > 100 % and a large IDR — it exports one variety of a commodity and imports another.
  4. With calories as the unit, commodities can be aggregated into a single food-energy self-sufficiency, which is how Swedish preparedness studies express it (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025).
Assumptions and validity

SSR counts only the final product. It ignores the imported fertiliser, fuel, feed, seed and spare parts without which domestic production would collapse — the "hidden dependencies" below. It is also an annual average: a country can be self-sufficient in potatoes on average and still run out in June.

Sweden is an instructive case because the answer depends entirely on what you count. In energy terms Swedish agriculture, aquaculture and fisheries produced on average 3,530 kcal per person and day in 2013–2023 after losses, enough in theory to feed about 14 million people at the 2,627 kcal per day that the authorities assume for the population's actual needs in a crisis. Direct consumption was about 3,070 kcal per person and day in 2023. Yet Sweden imported 2,920 and exported 2,240 kcal per person and day in 2024, about 70 % of it within the EU; the largest net inflows are fruit and vegetables and edible oils. Production is also concentrated: Skåne and Västra Götaland alone produced 52 % of the food energy in 2023, while the four northernmost counties depend entirely on transport from the south (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025).

Behind the production figures lies a second layer: the inputs. Figure 5 shows how much of each critical input reaches Swedish farms through foreign trade. Sweden produces no mineral fertiliser and no plant protection products; nine tenths of its motor fuel is imported. Until 2021 Russia and Belarus supplied on average about 22 % of Sweden's imported mineral fertiliser; by 2024 nothing came from them, and Germany and Finland supplied 62 % — but Finnish compound fertiliser is made from imported ammonia, and German nitrogen fertiliser from natural gas that may itself be Russian (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025). Supply chains are longer than trade statistics show.

Common misconception

"100 % self-sufficiency means food security." Autarky removes the redundancy that trade provides: in 2018 northern Europe was saved by southern European harvests, not by Swedish fields. Kummu et al. (2020) found that trade raised the supply diversity of most of the world's population between 1987 and 2013, at the cost of import dependency. The engineering goal is not zero imports but diverse, redundant and buffered imports, plus the capacity to produce essentials when trade fails.

Stocks and buffers: from just-in-time to just-in-case

Modern food chains are lean. Supermarkets replenish most products daily from central warehouses; fresh produce spends one to four days in the retail chain. Lean chains are efficient — little capital is tied up in inventory and little food spoils — but they have little absorptive capacity. How long would Swedish food last if deliveries stopped? The national indicator report gives rough answers (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025):

  • Retail: about 42,000 kcal per person in stores and central warehouses — roughly 16 days of the 2,627 kcal per day needed in a crisis.
  • Households: the authorities recommend that every household can manage at least one week without outside help (MSB, 2024). In a 2022 survey only 39 % said their food would last a week and only 19 % had water for a week; another 19 % said their water would not last beyond one day.
  • Public meals: about 2.3 million people eat at least one meal a day in schools, hospitals and care homes, but only 60 % of municipalities had a preparedness plan for public meals in 2021.
  • Fuel: Sweden holds emergency stocks of motor fuels equal to 90 days of net imports, under IEA and EU obligations.
  • Alternative resources: about 1.6 million tonnes of grain went to animal feed in 2023, equivalent in theory to about 1,200 kcal per person and day if people ate it; living farm animals represent about 45,240 kcal per person, or about 500 kcal per person and day if slaughtered over three months — but rebuilding a beef herd takes 12–18 months.

The physics of a buffer is a simple mass balance: a stock drains at the rate of the deficit it covers.

Duration of a buffer stock(15.2.6)
\[ \tau = \frac{S}{(1-\varphi)\,D} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad S_{\text{req}} = \tau\,(1-\varphi)\,D \]

a stock S lasts for a time τ equal to the stock divided by the deficit, which is the fraction of normal supply that fails to arrive times the normal demand; read backwards, the stock needed to bridge a disruption of length τ is the duration times the deficit rate.

Follow the derivation
  1. Mass balance of the stock: \(\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = \text{inflow} - \text{outflow} = \varphi D - D = -(1-\varphi)D\), assuming demand is met in full while stock remains.
  2. With constant \(\varphi\) and \(D\), the stock declines linearly: \(S(t) = S_0-(1-\varphi)Dt\).
  3. It is empty when \(S(\tau)=0\): \(\tau = S_0/[(1-\varphi)D]\). With \(\varphi=0\) this is the familiar "days of stock" \(S_0/D\); a stock covering 16 days of total demand lasts 32 days if half of normal deliveries continue.
  4. Rationing reduces \(D\) and extends \(\tau\) proportionally — the same arithmetic as a household budget. With time-varying \(\varphi(t)\), integrate: \(\int_0^\tau[1-\varphi(t)]D\,\mathrm{d}t=S_0\).
Assumptions and validity

Assumes the stock is where people need it (regionalisation), that it can be processed (a grain store is useless without mills, fuel and bakeries), that it does not spoil and that distribution works. The Swedish model of rotation stocks — the state owns grain stored and continuously rotated by private firms — addresses spoilage and cost (Jordbruksverket, 2026).

Frontier · as of 2026

Sweden is rebuilding food stocks for the first time since the Cold War stores were sold off. The government funded Jordbruksverket to establish emergency stocks of grain for human consumption and of critical farm inputs during 2026–2028. Procurement started in the four northernmost counties on 15 October 2025; contracts were awarded on 6 March 2026 for firms able to sell at least 1,000 t of wheat to the agency and store and rotate it, and the first stocks were in place in June 2026. Grain stocks for the rest of the country and stocks of mineral fertiliser, crop protection, seed and protein feed are next. A new law on emergency stocks in the food chain, adopted by the Riksdag, applies from 1 July 2026; the government decides which goods, how much and where (Jordbruksverket, 2026; Riksdagen, 2026). The 2026 Hormuz crisis showed why inputs matter: oil is covered by internationally coordinated emergency stocks (the IEA and EU 90-day obligations), fertiliser is not.

Controlled-environment and urban agriculture: asset or liability?

Vertical farms and greenhouses are often marketed as the answer to fragile supply chains: weather-proof, pest-free, close to consumers, harvesting every day of the year. The four Rs let you test that claim component by component.

  • Robustness to weather and pests — high. A plant factory is indifferent to drought, frost and hail, and its closed environment excludes most pests. A drought like 2018 would not touch its output.
  • Rapidity — high. Lettuce cycles of four to six weeks mean a farm can re-plan production within a month, far faster than an orchard or a beef herd.
  • Shorter chains — mixed. Local production removes long-distance transport and border crossings, but post-farmgate costs still dominate what consumers pay (FAO et al., 2026), and a farm still needs packaging, cold chains and retailers.
  • Energy dependency — low robustness. A vertical farm uses about 8–15 kWh of electricity per kilogram of lettuce (Lesson 15.1); in Graamans et al. (2018) a plant factory needed 247 kWh per kilogram of dry matter. A multi-day power cut or an electricity price spike hits it directly.
  • Input dependency — low robustness. Nutrient salts (whose nitrogen comes from natural gas), seeds from a few breeders, rock wool or coir, LEDs, sensors, software and spare parts all come through global supply chains (Figure 6).
  • Financial fragility — low robustness. Plant factories are capital-intensive. Zhuang et al. (2022), analysing 26 plant factories, found that a 30 % fall in either the yield or the price of lettuce "would easily bring PFALs bankruptcy". High-profile failures have followed: AeroFarms, once a pioneer of vertical farming, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2023 after struggling with high operating costs and declining investor confidence, and the greenhouse company AppHarvest also filed for bankruptcy (Rao et al., 2026). A farm that is insolvent after a shock has a resilience index of zero.
  • Contribution to calories — very small. Leafy greens deliver vitamins, minerals and fibre, not energy.

The energy dependency translates into a simple economic stress test. A farm survives a price shock as long as its revenue covers its costs; because electricity enters costs linearly, there is a critical electricity price above which every kilogram is sold at a loss.

Energy-price stress test of a controlled-environment farm(15.2.7)
\[ C = C_0 + e_{\text{el}}\,p_{\text{el}}, \qquad p^{*}_{\text{el}} = \frac{P_{\text{sale}} - C_0}{e_{\text{el}}}, \qquad \frac{\Delta C}{C} = s_{\text{el}}\,\frac{\Delta p_{\text{el}}}{p_{\text{el}}} \]

the cost per kilogram is the non-electric cost plus the electricity use per kilogram times the electricity price; the critical electricity price is the selling price minus the non-electric cost, divided by the electricity use; and the relative change in cost equals the electricity cost share times the relative change in the electricity price.

Assumptions and validity

Short-run test: the selling price and non-electric costs are held constant. In a real crisis, input costs, interest rates and consumer demand move together, and a farm on a fixed-price electricity contract is protected until the contract ends — then exposed all at once.

What about calories? Lettuce contains only 13–15 kcal per 100 g, about 150 kcal per kilogram (USDA, 2019). A large vertical farm producing 1,000 t of lettuce per year therefore delivers \(1.5\times10^{8}\) kcal per year, or 411,000 kcal per day — the energy needs of about 160 people at 2,627 kcal per day. Its contribution to preparedness lies in fresh vitamins and minerals, which domestic production covers least well (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025), not in energy. The same holds for urban agriculture: a meta-analysis of 200 studies found urban yields on a par with or higher than conventional yields per area (Payen et al., 2022), and a global estimate put the potential of intensive urban agriculture at 100–180 million tonnes of food per year (Clinton et al., 2018) — valuable, but a small fraction of global food production. In Sweden, hobby gardening contributes about 8 kcal per person and day, recreational fishing 3 and wild berries 3 (Livsmedelsverket & Jordbruksverket, 2025). Urban farms build skills, social capital and dietary diversity — all resourcefulness — more than calorie security.

Designing a resilient indoor farm

Resilience can be engineered in: on-site solar power and batteries or a standby generator for the critical loads (pumps, fans, controls) rather than the lights; a heat store; several smaller sites instead of one large one (redundancy); six months of seeds, nutrient salts and spare LED drivers on the shelf (cheap absorptive capacity); a control system that runs locally without the cloud; flexible contracts that let the farm dim its lights when electricity is scarce; and a crop mix that includes energy-dense or storable products where the physiology allows.

Networks, correlated shocks and chokepoints

Diversification works only if the baskets do not fall at the same time. Portfolio theory, developed for financial assets by Markowitz (1952), makes this precise. Let total supply be the sum of the supplies of several sources, each fluctuating from year to year.

Variance of a supply portfolio(15.2.8)
\[ \sigma_{\text{tot}}^{2} = \sum_{i} w_i^{2}\sigma_i^{2} + \sum_{i}\sum_{j\ne i} w_i w_j\,\rho_{ij}\,\sigma_i\sigma_j \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{CV}_{\text{tot}} = \text{CV}\,\sqrt{\frac{1+(n-1)\rho}{n}} \]

the variance of total supply is the sum of the variances of the weighted sources plus the sum of all pairwise covariances; for n equal sources with the same coefficient of variation and a common correlation ρ, the coefficient of variation of the total is the individual coefficient of variation times the square root of one plus (n − 1) ρ, divided by n.

\(w_i\)share of source \(i\) in expected supply, \(\sum w_i=1\)–
\(\sigma_i\)standard deviation of source \(i\), relative to its mean–
\(\rho_{ij}\)correlation between the fluctuations of sources \(i\) and \(j\)–1 to 1
CV, CV\(_{\text{tot}}\)coefficient of variation of one source and of the total–
\(n\)number of equal sources–
Follow the derivation
  1. Total supply relative to its mean is \(Y=\sum_i w_iX_i\), where \(X_i\) has mean 1 and standard deviation \(\sigma_i\) (so \(\sigma_i\) is also its CV).
  2. The variance of a sum is the sum of all covariances: \(\text{Var}(Y)=\sum_i\sum_j w_iw_j\,\text{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\) with \(\text{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j\) and \(\rho_{ii}=1\). Separate \(i=j\) from \(i\ne j\) to get the first form.
  3. Equal sources: \(w_i=1/n\), \(\sigma_i=\sigma\), \(\rho_{ij}=\rho\). There are \(n\) diagonal terms and \(n(n-1)\) off-diagonal terms: \(\sigma_{\text{tot}}^2 = n\frac{\sigma^2}{n^2}+n(n-1)\frac{\rho\sigma^2}{n^2} = \sigma^2\frac{1+(n-1)\rho}{n}\).
  4. Independent sources (\(\rho=0\)): \(\text{CV}_{\text{tot}}=\text{CV}/\sqrt n\) — four independent suppliers halve the variability. Perfectly correlated (\(\rho=1\)): \(\text{CV}_{\text{tot}}=\text{CV}\) — diversification is useless.
  5. As \(n\to\infty\), \(\text{CV}_{\text{tot}}\to\text{CV}\sqrt{\rho}\): the common risk cannot be diversified away. The effective number of independent sources is \(n_{\text{eff}} = n/[1+(n-1)\rho]\), which can never exceed \(1/\rho\).
Assumptions and validity

Uses variance as the measure of risk, which suits frequent, moderate fluctuations. Food crises are driven by rare, large, simultaneous failures — the tails of the distribution — and correlations tend to rise precisely in crises (a global heat wave, a war, export bans). Correlation is not only a matter of weather. Trade networks create it. Using staple-crop trade data for 1992–2009, Puma et al. (2015) showed that the number of wheat and rice trade connections doubled while flows grew by 42 % and 90 % — a more connected, more efficient network, but one in which large regional disruptions propagate further, not least because exporters tend to stop exporting when markets are tight. Least developed countries lost the most. In May 2022, after a heat wave had cut its harvest, India — the world's second-largest wheat producer — banned wheat exports: the kind of policy response that couples otherwise independent markets.

The physical network matters too. Bailey and Wellesley (2017) identified fourteen chokepoints — maritime straits and canals such as the Turkish Straits, the Suez Canal, Bab-el-Mandeb, the Strait of Hormuz, the Strait of Malacca and the Panama Canal, plus critical coastal and inland infrastructure such as the Black Sea ports and the inland waterways and Gulf Coast ports of the USA — through which a large share of internationally traded grain and fertiliser passes. A chokepoint couples every flow that crosses it: a single blockage raises the correlation of many suppliers to one. Designing for resilience

Efficiency and resilience pull in opposite directions. Every spare warehouse, second supplier and unused hectare looks like waste on a balance sheet — until the shock arrives. Ecologists found the same tension in natural networks. Ulanowicz et al. (2009) measured the organisation of flow networks with Shannon's information theory and showed that sustainable ecosystems sit in a "window of vitality": neither maximally efficient (all flow along a few optimal pathways, brittle) nor maximally redundant (many parallel pathways, stagnant). In their measure, robustness \(R = -a\ln a\) peaks at intermediate values of the relative organisation \(a\) (Figure 9).

Key terms

Food-system resilience
The capacity over time of a food system and its units at multiple levels to provide sufficient, appropriate and accessible food to all in the face of various, even unforeseen, disturbances (after Tendall et al., 2015).
Shock and stress
A shock is a sudden disturbance (drought, war, pandemic, blocked canal); a stress is a slow, persistent pressure (soil degradation, climate trends) that erodes the capacity to cope with shocks.
Engineering resilience
The speed with which a system returns to its single equilibrium after a disturbance.
Ecological resilience
The magnitude of disturbance a system can absorb before it shifts into a different state with different structure and feedbacks (Holling, 1973).
Absorptive, adaptive and transformative capacity
The capacities to cope without change (buffers), to adjust incrementally (new suppliers, crops, diets) and to change the system itself (new energy base, institutions).
Four Rs
Robustness (withstanding a shock), redundancy (substitutable elements), resourcefulness (mobilising resources) and rapidity (speed of recovery) (Bruneau et al., 2003).
Resilience triangle
The area between normal functionality and the functionality curve after a shock, \(L=\int(100-Q)\,\mathrm{d}t\); for a linear recovery \(\tfrac12\Delta Q\,T_r\). Normalised by the control period it gives the resilience index \(\Phi\).
Shannon diversity index
\(H=-\sum p_i\ln p_i\), the information entropy of supply shares; \(e^{H}\) is the effective number of equally important suppliers.
Herfindahl–Hirschman index (HHI)
The sum of squared shares; above 0.25 a supply (or market) is highly concentrated. \(1/\text{HHI}\) is the effective number of suppliers.
Self-sufficiency ratio (SSR)
Domestic production divided by domestic supply (production + imports − exports), in per cent.
Import dependency ratio (IDR)
Imports divided by domestic supply, in per cent.
Hidden dependency
Reliance of domestic production on imported inputs — fertiliser, fuel, feed, seed, crop protection, spare parts — that production statistics do not show.
Rotation stock
An emergency stock owned by the state but stored and continuously turned over by private firms, so that it does not spoil (the model used for Swedish emergency grain stocks from 2026).
Chokepoint
A strait, canal, port or inland route through which a large share of trade passes, so that its blockage couples many flows at once.
Portfolio effect
The reduction in the variability of total supply obtained by combining sources whose fluctuations are not perfectly correlated; it vanishes as the correlation approaches one.
