Aeroponics: roots in air

Take away the soil, then take away the water tank as well. What is left is a root system hanging in a dark, humid chamber, kept alive by a mist that switches on for a few seconds every few minutes. Aeroponics gives roots more oxygen than any other growing system — but it also replaces the huge water buffer of soil or a nutrient tank with a film of liquid a few hundredths of a millimetre thick. This lesson treats aeroponics as the engineering problem it really is: a problem of droplet physics, water films, timers, pumps and what happens when the power fails.

What you will be able to do

  • Define aeroponics and distinguish high-pressure, low-pressure and ultrasonic (fogponic) systems by pressure, droplet size, hardware and typical applications (LO1, LO2).
  • Describe a spray quantitatively with number-mean and Sauter mean diameters, and use the log-normal distribution to see which droplets carry most of the water (LO2, LO3).
  • Derive Stokes' law for the terminal velocity of a droplet, check its validity with the Reynolds number, and calculate droplet evaporation times and deposition on roots (LO2, LO3).
  • Design an on/off misting cycle from a water balance of the root film, and estimate how long roots survive a pump or power failure (LO3, LO7).
  • Explain why aeroponic roots are well oxygenated, evaluate the evidence for claimed water, nutrient and yield advantages, and name the main commercial uses such as seed-potato minitubers (LO1, LO2, LO7).

Roots in air: what aeroponics is

In every soilless system you have met so far, roots sit in something: a nutrient film, a tank of aerated solution or a porous substrate such as rock wool (Lesson 5.1). Aeroponics removes that medium. The plant is held in a collar or net pot in the lid of a closed, dark root chamber; its roots hang freely in air and are wetted intermittently by a fine spray (an aerosol) of nutrient solution. Excess liquid drips off the roots, collects at the bottom of the chamber and is normally returned to the reservoir (Weathers & Zobel, 1992; Lakhiar et al., 2018).

Three variants are distinguished mainly by how the droplets are made:

  • High-pressure aeroponics (HPA): a positive-displacement pump pressurises the nutrient solution to roughly 5.5–7 bar (80–100 psi); small-orifice nozzles break it into droplets of roughly 5–50 µm. This is "true" aeroponics in the sense of NASA-funded research and many commercial systems (Lakhiar et al., 2018).
  • Low-pressure aeroponics (LPA): an ordinary submersible or garden pump (well below 5 bar) drives sprinklers, spray jets or a solution trickling down a tower. The droplets are coarse, typically larger than about 100 µm, and much of the solution runs over the roots as a film, so these systems behave partly like hydroponics. Most home "aeroponic towers" belong here.
  • Ultrasonic fogging ("fogponics"): a piezoelectric disc vibrating at megahertz frequencies under a thin water layer throws off a fog of droplets of only a few micrometres. No high-pressure plumbing is needed, but the fog carries little water per unit time and, as we will see, very small droplets are poor at landing on roots.

The boundaries are fuzzy. Weathers and Zobel (1992) already contrasted aeroponics with "aero-hydroponics", in which a jet of solution entrains air and runs over the roots; commercial systems today also mix principles, for example an aeroponic nursery followed by a hydroponic finishing stage. What unites them is the idea that roots should live in air saturated with water vapour rather than in liquid water.

Key idea

Aeroponics trades a large, slow water buffer (a tank, a substrate) for a tiny, fast one (a thin film on the root surface, replenished by mist). This gives roots excellent access to oxygen, but it means that the system's reliability — pumps, nozzles, timers and power — becomes part of the plant's physiology.

From root research to seed potatoes and space

Aeroponics was invented as a laboratory tool, not as a farming method. Plant pathologists and physiologists wanted to watch roots grow, sample them without digging and expose them to precisely controlled conditions. In 1942 W. A. Carter described an apparatus for growing plants with their roots in water vapour "to facilitate examination of roots" (Carter, 1942). In the 1950s Frits Went, working with controlled-environment growth chambers, grew plants with roots in a nutrient mist and is usually credited with the name "aeroponics" (Went, 1957). In 1976 Zobel, Del Tredici and Torrey published a simple, cheap design in which the spinning disc of a household humidifier threw nutrient solution into a plastic-lined plywood box — enough to grow herbaceous and woody species, including nodulated legumes (Zobel et al., 1976).

History

Why would a root researcher want to grow roots in air? Because in soil you cannot see them, and in water they are surrounded by a liquid whose oxygen, pH and ion concentrations change with distance from the root. In a mist, every root tip is exposed to almost the same conditions and can be photographed, sampled or inoculated at any time. Much of our knowledge of root hairs, nodulation and root diseases comes from such chambers.

Anatomy of an aeroponic system

A high-pressure aeroponic unit is a small hydraulic plant. Figure 2 shows the typical components. Solution is drawn from a reservoir, filtered, pressurised by a pump, stored in a pressure accumulator (a steel vessel with a gas-filled bladder that acts as a hydraulic "battery"), released by a solenoid valve under the control of a timer or controller, and atomised by nozzles inside the root chamber. Run-off drains back to the reservoir, often through a disinfection step, because recirculating solution can carry root pathogens from plant to plant (Lesson 5.3).

For ultrasonic fog the droplet size is set by physics rather than by nozzle geometry. The vibrating surface forms standing capillary waves whose wavelength follows from Kelvin's dispersion relation, \( \lambda_c = (8\pi\sigma/\rho f^2)^{1/3} \), and Lang (1962) found experimentally that the median droplet diameter is about \(0.34\,\lambda_c\). For water (\(\sigma = 0.072\) N m⁻¹, \(\rho = 1000\) kg m⁻³) at an example frequency of 1.7 MHz, \(\lambda_c = (8\pi\times0.072/(1000\times(1.7\times10^{6})^2))^{1/3} = 8.6\) µm, so the median droplet is about 2.9 µm — a true fog.

Describing a spray: mean diameters and distributions

No nozzle produces droplets of a single size. A spray is a population spanning one to two orders of magnitude in diameter, and a single number can describe it only if we say which average we mean. The arithmetic (number) mean diameter \(D_{10}\) tells you about the typical droplet; but for aeroponics the more useful quantity is often the Sauter mean diameter \(D_{32}\), the diameter of a droplet that has the same volume-to-surface ratio as the whole spray.

Sauter mean diameter(7.1.1)
\[ D_{32} = \frac{\sum_i n_i\,d_i^{3}}{\sum_i n_i\,d_i^{2}} \qquad\text{compared with}\qquad D_{10} = \frac{\sum_i n_i\,d_i}{\sum_i n_i} \]

the Sauter mean diameter is the sum of the droplet counts times their diameters cubed, divided by the sum of the counts times their diameters squared. Because the numerator is proportional to total liquid volume and the denominator to total liquid surface, \(D_{32}\) is six times the volume of the spray divided by its surface area.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(D_{32}\)Sauter (volume–surface) mean diameterµm
\(D_{10}\)arithmetic (number) mean diameterµm
\(n_i\)number of droplets in size class \(i\)–
\(d_i\)diameter of size class \(i\)µm
Follow the derivation
  1. A sphere of diameter \(d\) has volume \(V=\pi d^3/6\) and surface \(S=\pi d^2\), so its volume-to-surface ratio is \(V/S = d/6\): bigger droplets have relatively less surface.
  2. For the whole spray, total volume is \(\sum n_i\pi d_i^3/6\) and total surface \(\sum n_i\pi d_i^2\).
  3. Ask: what single diameter \(D\) would give the same ratio? \(D/6 = \dfrac{\sum n_i\pi d_i^3/6}{\sum n_i\pi d_i^2}\). The \(\pi\) and 6 cancel, leaving \(D = \sum n_i d_i^3/\sum n_i d_i^2 \equiv D_{32}\).
  4. The subscripts refer to the powers in numerator and denominator. In the same notation \(D_{30}=(\sum n d^3/\sum n)^{1/3}\) is the volume-mean diameter.
Why aeroponics engineers care about \(D_{32}\)

Evaporation, nutrient transfer and wetting all happen at the droplet surface, while the amount of water and nutrients delivered depends on droplet volume. \(D_{32}\) therefore captures the balance between the two and is the standard summary statistic in atomisation engineering (Lefebvre & McDonell, 2017). A spray with a small \(D_{10}\) but a large \(D_{32}\) consists of countless fine droplets that carry very little liquid, plus a few large ones that carry most of it.

\(\dfrac{\mu\text{m}^3}{\mu\text{m}^2} = \mu\text{m}\) ✓ (the counts \(n_i\) are dimensionless)

Reveal the reasoning

  3. Volume sum. \(\sum n d^3 = 400\cdot125 + 300\cdot3375 + 200\cdot27\,000 + 100\cdot216\,000 = 28\,062\,500\) µm³.
  4. Sauter mean. \(D_{32} = 28\,062\,500/617\,500 = 45.4\) µm.
  5. Volume share of the largest class. \(21\,600\,000/28\,062\,500 = 0.77\): only 10 % of the droplets carry 77 % of the liquid.

Measured spray distributions are usually close to log-normal: the logarithm of the diameter is normally distributed with a median (the count median diameter, CMD) and a geometric standard deviation \(\sigma_g\). For such a distribution, every mean diameter can be calculated from these two numbers with the Hatch–Choate relations (Hinds, 1999).

Mean diameters of a log-normal spray (Hatch–Choate)(7.1.2)
\[ D_{10} = \text{CMD}\,e^{\,0.5\ln^2\sigma_g},\qquad D_{32} = \text{CMD}\,e^{\,2.5\ln^2\sigma_g},\qquad \text{MMD} = \text{CMD}\,e^{\,3\ln^2\sigma_g} \]

each characteristic diameter equals the count median diameter multiplied by the exponential of a constant times the square of the natural logarithm of the geometric standard deviation; the constant grows from 0.5 for the number mean to 2.5 for the Sauter mean and 3 for the mass (volume) median diameter.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
CMDcount median diameter (half the droplets are smaller)µm5–30 (HPA)
\(\sigma_g\)geometric standard deviation (≥ 1; 1 = all droplets equal)–1.5–2.2
MMDmass (volume) median diameter: half the liquid volume is in smaller dropletsµm
Follow the derivation
  1. Write \(x=\ln d\), normally distributed with mean \(\mu=\ln\text{CMD}\) and standard deviation \(s=\ln\sigma_g\).
  2. The \(k\)-th moment of a log-normal variable is \(\langle d^k\rangle = e^{k\mu + k^2 s^2/2}\) (the moment-generating function of the normal distribution).
  3. \(D_{32} = \langle d^3\rangle/\langle d^2\rangle = e^{3\mu+4.5s^2}/e^{2\mu+2s^2} = e^{\mu+2.5s^2} = \text{CMD}\,e^{2.5\ln^2\sigma_g}\).
  4. Weighting the distribution by \(d^3\) (volume) shifts its median by \(3s^2\) in log space, hence \(\text{MMD}=\text{CMD}\,e^{3\ln^2\sigma_g}\).
The exponential factors are dimensionless, so each diameter carries the unit of CMD (µm) ✓

Droplet physics: settling, evaporation and deposition

Once a droplet leaves the nozzle, three things can happen to it: it can settle under gravity onto a root or the chamber floor, it can be carried by the air and impact on a root as the air flows around it, or it can evaporate before it arrives anywhere. Which of these dominates depends almost entirely on the droplet diameter — and on the humidity of the chamber.

Settling: Stokes' law

A small droplet falling through still air quickly reaches a constant terminal velocity at which the drag force balances its weight (minus buoyancy). For droplets small enough that the air flows smoothly around them, the drag was derived by George Stokes in 1851.

Terminal settling velocity (Stokes' law)(7.1.3)
\[ v_t = \frac{(\rho_p-\rho_a)\,g\,d^{2}}{18\,\mu} \approx \frac{\rho_p\,g\,d^{2}}{18\,\mu}, \qquad \text{valid if}\quad \text{Re} = \frac{\rho_a\,v_t\,d}{\mu} \lesssim 1 \]

the terminal velocity equals the density difference between droplet and air, times gravitational acceleration, times the diameter squared, divided by eighteen times the dynamic viscosity of air. The formula holds while the droplet Reynolds number — air density times velocity times diameter over viscosity — stays below about one.

Follow the derivation
  1. Forces on a falling sphere. Weight \(F_g = \rho_p\,g\,\pi d^3/6\) downward; buoyancy \(F_b = \rho_a\,g\,\pi d^3/6\) upward (Archimedes: the weight of displaced air); drag \(F_D\) upward, opposing the motion.
  2. Stokes drag. For creeping flow (inertia of the air negligible compared with viscous forces, \(\text{Re}\ll1\)) Stokes solved the Navier–Stokes equations around a sphere and found \(F_D = 3\pi\mu\,d\,v\). Two-thirds of this comes from viscous shear on the surface and one-third from the pressure difference between front and back.
  3. Terminal velocity. At constant speed the net force is zero: \(3\pi\mu d v_t = (\rho_p-\rho_a) g\,\pi d^3/6\).
  4. Solve. Divide both sides by \(3\pi\mu d\): \(v_t = (\rho_p-\rho_a) g\,d^2/(18\mu)\). Because \(\rho_a/\rho_p \approx 0.0012\), buoyancy can be neglected.
  5. How fast is "quickly"? The velocity approaches \(v_t\) exponentially with the relaxation time \(\tau = \rho_p d^2/(18\mu)\), so \(v_t = g\tau\). For a 20 µm droplet \(\tau = 998\times(20\times10^{-6})^2/(18\times1.81\times10^{-5}) = 1.2\) ms: after a few milliseconds the droplet falls at constant speed.
Assumptions and validity

(i) The droplet is a rigid sphere — true for small droplets, whose surface tension keeps them spherical. (ii) \(\text{Re}\lesssim1\): for water droplets in air this means \(d\lesssim 60\text{--}80\) µm; for 100 µm droplets (Re ≈ 2) Stokes' law overestimates the speed by roughly 10–20 %, and for millimetre drops it fails completely. (iii) The air is still — in a root chamber the spray jet and fans move air at decimetres per second, much faster than the settling speed of fine droplets, so fine droplets go where the air goes. (iv) Below about 1 µm the air can no longer be treated as a continuum and a slip correction (Cunningham factor) increases \(v_t\) (Hinds, 1999).

\(\dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-3}\cdot\text{m s}^{-2}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{Pa s}} = \dfrac{\text{kg s}^{-2}}{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}} = \text{m s}^{-1}\) ✓ (1 Pa s = 1 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹)

  2. Check Re. \(\text{Re} = 1.20\times0.0120\times20\times10^{-6}/1.81\times10^{-5} = 0.016 \ll 1\) ✓ Stokes' law is excellent.
  3. Fall time. \(0.60/0.0120 = 50\) s — almost a minute, during which any air current will carry the droplet sideways.
  4. 100 µm. Because \(v_t\propto d^2\), the speed is \(25\times\) larger: 0.30 m s⁻¹; but \(\text{Re} = 1.2\times0.30\times10^{-4}/1.81\times10^{-5} = 2.0\), so the true speed is somewhat lower (≈ 0.25–0.27 m s⁻¹). Fall time ≈ 2–2.5 s.

Impaction: when a droplet cannot follow the air

Air approaching a root must bend around it. A droplet with little inertia bends with the air and misses; a heavier droplet keeps going straight and hits. The ratio between the droplet's "stopping distance" and the size of the obstacle is the Stokes number.

Stokes number for impaction on a root(7.1.4)
\[ \text{Stk} = \frac{\tau\,U}{d_c} = \frac{\rho_p\,d^{2}\,U}{18\,\mu\,d_c} \]

the Stokes number is the droplet relaxation time multiplied by the air velocity approaching the root, divided by the root diameter. Large Stokes numbers mean the droplet cannot follow the curving air and impacts; small Stokes numbers mean it is swept around the root.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(\tau\)relaxation time of the droplet, \(\rho_p d^2/18\mu\)s10⁻⁵–10⁻²
\(U\)air velocity approaching the rootm s⁻¹0.1–2
\(d_c\)root diameter (the "collector")m0.2–1 × 10⁻³
Follow the reasoning
  1. A droplet moving at speed \(U\) into still air comes to rest after travelling its stopping distance \(S = U\tau\) (it decays exponentially with time constant \(\tau\)).
  2. The air turns around the root over a distance comparable to the root diameter \(d_c\).
  3. If \(S\gg d_c\) the droplet cannot turn in time and hits the root; if \(S\ll d_c\) it follows the streamlines. The ratio \(S/d_c\) is the Stokes number.
  4. For ideal (inviscid) flow around a cylinder no impaction occurs below a critical value of about \(\text{Stk}\approx 0.1\); efficient collection requires \(\text{Stk}\gtrsim1\) (see the treatment of fibre impaction in Hinds, 1999).
Assumptions and validity

This is a scale argument, not a precise collection efficiency. Real roots are clustered, hairy and wet, air in a chamber is turbulent near the nozzles and still elsewhere, and droplets can also reach roots by settling and, below ~1 µm, by Brownian diffusion. Use the Stokes number to compare droplet sizes, not to predict exact percentages.

\(\dfrac{\text{s}\cdot\text{m s}^{-1}}{\text{m}} = 1\) — dimensionless ✓

With \(U = 0.5\) m s⁻¹ and a 0.5 mm root, a 20 µm droplet has \(\text{Stk}\approx1.2\) and impacts readily, whereas a 3 µm fog droplet has \(\text{Stk}\approx0.03\) and flows around the root. Fine fog therefore reaches roots mainly by slow settling over long residence times, which is one reason ultrasonic systems deliver much less liquid to the roots per minute than high-pressure nozzles. In the other direction, droplets much larger than ~50–100 µm impact very efficiently but coalesce into runs and drips that leave most of the root system unvisited. The 5–50 µm window of high-pressure aeroponics is a compromise between these two failure modes.

Evaporation: the humidity of the chamber decides

A droplet surrounded by unsaturated air loses water by diffusion of vapour away from its surface. Because the diffusion flux is inversely proportional to the droplet radius, small droplets evaporate astonishingly fast. The result is the classical "\(d^2\)-law".

Evaporation time of a droplet (d²-law)(7.1.5)
\[ t_{\text{evap}} = \frac{\rho_w\,R\,T\,d_0^{2}}{8\,D_v\,M_w\,p_s(T)\,(1-\text{RH})} \]

the time for a droplet to evaporate completely equals the density of water times the gas constant times the absolute temperature times the initial diameter squared, divided by eight times the diffusivity of water vapour in air, times the molar mass of water, times the saturation vapour pressure, times one minus the relative humidity.

Follow the derivation
  1. Vapour flux from a sphere. In still air, steady diffusion from a sphere of radius \(r\) with vapour density \(\rho_{v,s}\) at its surface into air with vapour density \(\rho_{v,\infty}\) gives a mass loss rate \(\dot m = 4\pi r D_v(\rho_{v,s}-\rho_{v,\infty})\) (solve \(\nabla^2\rho_v = 0\) in spherical coordinates).
  2. Vapour densities. With the ideal gas law, \(\rho_v = p M_w/(RT)\), so \(\rho_{v,s}-\rho_{v,\infty} = \dfrac{M_w\,p_s(1-\text{RH})}{RT}\) if the droplet is at air temperature.
  3. Mass balance of the droplet. \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho_w\right) = -\dot m\) ⇒ \(4\pi r^2\rho_w\,\dfrac{dr}{dt} = -4\pi r D_v\Delta\rho_v\) ⇒ \(r\,dr = -\dfrac{D_v\Delta\rho_v}{\rho_w}dt\).
  4. Integrate from \(r_0\) to 0: \(\tfrac12 r_0^2 = \dfrac{D_v\Delta\rho_v}{\rho_w}t_{\text{evap}}\). The squared radius — hence \(d^2\) — decreases linearly in time: the \(d^2\)-law.
  5. Substitute \(r_0 = d_0/2\) and \(\Delta\rho_v\): \(t_{\text{evap}} = \dfrac{\rho_w d_0^2}{8D_v\Delta\rho_v} = \dfrac{\rho_w R T d_0^2}{8D_v M_w p_s(1-\text{RH})}\).
  6. How far does it fall meanwhile? Since \(v_t\propto d^2\propto(1-t/t_{\text{evap}})\), the distance fallen before disappearing is \(\int_0^{t_{\text{evap}}} v_{t,0}(1-t/t_{\text{evap}})\,dt = v_{t,0}\,t_{\text{evap}}/2\) — proportional to \(d_0^4\).
Assumptions and validity

Evaporation cools the droplet towards the wet-bulb temperature, which lowers its vapour pressure; the formula therefore underestimates evaporation times, most at low humidity. Dissolved nutrient salts lower the vapour pressure slightly (Raoult's law). Relative motion between droplet and air speeds up evaporation for larger droplets, and for droplets below about 1 µm gas-kinetic (Fuchs) corrections and the Kelvin effect matter (Hinds, 1999). The \(1/(1-\text{RH})\) factor is the key message: at RH = 99 % droplets live ten times longer than at 90 %.

From nozzle to root film: designing the misting cycle

High-pressure systems do not spray continuously. A typical controller opens the solenoid for a few seconds and then pauses for minutes. Why? Because what the root needs is a continuous water film, not a continuous spray: once the root surface and its root hairs are coated, extra liquid simply drips off. The design question is therefore: how long can the pause be before the film dries out, and how long must the pulse be to rebuild it? To answer it we need the nozzle flow rate, the water held on the roots and the rate at which the plant uses it.

Nozzle flow rate (orifice equation) and pressure scaling of droplet size(7.1.6)
\[ Q = C_d\,\frac{\pi d_o^{2}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho}}, \qquad \frac{D_{32}}{D_{32,\text{ref}}} \approx \left(\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta P_{\text{ref}}}\right)^{-0.4} \]

the volume flow through a nozzle equals a discharge coefficient times the orifice area times the square root of twice the pressure drop divided by the liquid density. For pressure-swirl misting nozzles the Sauter mean diameter falls roughly as the pressure drop to the power minus 0.4.

Follow the derivation
  1. Bernoulli. Between the pressurised pipe (velocity ≈ 0) and the jet leaving the orifice at atmospheric pressure, energy conservation per unit volume gives \(\Delta P = \tfrac12\rho u^2\), so the ideal jet velocity is \(u=\sqrt{2\Delta P/\rho}\).
  2. Flow. Volume flow is velocity times area: \(Q_{\text{ideal}} = u\,\pi d_o^2/4\).
  3. Real nozzles. Friction, the contraction of the jet (vena contracta) and — in swirl nozzles — the air core in the orifice reduce the flow; all these effects are lumped into \(C_d\).
  4. Consequence. \(Q\propto\sqrt{\Delta P}\): doubling the pressure increases the flow by only 41 %, but the higher jet velocity shatters the liquid into finer droplets. Now the root film. Suppose each plant's root system has a wetted surface \(A_r\) that can hold a film of average thickness \(h_f\) before liquid starts to drip off. The film is drawn down by uptake \(U\) (the plant replaces transpired water) and, if the chamber air is not saturated, by evaporation \(E\) from the root surface. It is refilled only while the nozzles spray.

Misting-cycle design: maximum pause and minimum duty cycle(7.1.7)
\[ t_{\text{off,max}} = \frac{\varphi\,A_r\,h_f}{U + E}, \qquad D = \frac{t_{\text{on}}}{t_{\text{on}}+t_{\text{off}}} \;\ge\; D_{\min} = \frac{U+E}{\eta\,q} \]

the longest allowable pause equals the fraction of the film we are willing to use up, times the film volume (root area times film thickness), divided by the rate at which the film is consumed by uptake and evaporation. The fraction of time the nozzles are on — the duty cycle — must be at least the consumption rate divided by the rate at which spray actually lands on that plant's roots.

Follow the derivation
  1. Storage. The film volume per plant is \(V_f = A_r h_f\). Any liquid beyond \(V_f\) drains away; the film cannot store more.
  2. During the pause the film shrinks at \(dV/dt = -(U+E)\). We allow it to lose at most \(\varphi V_f\) before the next pulse, which gives \(t_{\text{off}}\le\varphi V_f/(U+E)\).
  3. Over a whole cycle (period \(T = t_{\text{on}}+t_{\text{off}}\)) the water deposited, \(\eta q t_{\text{on}}\), must at least equal the water consumed, \((U+E)T\) — otherwise the film shrinks cycle after cycle.
  4. Divide by \(T\eta q\): \(t_{\text{on}}/T\ge(U+E)/(\eta q)\). This is the minimum duty cycle.
  5. Pulse length. The pulse should also be long enough to rebuild the film in one go: \(t_{\text{on}}\gtrsim\varphi V_f/(\eta q)\). Anything beyond that is drainage (useful for flushing salts, costly in pumping).
Assumptions and validity

The film thickness and wetted area of real root systems are hard to measure and change as roots grow; the values above are illustrative assumptions chosen to show the method. Uptake follows transpiration and so varies with light, VPD and plant size (Lesson 2.2); in practice controllers therefore use shorter pauses during the photoperiod than at night. Root-hair zones hold more water than smooth older roots, and dripping is not instantaneous.

If the air in the chamber is not saturated — because the lid leaks, a fan exchanges air, or the pump has stopped — the film also evaporates. Treating the wet root surface like any wet surface exchanging vapour through a boundary layer gives a simple estimate.

\[ E = h_m\,A_r\,\rho_{v,s}(T)\,(1-\text{RH}), \qquad \rho_{v,s} = \frac{M_w\,p_s(T)}{R\,T} \]

the evaporation rate from the roots equals a mass-transfer coefficient times the wet root area times the saturated vapour density of the air times one minus the relative humidity.

The lesson of Eq. 7.1.8 is that a closed, saturated root chamber is part of the water supply. Fine droplets that evaporate (Worked example 3) are not wasted: they keep the chamber near 100 % RH, which switches off root-surface evaporation and allows long pauses between pulses.

Worked example 4Designing the timer for an aeroponic lettuce module

A module grows 25 lettuce plants m⁻². Each plant transpires about 80 g of water per day (≈ 2.0 kg m⁻² d⁻¹, close to the 2.1 kg m⁻² d⁻¹ NASA lists for lettuce; Anderson et al., 2018). Assume \(A_r = 0.08\) m² and \(h_f = 25\) µm per plant, \(\varphi = 0.5\), and a saturated chamber (E ≈ 0). One nozzle (0.4 mm, 6 bar, \(C_d = 0.6\)) serves four plants and 30 % of its spray lands on their roots. Find \(t_{\text{off,max}}\), the minimum duty cycle and a sensible timer setting.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Uptake. \(U = 80\ \text{g d}^{-1}/86\,400\ \text{s d}^{-1} = 9.26\times10^{-4}\) g s⁻¹ ≈ 0.93 µL s⁻¹ (1 g of water ≈ 1 mL).

  3. Maximum pause. \(t_{\text{off,max}} = 0.5\times2.0\ \text{mL}/0.93\ \mu\text{L s}^{-1} = 1.0/0.000926 = 1080\) s = 18 min.
  4. Nozzle flow. Eq. 7.1.6: \(Q = 0.6\times\pi(0.4\times10^{-3})^2/4\times\sqrt{2\times6\times10^5/1000} = 0.6\times1.257\times10^{-7}\times34.6 = 2.61\times10^{-6}\) m³ s⁻¹ = 9.4 L h⁻¹. Per plant \(q = 2.35\) L h⁻¹ = 0.65 mL s⁻¹; deposited \(\eta q = 0.20\) mL s⁻¹.

  7. Choose. On 5 s, off 5 min gives \(D = 5/305 = 1.6\,\% > 0.47\,\%\) ✓ and a pause of 5 min, well below 18 min, leaving a safety margin for larger plants, warm days or a partly blocked nozzle.
  8. Water sprayed. Per plant per day: \(0.65\ \text{mL s}^{-1}\times5\ \text{s}\times(86\,400/305) = 921\) mL — about 11 times what the plant uses; the rest drains back to the reservoir. This is why recirculation (and disinfection) is essential.

Why roots like air: humidity, oxygen and root morphology

Roots respire. Every molecule of ATP that powers nutrient uptake in the root comes from aerobic respiration, which needs oxygen at the root surface (Lesson 5.3). In deep-water culture the oxygen must dissolve in the solution and then diffuse through a layer of nearly stagnant water next to the root; in soil it diffuses through air-filled pores. In aeroponics the root is surrounded by air and covered only by a film a few tens of micrometres thick. Two physical facts make this decisive:

  • Air holds far more oxygen. At 20 °C, air contains \(0.21\times101\,325/(8.314\times293) = 8.7\) mol O₂ m⁻³; air-saturated water only about 9.1 mg L⁻¹ = 0.28 mol m⁻³ — some 30 times less.
  • Oxygen diffuses about 10 000 times faster in air (\(D\approx2\times10^{-5}\) m² s⁻¹) than in water (\(D\approx2\times10^{-9}\) m² s⁻¹).
Oxygen supply through a water layer (Fick's first law)(7.1.9)
\[ J_{\text{O}_2} = D_{\text{O}_2,w}\,\frac{C_{\text{sat}}-C_{\text{root}}}{\delta} \]

the flux of oxygen to the root surface equals the diffusion coefficient of oxygen in water times the difference between the saturation concentration at the outer surface of the water layer and the concentration at the root surface, divided by the thickness of the water layer.

\(J_{\text{O}_2}\)oxygen flux per unit root areamol m⁻² s⁻¹
\(D_{\text{O}_2,w}\)diffusion coefficient of O₂ in waterm² s⁻¹≈ 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ at 20 °C
\(C_{\text{sat}}\)O₂ concentration in equilibrium with air (Henry's law)mol m⁻³0.28 (9.1 mg L⁻¹ at 20 °C)

Follow the reasoning
  1. Fick's first law: diffusive flux is proportional to the concentration gradient, \(J = -D\,dC/dx\).
  2. Across a thin layer at steady state the profile is linear, so \(dC/dx\approx-(C_{\text{sat}}-C_{\text{root}})/\delta\).
  3. In aeroponics the outer surface of the film touches air and is kept at \(C_{\text{sat}}\) because the gas phase replenishes it 10 000 times faster than the liquid can transport oxygen.
  4. The maximum supply (with \(C_{\text{root}} = 0\)) is \(D C_{\text{sat}}/\delta\). With δ = 25 µm: \(2\times10^{-9}\times0.284/25\times10^{-6} = 2.3\times10^{-5}\) mol m⁻² s⁻¹ = 23 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. With δ = 1 mm of stagnant solution the maximum is only 0.57 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — forty times less.
Assumptions and validity

This compares diffusion limits, not actual respiration rates. In well-aerated, well-stirred DWC the unstirred layer is much thinner than 1 mm, and in practice it is warm solution (low \(C_{\text{sat}}\)) combined with high root respiration that causes hypoxia. The comparison explains why aeroponic roots are practically never oxygen-limited — as long as the film stays thin and the solution drains freely.

\(\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-1}\cdot\dfrac{\text{mol m}^{-3}}{\text{m}} = \text{mol m}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\) ✓

Root morphology responds. Roots grown in humid air are typically more branched and carry dense root hairs, which are suppressed when roots are submerged. In a direct comparison of two lettuce cultivars, aeroponic plants had significantly greater root length, surface area, volume and root-to-shoot ratio than plants in hydroponics or substrate — but the hydroponic plants had larger shoots (Li et al., 2018). Aeroponic plants invest more in roots, which is exactly what you want for potato minitubers or medicinal roots, but not necessarily for a lettuce head sold by weight. Droplet size and spray interval also shape the result: in butterhead lettuce, an air-assisted nozzle producing ≈ 11 µm droplets, sprayed for 5 min every 30 min, gave better growth and more stable solution chemistry than nozzles producing 5, 17 or 26 µm droplets or longer intervals (Tunio et al., 2021), and droplet size affected growth and antioxidant content of leaf lettuce in a related study (Lakhiar et al., 2019).

Common misconception

"Aeroponics uses 98 % less water and gives 45–75 % higher yields." These figures come from a NASA Spinoff article describing one company's system (NASA Spinoff, 2006) and do not state the baseline — they are best read as a comparison with conventional growing in soil. Most of the water saving comes from growing in a closed, recirculating system — which any recirculating hydroponic system or plant factory also does (Lesson 7.2) — not from misting as such. Controlled comparisons between aeroponics and good hydroponics show smaller and crop-dependent differences, sometimes in favour of hydroponics for shoot yield (Li et al., 2018). Reviews therefore call for more rigorous physiological studies before aeroponic advantages are generalised (Eldridge et al., 2020).

Where aeroponics does have a clear, well-documented advantage is in access to the root system. Roots, stolons and tubers can be inspected and harvested without disturbing the plant, and the root-zone environment can be changed within seconds. That is the reason it began as a research tool, and the reason it dominates one commercial application: the production of seed-potato minitubers.

Failure modes and how to design for resilience

A plant in soil can survive days without irrigation; a lettuce raft in a deep-water tank can survive a pump failure for hours because the tank still holds water (though oxygen will fall). An aeroponic root system has a buffer of a few millilitres per plant. Worked example 4 showed that in a saturated chamber the film lasts roughly half an hour without replenishment. But a power cut also stops fans and cooling, the lid may be opened to inspect, and humidity drops: at 85 % RH, Eq. 7.1.8 adds 2 mg s⁻¹ of evaporation per plant, and the whole film is gone in about 10–15 minutes. Root tips and root hairs, which have thin walls and no protective bark, begin to desiccate soon after. Losing an entire crop to a single stuck relay is a real risk that designers must engineer away.

The accumulator in Figure 2 is the first line of defence. It stores solution under the pressure of a pre-charged gas bladder; when the pump stops, the gas expands and pushes the stored liquid to the nozzles. How much can it deliver?

Worked example 5How many minutes does the accumulator buy?

The module of Worked example 4 has 40 nozzles (9.4 L h⁻¹ each) and an 8 L accumulator pre-charged to 6.0 bar(a), working between 6.5 and 9.0 bar(a). The timer runs 5 s on / 5 min off. During a power cut the controller is on a small UPS but the pump is not. How many spray pulses can the accumulator deliver, and how long does that extend the life of the root film?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Usable volume. \(V_{\text{use}} = 8\times6.0\times(1/6.5-1/9.0) = 48\times0.04274 = 2.05\) L.
  2. Volume per pulse. \(40\times9.4\ \text{L h}^{-1}/3600\ \text{s h}^{-1}\times5\ \text{s} = 0.52\) L.
  3. Number of pulses. \(2.05/0.52\approx3.9\) → 3 full pulses (the 4th would drop below \(p_{\min}\) before it ends).
  4. Time gained. 3 pulses × 5 min ≈ 15 min, after which the ≈ 18–30 min film reserve starts to run down.
  5. Conclusion. The accumulator bridges a short outage (and a generator start-up) but not a long power cut. Critical installations need a UPS or generator for the pump and an alarm that reaches a person — and, ideally, shorter pulses during an outage to stretch the stored volume.

Safety and resilience

An aeroponic system is only as reliable as its weakest component and its alarm chain. Design for a single-fault tolerant system: no single failed pump, valve, nozzle, sensor or power supply should kill the crop. For a teaching or project system, the simplest protection is to make the chamber deep enough to hold a few centimetres of solution at the bottom (a hybrid "aero-hydroponic" design): if the mist stops, the lower roots still reach water.

Applications and evidence: seed potatoes, farms and space

Seed-potato minitubers

Potatoes are propagated vegetatively, and every field generation of seed tubers accumulates viruses and other pathogens. Certified seed schemes therefore start each year from disease-free tissue-culture plantlets, which are grown to produce small "minitubers" in a protected environment; these are then multiplied for a few generations in the field. Aeroponics is ideally suited to the minituber step: stolons hang in the dark, humid chamber and tubers can be picked when they reach the target size while the plant continues to produce more.

  • In Spain, aeroponic potatoes produced more than 2.5 times as many tubers per plant as a hydroponic system and almost 70 % more tuber mass, although individual tubers were a third smaller and the vegetative cycle was extended to about seven months (Ritter et al., 2001).
  • At 60 plants m⁻² and harvests every 7 days, cultivar Zorba gave 118.6 g of minitubers per plant — four times more than at 100 plants m⁻² (Farran & Mingo-Castel, 2006).
  • The International Potato Center (CIP) adapted aeroponics to low-cost greenhouses in the Andes and reported yields of more than 100 minitubers per plant at its Huancayo facility (Otazú, 2010). A technical and economic analysis across Latin America found multiplication rates up to 45 times and more than 900 minitubers per m², at a lower cost per minituber than conventional systems despite higher set-up costs (Mateus-Rodriguez et al., 2013). CIP reported transferring the technology to ten countries in Latin America, Africa and Asia and five- to tenfold higher seed production than conventional pot systems (International Potato Center, n.d.).
Worked example 6Sizing an aeroponic seed-potato unit

A seed programme needs 150 000 minitubers per season. Using the figure of 900 minitubers per m² per season reported by Mateus-Rodriguez et al. (2013), how much aeroponic bed area is needed? If a conventional substrate system produces one fifth as many minitubers per m², how much area would that need?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Aeroponic area \(= 150\,000/900 = 167\) m² of growing beds.
  2. Conventional productivity \(= 900/5 = 180\) minitubers m⁻²; area \(= 150\,000/180 = 833\) m².
  3. Greenhouse floor area is larger than bed area (paths, reservoirs, work space); with a bed-to-floor ratio of 0.6, the aeroponic unit needs ≈ 280 m² of greenhouse — a single modest tunnel.
  4. Sanity check: the saving in protected area is what justifies the higher equipment cost and the need for reliable electricity.

Vertical farms and research

Aeroponics is used in several indoor vertical farms, most famously by AeroFarms, whose trays hold plants above a cloth through which roots grow into a misted chamber. The company's story — Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2023, a narrowed focus on one farm in Danville, Virginia, and acquisition by an affiliate of an investment office in 2026, now producing microgreens — is discussed in Lesson 7.2. It illustrates that a growing method, however elegant, does not by itself make a farm profitable. In research, aeroponics remains a method of choice for studying root development, root–microbe interactions and nutrient uptake, because root-zone conditions can be changed and sampled at will (Weathers & Zobel, 1992; Eldridge et al., 2020).

Space

Soil is heavy and water is precious in space, so NASA funded aeroponic research in the 1990s, including an experiment on the Mir station in 1997 (NASA Spinoff, 2006). Microgravity, however, changes droplet behaviour completely: without settling (Eq. 7.1.3 with \(g\approx0\)), liquid clings to surfaces by surface tension and free droplets drift. Current space plant-growth hardware therefore uses porous substrates and capillary wicking instead — a story continued in Lesson 7.3.

Frontier 2024–2026

Open research questions include how droplet size, spray interval and root-zone CO₂ and O₂ interact with root hair development and nutrient uptake; how beneficial microbes behave on aerial roots; and whether sensors of root-surface wetness can replace fixed timers with closed-loop control (Eldridge et al., 2020; Tunio et al., 2021). Combining a model of the root film (Eqs. 7.1.7–7.1.8) with a humidity sensor and a transpiration estimate is a neat student project in Lesson 10.4 style control.

Key terms

Aeroponics
Soilless cultivation in which roots hang in a closed, dark chamber and are wetted intermittently by an aerosol of nutrient solution.
High-pressure aeroponics (HPA)
Aeroponics with a pump pressure of roughly 5.5–7 bar and fine nozzles producing droplets of roughly 5–50 µm.
Low-pressure aeroponics (LPA)
Aeroponics driven by ordinary low-pressure pumps, producing coarse droplets (≳ 100 µm) and runs of solution over the roots.
Fogponics
Ultrasonic fogging of nutrient solution by piezoelectric transducers, producing droplets of a few micrometres.
Sauter mean diameter (D₃₂)
The diameter of a droplet with the same volume-to-surface ratio as the whole spray, \(\sum nd^3/\sum nd^2\).
Geometric standard deviation
The spread parameter \(\sigma_g\) of a log-normal size distribution; 1 means all droplets are equal.
Stokes' law
Drag on a small sphere in creeping flow, \(3\pi\mu dv\); gives the terminal velocity \(v_t = \rho g d^2/18\mu\) for Re ≲ 1.
Stokes number
Ratio of a particle's stopping distance to the size of an obstacle; large values mean impaction, small values mean the particle follows the air.
d²-law
For a diffusion-limited evaporating droplet, the squared diameter decreases linearly in time.
Duty cycle
Fraction of time a pump or valve is on: \(t_{\text{on}}/(t_{\text{on}}+t_{\text{off}})\).
Root water film
The thin layer of solution held on the root surface and root hairs between spray pulses; the aeroponic plant's only water buffer.
Pressure accumulator
A vessel with a gas-charged bladder that stores pressurised solution, smoothing pressure and bridging short pump outages.
Minituber
A small seed-potato tuber produced from disease-free plantlets in a protected environment, the first generation of certified seed.
