Monitoring protocol

Your report will be exactly as good as the numbers you write down during the next five weeks. This protocol tells you what to measure, how often, with which accuracy and in which format — from the daily EC and pH round to the harvest in the drying oven — so that at the end you can calculate growth rates, test your hypothesis and close the water, energy and light balances of your own system.

What you will be able to do

  • Follow a measurement schedule for the root zone, the climate, the light and the resources of your system (LO4, LO5).
  • Calibrate pH, EC and dissolved-oxygen meters, judge whether a probe is fit for use and document the calibration (LO5).
  • Measure plant growth non-destructively (photographs, leaf counts, height) and destructively (fresh mass, dry mass, dry-matter content, roots) (LO4).
  • Record the water and electricity data needed for the balances of your system (LO3).
  • Keep a tidy, validated data file that other people — and the course's laboratories — can read (LO4, LO5).

What to measure, and why

Every measurement in the project serves one of three purposes: it is a response (how the plants grew), a condition (what the plants experienced — which lets you check that the treatments were what you intended and that nothing else differed), or an input to a balance (water, electricity, light). Key idea — same time, same place, same way

Most of the “noise” in student data is procedural: measuring at 9:00 on Monday and 16:00 on Tuesday, the probe at the surface one day and at the bottom the next, a different phone camera every week. Fix the time of day, the probe position and the procedure in week 3 and write them into your plan. A measurement that is always taken the same way can be compared; one that is not, cannot (Poorter et al., 2012).

The daily round

The daily round takes about ten minutes for twelve jars. Always follow the same order — from the least to the most disturbing measurement — and write directly into a printed data sheet or the grow log on a phone or laptop.

Contamination between units

Rinse probes with deionised water between units and between treatments — especially when you go from a high-EC unit to a low-EC one. Measure units in the same order every day (for example J01 → J12), or better, block by block, so that any drift during the round affects all treatments equally.

Calibration procedures

A meter that has not been checked is an opinion, not a measurement. Calibrate at the start of each measurement day for pH, and weekly for EC and DO, and write every calibration into a calibration log (Table 3). The theory of the electrodes is in Lesson 9.2; the uncertainty budget in Lesson 9.3.

pH: two-point calibration with buffers 4.01 and 7.00

  1. Let the buffers reach room temperature; note the temperature. Use fresh portions — never pour used buffer back.
  2. Rinse the electrode with deionised water and blot it (do not rub). Immerse in pH 7.00, wait until the reading is stable, and accept the first calibration point.
  3. Rinse and blot; immerse in pH 4.01; wait and accept. Buffers bracket the working range of nutrient solutions (5.5–6.5).
  4. Read the slope and offset from the meter (or calculate them from the millivolt readings with the calculator below) and record them.
  5. Check: measure pH 7.00 again as a sample; it should read 7.00 ± 0.05. Store the electrode in its storage solution, never in deionised water.
Electrode slope and offset from a two-point calibration(P2.1)
\[ S = \frac{E_{7} - E_{4}}{7.00 - 4.01}, \qquad \text{slope \%} = 100\,\frac{|S|}{S_{\text{N}}(T)}, \qquad S_{\text{N}}(T) = \frac{\ln 10\,R\,(T + 273.15)}{F} \]

the electrode slope is the change in millivolts between the two buffers divided by their pH difference; its size relative to the theoretical Nernst slope at the calibration temperature is the slope percentage. The offset is the reading in the pH 7 buffer.

Acceptance criteria

As a practical guide, one manufacturer rates an electrode with a slope of 95–105 % and an offset within ±15 mV as in good condition, 90–94 % or ±15–35 mV as needing cleaning, and below 90 % or beyond ±35 mV as faulty (Mettler-Toledo, n.d.; Lesson 9.2). Record the values: a slope that falls from week to week tells you the electrode is ageing.

mV ÷ (pH units) = mV per pH ✓; slope % = (mV/pH) ÷ (mV/pH) × 100 — dimensionless ✓

EC: check against a standard, and compensate for temperature

Weekly, measure a conductivity standard of 1.413 mS cm⁻¹ at 25 °C (0.01 mol L⁻¹ KCl, the standard used in Lesson 5.2). If the reading deviates by more than about 3–5 %, recalibrate the meter according to its manual (Lesson 9.3, Table 6). Make sure the meter reports temperature-compensated EC (EC₂₅); if it does not, convert your readings:

Temperature compensation of EC(P2.2)
\[ \text{EC}_{25} = \frac{\text{EC}_{T}}{1 + \alpha\,(T - 25\,{}^{\circ}\text{C})} \]

the conductivity at the reference temperature of 25 °C equals the conductivity measured at temperature T divided by one plus the temperature coefficient times the difference from 25 °C.

\(\text{EC}_T\)uncompensated conductivity at the solution temperaturemS cm⁻¹

\(T\)solution temperature°C18–24

Dissolved oxygen, thermometers and the quantum sensor

  • DO: weekly air-saturation check — a membrane or optical sensor in water-saturated air (or in vigorously aerated water) must read 100 % saturation; convert to mg L⁻¹ with the temperature (saturation 9.09 mg L⁻¹ at 20 °C; Lesson 5.3). Clark-type sensors need stirring during the reading.
  • Thermometers: an ice-point check (crushed ice and water, 0.0 °C) at the start and the end of the project; compare all thermometers with each other in one bucket of water.
  • Quantum sensor: factory calibration, typically every two years; at the start, compare it with a second sensor if one exists. Level it, and keep your shadow off it (Lesson 4.1).

Non-destructive growth measurements

Non-destructive measurements follow the same plants through time, which gives growth curves and relative growth rates for every plant (Lesson 2.4). The main one is the projected leaf area from a top-view photograph; leaf number and height are cheap secondary responses.

  1. Set-up. Camera on a stand, fixed height and zoom, lens horizontal (check with a spirit-level app); the same diffuse light every time — switch the LED fixture off or photograph beside it, so that the purple light does not change the colours between treatments.
  2. Each plant, each week (DAT 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35), at the same time of day, with the ID label and the scale card in the frame. Name the file ID_YYYY-MM-DD.jpg; copy the photos to the group folder the same day.
  3. Segment and measure green pixels with a colour index such as ExG = 2G − R − B and a threshold (Woebbecke et al., 1995), in the plant-vision lab or with ImageJ/Fiji; area = green pixels × (card area ÷ card pixels). Enter the value in area_cm2.
  4. Leaf number: count every leaf longer than 1 cm that you can see without damaging the plant, including yellowing ones; at harvest count all leaves longer than 1 cm.
  5. Height: from the top of the stone-wool cube (or the lid surface) to the highest point of the plant, without stretching the leaves; resolution 0.5 cm.
From area to mass

Projected area is not mass: when leaves overlap and heads form, area grows more slowly than mass. At harvest you will have both for every plant, and a power-law calibration FW = a·Ab converts area into an estimate of fresh mass (Lesson 2.4, Eq. 2.4.10). A few spare plants harvested at DAT 14 and 21 make that calibration far more reliable — plan them as a separate “calibration” group, not as part of a treatment.

Destructive harvest measurements

The harvest (≈ DAT 35, week 8) delivers your primary response. It is also irreversible: rehearse it, prepare labels and bags the day before, and harvest block by block, so that time of day does not become confounded with treatment.

  1. Before cutting: final photograph, leaf count, height; blind tipburn score (0 = none, 1 = one or two small necrotic spots, 2 = several leaves affected, 3 = many leaves, 4 = inner leaves severely necrotic) by a person who does not know the treatments.
  2. Shoot fresh mass: cut the shoot at the surface of the stone-wool cube, remove any stone wool, and weigh immediately to 0.1 g (fw_g). Plant tissue loses water within minutes — weigh before you do anything else.
  3. Roots: lift the net pot, cut the roots below the cube, rinse them gently, blot with paper towel and weigh (root_fw_g); note colour and any brown, slimy roots.
  4. Drying: put each shoot into a labelled paper bag, open the head so that air reaches the inner leaves, and dry in a ventilated oven at 70 °C to constant mass — typically at least 72 h (Pérez-Harguindeguy et al., 2013). Weigh one bag daily from day 3; it is dry when two weighings 24 h apart differ by less than 1 %. Let the bags cool in a closed box before weighing (dry tissue absorbs moisture from the air).
  5. Dry mass: weigh the dry shoot without the bag to 0.01 g (dw_g) and enter it on the harvest row.
Dry-matter content and root-to-shoot ratio(P2.3)
\[ \text{DMC} = 100\,\frac{\text{DW}_{\text{shoot}}}{\text{FW}_{\text{shoot}}}, \qquad \text{water content} = 100 - \text{DMC}, \qquad \text{R:S}_{\text{FW}} = \frac{\text{FW}_{\text{root}}}{\text{FW}_{\text{shoot}}} \]

the dry-matter content is the dry mass of the shoot as a percentage of its fresh mass; the rest is water. The root-to-shoot ratio on a fresh-mass basis divides the root fresh mass by the shoot fresh mass.

Why dry mass matters

Fresh mass is what is sold, but it contains 94–97 % water whose amount depends on the time since the last watering, the time of day and how long the shoot waited before weighing. Dry mass is the photosynthetic product; you need it for light-use efficiency and for comparing systems per kilogram of dry matter (Lesson 8.4, Worked example 3).

Water and electricity for the balances

The resource-use indicators of Lesson 8.4 — litres per kilogram, kWh per kilogram, grams per mole of photons — need three input records that are easy to take and impossible to reconstruct afterwards. Enter them in the system log of the grow-log tool (columns date, unit, water_l, kwh, air_t, rh, notes).

  • Water in Kratky jars: weigh each jar (with lid and plant) at DAT 0 and weekly; the mass lost is the water that left the jar by transpiration and evaporation (1 kg ≈ 1 L). Alternatively read the level scale. Record the loss since the last reading in water_l.
  • Water in DWC and NFT: top up to a marked level with a measuring jug at every visit and record the volume added; record the initial fill and any solution discarded (with its volume) in the notes.
  • Electricity: a plug-in energy meter on the circuit of the lamp (and pump). Reset or read it at DAT 0, then weekly and at harvest (kwh, cumulative reading, unit ALL). Note the lamp power at the plug and the photoperiod — they let you check the reading: \(E \approx P\,t_{\text{photo}}\,n_{\text{days}}\).
  • Light: the PPFD maps and the photoperiod give the daily light integral (DLI).
Water use per plant and day, and the energy cross-check(P2.4)
\[ q_w = \frac{\Delta V}{n_p\,\Delta t}, \qquad E_{\text{expected}} = \frac{P_{\text{el}}\,t_{\text{photo}}\,n_{\text{days}}}{1000} \]

the water use per plant and day is the volume lost (or added) between two readings divided by the number of plants that share the container and by the number of days between the readings. The electricity you expect is the power at the plug in watts times the hours of light per day times the number of days, divided by a thousand to give kilowatt-hours.

Common misconception

“The water we added is the water the plants used.” Only if the level is restored to exactly the same mark, nothing leaked and no solution was discarded. In a Kratky jar, weighing measures the water that left the jar; the few hundred millilitres stored in the growing plant stay in the jar and are not counted — a difference of about 5 % that you can quantify with the harvest fresh mass. State which definition you used (Lesson 8.4).

Optional: an ESP32 data logger

A microcontroller logger turns spot readings into continuous records: day–night air temperature and humidity, solution temperature during a warm spell, even PPFD if you have an analogue quantum sensor. Lesson 9.4 describes a complete reference design for the project — an ESP32 board with a DS18B20 waterproof temperature probe, an SHT4x air sensor in a radiation shield, a 16-bit ADS1115 converter for analogue pH, EC and PAR signals, a real-time clock and a microSD card that stores every value in a CSV file before anything is sent over Wi-Fi.

  • Calibrate the logger's sensors exactly like the handheld meters, and compare them side by side once a week — a logger that disagrees with a calibrated handheld meter is wrong until proven otherwise.
  • Mains-powered electronics near water: the USB supply stays above the water level on an RCD-protected strip; only the waterproof probes go into the solution.
  • Store time stamps in UTC (ISO 8601), log the firmware version and every event (probe cleaned, power cut) in the notes.

Data-quality rules

Research data are trustworthy when anyone can see how every number was obtained and what happened to it afterwards (Lesson 9.3). Ten rules for the project:

  1. Write it down immediately — on the sheet or in the tool, never from memory at the end of the day.
  2. ISO dates and SI units in every file: 2026-02-16, mS cm⁻¹, °C, g, L. One unit per column, never inside the value (write 1.62, not “1.62 mS”).
  3. Empty is empty. A missing value is left blank; never enter 0 for “not measured”.
  4. Never delete or overwrite raw data. Correct an entry error in the tool and describe the correction in the notes; keep the original paper sheet or export.
  5. Outliers are investigated, not removed. A strange value is an error only when you have found the cause; an excluded plant is reported with its reason and your pre-planned rule (Lesson 3.2).
  6. Check every day's entries with the quality check of the grow log (range, duplicates, falling areas) and fix typos while you still remember the day.
  7. Four eyes. A second group member compares the typed values with the paper sheet once a week.
  8. Export after every session (CSV and JSON) into the group folder with the date in the file name.
  9. Metadata: instruments and IDs, calibration log, photographs of the set-up, the randomisation seed, events and deviations from the plan.
  10. Blind scoring for anything subjective (tipburn, colour): the scorer does not know the treatments.
Fabrication and falsification

Inventing a missed measurement, “smoothing” a value that looks wrong, or deleting a plant because it spoils the result are research misconduct, and in coursework they are cheating (Lesson 3.4). A gap in the data, honestly reported, costs you nothing.
