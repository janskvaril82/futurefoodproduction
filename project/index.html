Project · Overview

Course project: grow, measure, taste

For eight weeks your group runs a small food-production system of its own. You build a soilless system, grow a leafy crop from seed to harvest, measure everything that matters — growth, nutrients, water, electricity, light — and then ask the question every new food technology must answer: will people actually eat it? The project is where the physics, biology, chemistry, statistics and social science of the course meet real plants, real data and real people.

What you will be able to do

  • Plan, build and operate a replicated soilless growing experiment with a testable hypothesis, randomisation and blocking (LO2, LO4).
  • Monitor the system with calibrated sensors and image-based measurements, and keep a complete, quality-checked grow log (LO4, LO5).
  • Calculate growth rates, water, energy and light balances and resource-use indicators for your own system and compare them with published systems (LO3, LO1, LO7).
  • Fit growth models to your own data and forecast the harvest (LO5).
  • Design, run and analyse an ethical, legal sensory study of consumer acceptance of a novel food (LO4, LO6).
  • Report the whole project in an IMRaD report and a poster, with correct statistics and an honest discussion of limitations (LO4, LO7).

Aims: one system, two studies

Controlled-environment agriculture promises more food per square metre, per litre and per kilogram of fertiliser — but it pays in electricity, capital and complexity, and its products have to win over consumers who value "natural" food. You cannot judge those claims from brochures. In this project you generate the evidence yourself, at small scale, with the same indicators professionals use (Lesson 8.4).

The project has two linked studies:

  1. The growing experiment. Your group tests one factor — for example the electrical conductivity (EC) of the nutrient solution, the light intensity or spectrum, aeration, or the type of soilless system — in a replicated and randomised experiment with a leafy crop (butterhead lettuce by default, or basil or pak choi). You follow the plants non-destructively with photographs, measure the root-zone and the light, harvest destructively after about 35 days, and quantify growth and resource use.
  2. The sensory study. Your group designs and runs a small, ethical tasting study with a panel of volunteers: do people perceive a difference between a novel food and a conventional one, how much do they like it, and does their liking depend on food neophobia? The product must be legally on the EU market — you may, for example, compare bread with authorised cricket powder against wheat bread, or your hydroponic lettuce against soil-grown lettuce from the shop.

What makes an excellent project

Not a large lettuce. An excellent project has a sharp question, a fair test (replicated, randomised, blocked), complete and honest data (including the plants that died), quantitative answers with uncertainty, and a critical comparison with published systems. A failed crop that is well documented and well explained can earn the highest grade; a beautiful crop with a pseudo-replicated design cannot.

    Why a preregistered plan?

    Writing down your hypothesis, design, primary response and analysis before you have data protects you from the most common questionable research practices — choosing the analysis that gives the smallest p-value, or inventing the hypothesis after seeing the results (Lesson 3.1; Nosek et al., 2018). Deviations are allowed; they must simply be reported as deviations.

    Working as a group

    A growing experiment does not pause for weekends, and a group of four can easily duplicate or forget tasks. Distribute four roles and rotate them after weeks 3 and 6, so that everybody has built, measured, analysed and managed. In a group of three, the coordinator also manages the data.

    Safety first

    Your project combines mains electricity and water, intense light, chemicals, food and — in the sensory study — other people. Before you build anything, every member completes the safety induction in the first laboratory session. The detailed rules are in the build guide (P1, safety) and in the sensory guide (P5, ethics).

    Three rules without exceptions

    (1) No mains-powered device may be able to fall into, or be splashed by, the nutrient solution, and every socket strip is protected by a 30 mA residual-current device. (2) Concentrated stock solutions A and B are never mixed with each other — calcium from A and sulfate and phosphate from B precipitate (Lesson 5.2). (3) Nobody tastes anything in the sensory study that is not legally sold as food in the EU, and nobody tastes without informed consent.

    Safety induction — tick when done (each member):

    • I know where the RCD, the main switch, the eye-wash station and the first-aid kit of our lab are, and I have pressed the RCD test button.
    • I have read the safety data sheets of our fertiliser concentrates and pH-adjusting chemicals.
    • I know how to prepare stock and working solutions without mixing the concentrates.
    • I know why we never look into the LED fixture and how we switch it off to work close to the plants.
    • I know the rules for food safety and the sensory study, and whom to call in an emergency (112 in Sweden).

    How the project is assessed

    The project is assessed on the group report, the poster or presentation, the peer review you write, and an individual contribution statement. The rubric describes the criteria at each grade level, mapped to the learning outcomes of Table 1. The formally adopted course syllabus decides how the parts are weighted and how individual grades are set. Three principles apply throughout:

    • Process counts. The approved plan, the grow log and the approved sensory protocol are part of the evidence; a report cannot be better than the data behind it.
    • Honesty counts more than success. Failed plants, deviations from the plan and non-significant results are reported and explained, never hidden (Lesson 3.4).
    • Everyone must be able to explain everything. At the poster session any member can be asked about any part of the project.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can we choose our own crop, system or treatment?

    Yes, within the equipment available and after the teacher has approved your plan. Leafy crops with a short cycle (lettuce, basil, pak choi) fit the eight weeks; fruiting crops such as tomato or strawberry do not. Good treatments change one factor you can control reliably: EC, light intensity, spectrum, aeration, system type or substrate. See the treatment options.

    How many plants do we need?

    Count experimental units, not plants: the unit is the smallest thing to which a treatment is independently applied. With one plant per jar and EC as the treatment, a jar is a unit; with one lamp per light treatment, the lamp compartment is the unit. Plan at least four units per treatment and use a power analysis (Lesson 3.1) to see what you can detect. Twelve units in three treatments and four blocks is a typical, feasible design.

    What if our plants die or our result is not significant?

    Record what happened and why, keep the data, and analyse what you have. A non-significant result is reported with its confidence interval and the effect you could have detected (the minimum detectable difference). Dead plants are data too: report them and your pre-planned exclusion rule. You are graded on the quality of the science, not on the size of the lettuce.

    Who looks after the plants at weekends?

    The rota in your group contract. A Kratky system needs a check every one or two days; DWC and NFT depend on a pump and need a daily check and a failure plan (a spare air pump, a phone alarm, a colleague who can step in). Avoid treatments or systems whose failure would ruin the experiment over a long weekend.

    Can we eat our lettuce?

    Only if it was grown in food-grade containers with drinking-quality water and food-grade fertiliser, handled hygienically, and nothing non-food (laboratory chemicals, glue, uncoated metal) was in contact with the solution. Weigh the harvest first — the plants are your data. Your own produce may only be served in the sensory study if the teacher has approved it; purchased produce is the safer choice.

    May we serve insects, algae or cultivated meat in the tasting?

    Insect ingredients and other novel foods only if they are authorised under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, are bought from a registered food business and carry the required labelling; cultivated meat is not authorised in the EU (as of 2026) and may not be served. See choosing a legal product.

    Do we need ethical approval for the tasting?

    Not formal ethical review: the Swedish Ethical Review Act (2003:460) does not define work within first- and second-cycle education as research. But the ethical principles apply fully — informed consent, voluntariness, allergen information, anonymity, GDPR — and the teacher must approve your protocol before you recruit anyone (Lesson 13.3).

    Which statistical test should we use?

    The design decides: randomised complete block ANOVA for blocked growing experiments, one-way ANOVA or Welch's t-test for completely randomised ones, the exact binomial test for triangle tests, and a block ANOVA with panellists as blocks for hedonic ratings. The analysis guide and the statistics workbench walk you through it.

    Our lab has an ESP32 and sensors — must we use them?

    No. Manual measurements with calibrated handheld meters are enough for an excellent project. A logger adds high-resolution data (day–night temperature, solution temperature during a warm spell) and good material for LO5, but it also adds work: calibrate it and check it against the handheld meters.

    May we use generative AI?

    Within the course rules: for example to check grammar or to explain a concept, declared in an AI-use statement — never to generate data, results or text you present as your own, and never with personal data from the sensory study. See academic integrity and AI use.

    What if a group member does not contribute?

    Talk to the person first, with reference to your group contract. If that does not help, contact the teacher early. The individual contribution statements and the rota are used when individual grades are set.

    Our browser lost the grow log. What now?

    Import the latest CSV or JSON export into the grow log (Import) — this is why you export after every session. Then re-enter what is missing from your paper data sheets or lab notebook.
