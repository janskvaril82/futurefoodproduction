Sensory study of a novel food

A new food only feeds people if they choose to eat it. In the second study of the project your group puts that question to real people: can they taste a difference between a novel food and a familiar one, how much do they like it, does being told what it contains change their mind — and does their answer depend on how neophobic they are? This guide takes you from choosing a product that is legal to serve, through ethics and the tasting session, to the statistics and the report.

What you will be able to do

  • Choose a tasting product that is legally on the EU market and explain its status under the Novel Food Regulation (LO6).
  • Formulate a sensory research question with testable hypotheses and choose a suitable method — triangle, hedonic, preference, CATA or willingness to try (LO4, LO6).
  • Justify the panel size with a power calculation and state the minimum detectable difference (LO4).
  • Measure food neophobia and food-technology neophobia and relate them to acceptance (LO6).
  • Run an ethical tasting: informed consent, allergen information, anonymity under the GDPR, a controlled booth set-up and a balanced serving protocol (LO4, LO6).
  • Analyse and report discrimination and hedonic data correctly (LO4).

The question: will people eat it?

Acceptance of a new food is a chain: people must be aware of it, be willing to try it, like it when they taste it, and choose it again (Lesson 13.1). A small tasting study can measure three links of the chain — whether a difference is perceived, how much the product is liked, and how willing people are to try or buy it — and one of their strongest predictors, the trait of food neophobia. It can also separate the two sources of liking: what the product delivers to the senses (a blind test) and what people expect from what they are told (an informed test).

Choosing a product that is legal to serve

Under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, food not used for human consumption to a significant degree in the EU before 15 May 1997 is a novel food, and only novel foods authorised and included in the Union list may be placed on the market (Art. 6(2)). “Placing on the market” includes any transfer, whether free of charge or not (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, Art. 3(8)) — handing out an unauthorised novel food in a tasting is therefore not allowed, even for free, even for science (Lesson 13.3; Lesson 14.1). The course rule is simple: serve only foods that are legally on the EU market, bought from a registered food business, used as intended, with their labels and receipts kept.

Key idea — the panellist is the unit

Twenty-four panellists give twenty-four independent judgements, however many samples each tastes. When every panellist tastes every sample, treat the panellist as a block — the ratings of one person are correlated, and ignoring this is the most common error in student sensory reports (Lesson 13.4).

A realistic 40-minute session combines methods in a fixed order (Lesson 13.4): information and consent; the neophobia questionnaire and willingness to try; the triangle test first, while palates are fresh; the blind hedonic test (with CATA if used); then — separately — the informed question and willingness to buy; debriefing. Three or four samples per test is a sensible maximum for untrained panellists.

How many panellists?

Student panels of 20–40 are small by professional standards. That is acceptable if you plan for the effects you can realistically detect and say so in the report. Do the calculation before you recruit.

Power of a triangle test and panel size for a liking difference(P5.1)
\[ 1 - \beta = P\!\left(X \ge x_c \,\middle|\, n,\; p_c = p_d + \tfrac{1}{3}(1 - p_d)\right), \qquad n_{\text{hedonic}} \approx \left(t_{1-\alpha/2} + t_{1-\beta}\right)^{2}\frac{\sigma_d^{2}}{\Delta^{2}} \]

the power of a triangle test is the probability that at least the critical number of panellists answer correctly when a proportion pd of the population really discriminates (and the rest guess). For a within-panellist liking comparison, the number of panellists grows with the square of the ratio between the standard deviation of the panellists' differences and the difference you want to detect (Eq. 13.4.13).

Worked example 1What can a class of 24 detect?

You can recruit 24 classmates. (a) What is the critical number for a triangle test, and what is the power if 30 % of the population can really tell the breads apart? (b) For the hedonic comparison you expect an SD of within-panellist differences of about 1.6 points; how many panellists would 80 % power for a 1-point difference need, and what is the minimum detectable difference with 24?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) The smallest k with \(P(X\ge k\mid24,\tfrac13)\le0.05\) is 13 (Figure 3). With pd = 0.3, \(p_c = 0.3 + 0.7/3 = 0.533\), and the power \(P(X\ge13\mid24, 0.533)\) is only about 0.55 — little better than a coin flip.
  2. (b) Normal approximation: \(n = (1.960 + 0.842)^2\times1.6^2/1^2 = 7.85\times2.56 = 20.1\); iterating with t quantiles gives about 23 (the calculator above).
  3. MDD with 24: \((t_{0.975,23} + t_{0.80,23})\times1.6/\sqrt{24} = (2.069 + 0.858)\times0.327 = 0.96\) points (Lesson 13.4, Worked example 10).
  4. Consequence. A class panel is exploratory: it detects large differences only. Say so in the protocol, pool with another group if the protocols are identical, and prefer within-panellist designs.

Measuring food neophobia

The Food Neophobia Scale (FNS; Pliner & Hobden, 1992) has ten statements rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); five are reverse-scored, and the total runs from 10 (neophilic) to 70 (neophobic). For technology-based products — insects in processed form, mycoprotein, vertical-farm lettuce — add the 13-item Food Technology Neophobia Scale (FTNS, 13–91; Cox & Evans, 2008). Scoring, reverse items and reliability are explained in Lesson 13.1.

  • When: before tasting, so that the tasting cannot influence the answers.
  • Report: mean, SD and Cronbach's α of your panel, compared with a published reference (Swedish samples have reported means of about 25–27; Szakály et al., 2021). A negative α means you forgot to reverse-score.
  • Analyse: the correlation between FNS and liking of the novel sample (or a regression of liking on FNS); do not split the panel at the median — it throws information away.
  • Remember: students are usually less neophobic than the general population — a convenience sample.

Ethics, consent, allergens and data protection

A tasting study asks people to put something in their mouths; it is the most ethically sensitive part of the project, and your protocol (D7) must be approved by the teacher before you recruit anyone. The principles of research with human participants — respect for persons, voluntary informed consent, minimal risk, confidentiality — apply to a student tasting as much as to a clinical study (World Medical Association, 2025).

  • Swedish law. The Ethical Review Act (Lag (2003:460) om etikprövning av forskning som avser människor) requires approval by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority for research involving, for example, sensitive personal data or physical interventions. Its definition of research (§ 2) excludes work carried out within first- and second-cycle higher education, so a course tasting needs no formal ethical review — but the principles apply, the teacher approves the protocol, and your university's local rules for student projects must be followed. If the data are to be used in research, the Act applies and approval must be obtained before data collection (Lesson 13.3).
  • Informed consent. Written information and a signed consent before tasting: purpose, what participants do and for how long, every ingredient and every allergen, risks, voluntariness, the right to withdraw at any time without giving a reason, what data are collected and how they are kept, and whom to contact. Participation never affects grades; nobody is pressured by friends or teachers.
  • Allergens. The 14 allergens that EU law requires to be declared — cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphur dioxide and sulphites, lupin and molluscs (Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, Annex II) — plus product-specific warnings such as the crustacean, mollusc and dust-mite cross-reactivity of insect ingredients.
  • Exclusion by self-selection. State the exclusion criteria (allergies or intolerances to any ingredient, coeliac disease, diets that exclude the product, age under 18, feeling unwell) and let people exclude themselves — so that no health data enter your files (GDPR Art. 9).
  • Data protection. Record responses under panellist numbers; collect only what you analyse (Art. 5(1)(c)); keep signed consent forms physically separate from the data; do not make a code–name key; tell participants what you collect (Art. 13); store data on university systems and delete raw data when the course is over. Anonymous data fall outside the GDPR (Recital 26); pseudonymised data do not (Art. 4(5)).
  • Food safety and emergencies. Food-grade utensils, clean hands, cold chain, a clean kitchen (not a laboratory); a named person responsible for first aid; in an emergency call 112. Debrief afterwards: reveal the samples, thank everyone, answer questions.

Room and booth set-up

ISO 8589:2007 describes the ideal test room: a testing area with individual booths, a separate preparation area, neutral colours, uniform controllable lighting, no odours and no noise (Lesson 13.3). A classroom can approximate it well enough for the project:

Serving protocol

Everything that differs between samples other than the test variable is a competing explanation of your result. ISO 4120:2021 lists the rules for the triangle test, and they are good practice for every method (Lesson 13.3):

  1. Prepare out of sight and identically: same recipe, oven, batch, day, cutting and container; buy both lettuces on the same day and store them together.
  2. Code every sample with a random three-digit number — preferably new codes for every panellist; never 1, 2, 3 or A, B, C, and no memorable numbers such as 123 or 999.
  3. Balance the serving order: the six triangle arrangements ABB, AAB, ABA, BAA, BBA and BAB equally often, randomly within each group of six panellists; for three or more samples in a rating test, Williams Latin squares that balance position and first-order carry-over (Williams, 1949; MacFie et al., 1989). The sensory study designer generates both, with codes, serving sheets and ballots.
  4. Identical portions at the temperature of normal consumption — one or two bites (≈ 10–20 g of a solid, 15–30 mL of a liquid) are enough; serve all samples of a tray at the same moment.
  5. Palate cleansing: water and plain table-water crackers between samples (the cleanser that worked best across foods in Lucak & Delwiche, 2009), and a rest of at least one minute.
  6. Instructions: say whether to swallow or expectorate, taste from left to right, and give no information about the samples or expected effects until everyone has finished.
  7. Blind first, informed later: if you study the effect of information, run the informed part after the blind part, or in a separate session.

Analysis

  • Triangle: count the correct answers x of n panellists; the one-sided exact binomial test \(P(X\ge x\mid n, \tfrac13)\) (Eq. 13.4.2); report \(\hat p_d = (3x/n - 1)/2\) with its confidence interval and the Thurstonian d′ (Eqs. 13.4.3–13.4.4). With 13 of 24 correct, p = 0.028, p̂d = 0.31 (95 % CI 0.01–0.61) and d′ ≈ 1.7 (Lesson 13.4, Worked example 3).
  • Hedonic: randomised-block ANOVA with panellists as blocks, then Tukey's HSD (tukeyRCBD); report means with 95 % CIs and partial η². With two samples, the paired t-test gives the same result.
  • Preference: two-sided exact binomial test with p₀ = 1/2; do not count “no preference” as a vote.
  • CATA: Cochran's Q across samples for each word, McNemar for pairs; correct for the number of words tested.
  • Neophobia: Cronbach's α; correlation of FNS with the liking of the novel sample.

The sensory study designer analyses triangle, hedonic and preference data as soon as you enter the responses, and stores them in the format read by the triangle-test and hedonic-analysis laboratories.

Reporting the sensory study

Report the sensory study as its own Methods and Results subsections in the project report, following the checklist of Lesson 13.4, Table 6:

  • Aim, primary question and hypotheses; which analyses were planned and which are exploratory.
  • Samples: products, suppliers, novel-food status and authorisation, recipe and preparation, storage, portion size, shape, temperature, coding.
  • Panel: number, recruitment, age range, gender, exclusion criteria, neophobia scores with α.
  • Procedure: methods and exact questions, blind or informed, booths and lighting, serving design (arrangements, Williams square), palate cleansing, duration.
  • Ethics: informed consent, allergen information, data handling, teacher approval.
  • Statistics: test and model, exact p-values, effect sizes with CIs (pd, d′, mean differences, η²), power or minimum detectable difference.
  • Limitations: convenience sample, first exposure, small panel, single session.
