Analysing your growth data

Five weeks of measurements become three answers: how fast your plants grew, whether your treatments made a difference, and how efficiently your system turned water, electricity and light into food. This guide works through each analysis with an illustrative dataset — and every figure on the page can switch to your own grow log, so that you can repeat the analysis on your data as you read.

What you will be able to do

  • Calculate relative growth rates between measurement dates and describe how RGR changes as plants grow (LO3, LO4).
  • Fit a logistic growth curve to your own data, interpret its parameters and forecast the harvest (LO5).
  • Choose and carry out the correct treatment comparison — Welch's t-test, one-way ANOVA or randomised-block ANOVA with Tukey's HSD — and report effect sizes with confidence intervals (LO4).
  • Calculate water productivity, specific energy use, the daily light integral and light-use efficiency of your system, and compare them with published systems (LO3, LO1, LO7).
  • Estimate the uncertainty of your indicators and state what your data can and cannot show (LO4, LO5).

The example dataset

The worked examples use an illustrative, simulated experiment: butterhead lettuce in twelve 5 L Kratky jars (one plant per jar) under one 95 W LED fixture, three EC levels (0.8, 1.6 and 2.4 mS cm⁻¹) in four blocks along the light gradient under the fixture, 16 h photoperiod, harvest 35 days after transplanting. Load it in the grow log (“Load example”) to see every number. Its values are realistic but invented — never cite them as results.

Look at the data first

Before any test, plot every plant. The growth curves show whether the plants grew smoothly, whether one plant behaves oddly (a damaged leaf, a missing photo), and when the treatments started to diverge; the dot plot of the harvest shows the spread within treatments and any outlier. Tables of means without the spread hide exactly the information you need (Weissgerber et al., 2015; Lesson 3.2).

Common misconception

“We measured each of our four plants per treatment six times, so n = 24.” Repeated measurements of the same plant are not independent replicates — the plant, not the photograph, is the experimental unit. Use one value per plant (the harvest mass, or a per-plant growth rate) in the treatment comparison (Hurlbert, 1984; Lesson 3.1).

Relative growth rate

A plant grows like capital with compound interest: the more leaf area it has, the more it can add. The natural measure of growth is therefore the relative growth rate, the growth per unit of size already present (Lesson 2.4). With repeated photographs you can calculate it for every plant and every interval.

Relative growth rate between two measurement dates(P4.1)
\[ \text{RGR}_{i} = \frac{\ln W_{i,2} - \ln W_{i,1}}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad \overline{\text{RGR}} = \frac{\overline{\ln W_2} - \overline{\ln W_1}}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad t_d = \frac{\ln 2}{\text{RGR}} \]

the RGR of plant i is the difference between the natural logarithms of its size at the second and the first date, divided by the time between them. For a treatment, use the difference between the means of the logarithms (Hoffmann & Poorter, 2002). The doubling time is ln 2 divided by the RGR.

SymbolMeaningUnitTypical value
\(W\)size: projected leaf area (or fresh mass, dry mass — say which)cm² (g)10–600 cm²

\(\text{RGR}\)relative growth rated⁻¹0.2 early, < 0.05 at harvest
\(t_d\)doubling timed3–6 d for young lettuce
Why the logarithm?

If a plant grows exponentially, \(W = W_1e^{\text{RGR}(t-t_1)}\); taking logarithms, \(\ln W = \ln W_1 + \text{RGR}(t - t_1)\) — a straight line whose slope is the RGR. Between two dates the formula gives the mean RGR over the interval, whatever happened in between. Because \(\ln W\) of the same plant is measured on both dates, the per-plant RGRs are independent across plants and can be averaged and compared between treatments.

(ln cm² − ln cm²)/d = ln(cm²/cm²)/d = d⁻¹ ✓ — the logarithm of a ratio is dimensionless
Worked example 1RGR of one plant and of a treatment

Plant J05 (EC 1.6) had a projected leaf area of 43 cm² at DAT 7 and 137 cm² at DAT 14. (a) Calculate its RGR and doubling time. (b) The four EC 1.6 plants had mean ln-areas of 3.69 at DAT 7 and 4.94 at DAT 14; what is the treatment RGR? (c) Why do the RGRs of all treatments fall from about 0.20 d⁻¹ in the first week to below 0.03 d⁻¹ in the last week?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. (a) \(\text{RGR} = \ln(137/43)/7 = \ln 3.186/7 = 1.159/7 = 0.166\) d⁻¹; \(t_d = 0.693/0.166 = 4.2\) d — the plant's leaf area doubled about every four days.
  2. (b) \((4.94 - 3.69)/7 = 0.179\) d⁻¹ (the grow log gives 0.178 ± 0.007 d⁻¹, mean ± SE of the four plants).
  3. (c) Ontogenetic drift. As the rosette grows, leaves shade each other, a growing share of the plant is support tissue, and the projected area saturates when leaves overlap and the head closes (Lesson 2.4). Compare RGRs only over the same interval, never early RGR of one treatment with late RGR of another.
  4. Sanity check. 0.17 d⁻¹ lies within the 0.11–0.36 d⁻¹ reported for fast-growing herbaceous plants (Poorter & Remkes, 1990).
(a) 0.166 d⁻¹, doubling in 4.2 d; (b) 0.179 d⁻¹

Growth curves and the harvest forecast

RGR describes growth interval by interval; a growth curve describes the whole season with a few parameters that have a biological meaning. For projected leaf area, which saturates as the rosette closes, the logistic curve is a natural first model; the model-fitting lab lets you compare it with the exponential, Gompertz and other curves using AIC and cross-validation (Lesson 10.1).

Logistic growth curve and the time to reach a target(P4.2)
\[ A(t) = \frac{K}{1 + e^{-r\,(t - t_0)}}, \qquad t_{p} = t_0 + \frac{1}{r}\ln\frac{p}{1 - p} \]

the projected area at time t approaches the ceiling K along an S-shaped curve with initial relative growth rate r; the steepest growth is at the inflection time t₀, when the area is half of K. The time at which the area reaches a fraction p of K follows by solving the equation for t.

SymbolMeaningUnitExample (EC 1.6)
\(K\)asymptotic projected areacm²582
\(r\)relative growth rate while the plant is smalld⁻¹0.199
\(t_0\)inflection time (A = K/2, maximum absolute growth rate)d19.8
\(p\)fraction of K defining “ready”–0.95
Follow the derivation
  1. Set \(A(t_p) = pK\): \(1 + e^{-r(t_p - t_0)} = 1/p\), so \(e^{-r(t_p - t_0)} = (1-p)/p\).
  2. Take logarithms: \(-r(t_p - t_0) = \ln\frac{1-p}{p}\), i.e. \(t_p = t_0 + \frac1r\ln\frac{p}{1-p}\).
  3. For p = 0.95: \(\ln 19 = 2.944\), so \(t_{95} = t_0 + 2.944/r\).
  4. For small \(t\) the curve is exponential with RGR ≈ r, which links the parameters to Eq. P4.1.
Assumptions and validity

A symmetric S-curve with a fixed ceiling. Projected area saturates for geometric reasons (overlap, heading) even while mass keeps increasing, so K is not a “final plant size”. Fitting all photographs of the same plants treats them as independent points and makes the standard errors too small; fit per plant and average the parameters if you want to test them between treatments.

\(r(t - t_0)\): d⁻¹ × d — dimensionless ✓; \(t_p\): d + (1/d⁻¹) × dimensionless = d ✓
Worked example 2Forecasting the harvest from DAT 28

At DAT 28 your EC 1.6 plants average 485 cm² and the logistic fit to all data so far gives K = 580 cm², r = 0.20 d⁻¹ and t₀ = 19.8 d. The teacher asks when the plants will reach 95 % of K, and what fresh mass to expect, if the harvest calibration of an earlier group was FW = 0.122·A1.18 (the illustrative calibration of Lesson 10.3).

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Time to 95 %: \(t_{95} = 19.8 + \ln 19/0.20 = 19.8 + 14.7 = 34.5\) d → plan the harvest for DAT 35.
  2. Area then: 0.95 × 580 = 551 cm².
  3. Fresh mass: \(0.122\times551^{1.18} = 0.122\times e^{1.18\ln 551} = 0.122\times e^{7.449} = 0.122\times1718 = 210\) g.
  4. Check against the example harvest: the EC 1.6 plants weighed 205 ± 17 g (mean ± SD). The forecast is good — but it borrowed another group's calibration; the uncertainty of b dominates (a change of ±0.1 in b changes the forecast by a factor \(551^{\pm0.1} = e^{\pm0.63}\), i.e. by +88 % or −47 %!). Calibrate with your own destructive harvests.
Harvest at ≈ DAT 35; expected ≈ 210 g per plant (highly sensitive to the calibration exponent)
Calibrating area to mass needs a range of sizes

Fitting FW = a·Ab to the twelve harvested plants of the example gives b = 0.94 (95 % CI 0.81–1.07), well below the 1.18 used to simulate them. The harvest covers only a narrow range of sizes (370–614 cm²), and random error in the measured areas flattens the fitted slope (regression dilution). Two or three spare plants harvested at DAT 14 and 21 spread the range and fix the problem (Lesson 2.4, Eq. 2.4.10).

Comparing treatments

The confirmatory test is the one in your plan, applied to the primary response with one value per experimental unit. Which test? The design decides (Table 1; Lesson 3.3).

The randomised complete block model(P4.3)
\[ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad F = \frac{\text{MS}_{\text{treat}}}{\text{MS}_{\text{E}}} = \frac{\text{SS}_{\text{treat}}/(t-1)}{\text{SS}_{\text{E}}/\big((t-1)(b-1)\big)} \]

each observation is the grand mean plus the effect of its treatment plus the effect of its block plus random error. The F statistic compares the variation between treatment means with the error variation that remains after the block differences have been removed.

\(\varepsilon_{ij}\)error, assumed independent and normal with constant varianceg

Why blocking increases power

Without blocks, the block-to-block differences end up in the error term. With blocks they get their own sum of squares, the error mean square shrinks, and F grows — at the price of \(b - 1\) error degrees of freedom. Blocking pays when the blocks really differ, as they do along the light gradient under a single fixture (Lesson 3.1).

g² / g² — F is dimensionless ✓
Worked example 3What blocking did to the EC experiment

The harvest fresh masses (g) of the example, by block (B1–B4) for EC 0.8 / 1.6 / 2.4, were: B1 153.4 / 183.8 / 163.4; B2 168.2 / 225.4 / 204.4; B3 172.4 / 210.6 / 195.4; B4 144.4 / 201.4 / 209.9. Compare a one-way ANOVA with the planned block ANOVA, and report the result.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Means: EC 0.8: 159.6 g; EC 1.6: 205.3 g; EC 2.4: 193.3 g; grand mean 186.1 g. Block means: 166.9, 199.3, 192.8 and 185.2 g — the two central blocks under the fixture grew more.
  2. Sums of squares: SStreat = 4 × Σ(mean − 186.1)² = 4489.5; SSblock = 3 × Σ(block mean − 186.1)² = 1772.0; SStotal = 7203.5; SSE = 7203.5 − 4489.5 − 1772.0 = 942.0.
  3. One-way ANOVA (blocks ignored, so the error is 1772.0 + 942.0 = 2714.0 with 9 df): MSE = 301.6, F(2, 9) = 2244.8/301.6 = 7.44, p = 0.012.
  4. Block ANOVA: MSE = 942.0/6 = 157.0; F(2, 6) = 2244.8/157.0 = 14.30, p = 0.005; blocks F(3, 6) = 3.76, p = 0.079. Partial η² = 4489.5/(4489.5 + 942.0) = 0.83.
  5. Tukey's HSD after the block ANOVA (q0.95(3, 6) = 4.34, HSD = 4.34 × √(157.0/4) = 27.2 g): EC 1.6 − EC 0.8 = 45.7 g (95 % CI 18.5 to 72.9, p = 0.005); EC 2.4 − EC 0.8 = 33.7 g (6.5 to 60.9, p = 0.021); EC 2.4 − EC 1.6 = −12.0 g (−39.2 to 15.2, p = 0.42). Without blocks, EC 2.4 − EC 0.8 would not have been significant (p = 0.054).
  6. Report: “Shoot fresh mass at harvest differed between EC levels (randomised-block ANOVA, F(2, 6) = 14.3, p = 0.005, partial η² = 0.83). Plants at EC 1.6 and 2.4 mS cm⁻¹ were 46 g (95 % CI 18–73 g) and 34 g (7–61 g) heavier than at 0.8 mS cm⁻¹ (Tukey HSD); 1.6 and 2.4 did not differ detectably (−12 g, −39 to 15 g).”
Blocking halved the error mean square (301.6 → 157.0 g²) and turned a borderline comparison into a clear one

Always check the assumptions: plot the residuals against the fitted values (roughly constant spread?), look at a normal QQ plot, and compare the spreads of the groups — the Brown–Forsythe test on the example gives p = 0.91, no sign of unequal variances (Lesson 3.3). With four units per treatment such checks have little power; plan a transformation (e.g. ln FW) in advance if variances are expected to grow with the mean.

Resource-use indicators of your system

Your system is a small production system, and the indicators of Lesson 8.4 apply to it exactly as to a commercial farm: yield per litre, kilowatt-hours per kilogram, grams per mole of photons. Every indicator needs a numerator, a denominator and a boundary: here, the growing period from transplanting to harvest, the jars and the lamp (not the seedling stage, the room heating or the drying oven), and marketable shoot fresh mass as the product.

Water, energy and light indicators of a project system(P4.4)
\[ \text{WP} = \frac{Y_{\text{FW}}}{W}, \quad \text{PWU} = \frac{W}{Y_{\text{FW}}}, \quad e = \frac{E}{Y_{\text{FW}}}, \quad \text{DLI} = \frac{\overline{\text{PPFD}}\;t_{\text{photo}}\cdot3600}{10^{6}}, \quad \text{LUE}_{\text{DW}} = \frac{Y_{\text{DW}}}{A\;\text{DLI}\;n_{\text{d}}} \]

water productivity is the fresh yield per litre of water used; product water use is its inverse; the specific energy use is the electricity per kilogram of fresh yield; the daily light integral follows from the mean PPFD and the photoperiod; and the light-use efficiency is the dry yield divided by the photons that fell on the lit area during the growing days. These are Eqs. 8.4.2, 8.4.4 and 8.4.5 of Lesson 8.4 for a project system.

Choices you must state
  • Water: water that left the jars (weighing) or water supplied (fill + top-ups, including solution discarded at harvest)? In a Kratky jar the supply is the whole initial fill; about 2 L per jar were left over and discarded — counting it raises PWU by two thirds.
  • Energy: lamp only, or lamp + pump + propagation + HVAC share? Commercial numbers (7.7–12.5 kWh kg⁻¹ for vertical farms; Pennisi et al., 2025) include climate control.
  • Product: fresh or dry, shoots only, whole heads or trimmed? Converting with the dry-matter content changes rankings (Lesson 8.4, Worked example 3).

Worked example 4The balance sheet of twelve Kratky jars

From the example grow log: 12 plants harvested at DAT 35 with a total shoot fresh mass of 2233 g and dry mass 110.3 g; the jars lost 35.66 L of water; the energy meter read 53.29 kWh; the mean PPFD was 237.3 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ over a lit area of 0.6 m², 16 h per day. Calculate WP, PWU, e, EUE, DLI and LUE, and compare them with Lesson 8.4.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Water: WP = 2233 g / 35.66 L = 62.6 g L⁻¹; PWU = 35.66 L / 2.233 kg = 16.0 L kg⁻¹; per plant 2.97 L, i.e. 0.085 L per plant and day — within Kratky's 3–6 L per head (Kratky, 2009). Comparable with the median vertical farm (16 L kg⁻¹) and far better than field lettuce (WP 4–15 g L⁻¹; Lesson 8.4).
  2. Energy: e = 53.29 kWh / 2.233 kg = 23.9 kWh kg⁻¹; EUE = 1/23.9 = 0.042 kg kWh⁻¹. The meter agrees with 95 W × 16 h × 35 d = 53.2 kWh.
  3. Light: DLI = 237.3 × 16 × 3600/10⁶ = 13.7 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ (lettuce target 12–17 ✓). Photons on the bench: 13.67 × 35 × 0.6 = 287 mol.
  4. LUE: 110.3 g / 287 mol = 0.38 g DW mol⁻¹ (7.8 g FW mol⁻¹) — like a greenhouse (0.39), below the vertical-farm mean of 0.55 (Jin et al., 2023).
  5. Why is e two to three times a vertical farm's, although it excludes climate control? Two reasons you can quantify: (1) photons delivered to the bench per joule, 237.3 × 0.6 / 95 = 1.5 µmol J⁻¹, compared with 2.5–3.5 µmol J⁻¹ for modern fixtures — much light misses the bench; (2) for the first two weeks the small plants intercept only a fraction of the light on the bench. Both are design problems, not biology: reflective walls, a smaller lit area early (moving plants together) or denser planting would cut e (Eq. 8.4.6).
WP 62.6 g L⁻¹ (PWU 16.0 L kg⁻¹) · e 23.9 kWh kg⁻¹ · DLI 13.7 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ · LUE 0.38 g DW mol⁻¹

With the Swedish generation mix (about 35 g CO₂e kWh⁻¹; Ember, 2026, as used in Lesson 8.4) the electricity of the example adds about 0.8 kg CO₂e per kilogram of lettuce; with a coal-heavy grid of several hundred grams per kWh it would be ten times more. The same kilowatt-hour can be a small or a dominant part of a footprint — which is why life-cycle assessments of indoor farming depend so strongly on the grid (Lesson 15.1).

Uncertainty: how sure are your numbers?

Every indicator inherits the uncertainty of its inputs. Two kinds matter: measurement uncertainty (meter accuracy, sensor calibration, reading error) and biological variability (plants differ, and twelve plants estimate the mean of the system only approximately). For quotients and products of independent inputs, relative uncertainties add in quadrature (Lesson 9.3; JCGM, 2008).

Relative standard uncertainty of a quotient or product(P4.5)
\[ q = \frac{x_1\,x_2\cdots}{y_1\,y_2\cdots} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{u(q)}{q} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{u(x_1)}{x_1}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{u(x_2)}{x_2}\right)^2 + \cdots + \left(\frac{u(y_1)}{y_1}\right)^2 + \cdots} \]

when an indicator is a product or a quotient of independent inputs, its relative uncertainty is the square root of the sum of the squared relative uncertainties of the inputs.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(q\)indicator (e, WP, LUE …)as defined
\(u(x)/x\)relative standard uncertainty of input x (standard deviation of its estimate ÷ its value)–
Example: how uncertain is 23.9 kWh kg⁻¹?

Assume an energy meter accurate to about 1 % (check your meter's specification). For the yield, the balance error (0.1 g on 2233 g) is negligible, but the mean fresh mass of 12 plants carries a biological standard error of 7.4 g on 186.1 g (4.0 %). Then \(u(e)/e = \sqrt{0.01^2 + 0.040^2} = 0.041\): e = 23.9 ± 1.0 kWh kg⁻¹ (standard uncertainty) — report “24 ± 1 kWh kg⁻¹”. For LUE the quantum sensor's calibration (≈ 5 %) and the sampling of the PPFD map dominate.

every term is a ratio of quantities with the same unit — dimensionless ✓

For the treatment comparison, the uncertainty is expressed by confidence intervals of the means and of their differences — which is why the Results report differences with 95 % CIs, not only p-values. And for the whole study, the minimum detectable difference tells the reader what a non-significant result can exclude (Lesson 3.1).

What to report

  • The design: treatments, experimental unit, blocks, randomisation, n per treatment, excluded plants and why.
  • A table of descriptive statistics (n, mean, SD) of every response per treatment, and a figure that shows every plant.
  • Growth: RGR per interval (with the size variable and the dates), the fitted curve with parameters, R², RMSE and the model comparison.
  • The planned test with its statistic, degrees of freedom and exact p-value, the effect size and 95 % confidence intervals of the differences.
  • Each resource indicator with its unit, boundary, product basis (FW/DW), period and uncertainty, compared with published values.
  • Assumption checks, deviations from the plan, and which analyses were exploratory.
  • Data availability: the grow-log CSV/JSON and the analysis files in an appendix or repository.

The report guide shows where each item goes; Lesson 3.4 shows how to write statistics so that readers can check them.
