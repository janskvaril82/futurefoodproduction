Build your soilless system

A good experiment starts with a system that works for 35 days without surprises, is cheap enough to build in several identical copies, and lets you measure what goes in and what comes out. This guide gives you three proven designs — Kratky, deep-water culture and a mini nutrient-film system — with materials, costs and construction drawings, and then everything you need before day 0: seedlings, light, nutrient solution, a fair experimental design and a safe set-up.

What you will be able to do

  • Choose, size and build a Kratky, DWC or NFT system and explain how each supplies water, nutrients and oxygen to the roots (LO2, LO3).
  • Raise uniform seedlings in stone wool and set the PPFD and photoperiod for a target daily light integral (LO2, LO3).
  • Prepare a nutrient solution to a target EC and pH from a two-part concentrate (LO2, LO3).
  • Turn a treatment idea into a replicated, randomised and blocked design with the correct experimental unit (LO4).
  • Carry out a risk assessment for electricity, light, chemicals, hygiene and food safety (LO4).

Choose your system

All three designs in this guide are closed soilless systems in which the roots grow in a nutrient solution; they differ in how oxygen reaches the roots and in what happens when something fails (Lesson 5.1). In a Kratky system the solution level falls as the plant drinks, and roots in the growing air gap supply themselves with oxygen — no pump at all (Kratky, 2009). In deep-water culture (DWC) the roots hang in a deep, aerated tank. In the nutrient film technique (NFT) a thin film of solution flows past the roots along a sloping channel (Graves, 1983).

Recommended default

For most groups, twelve Kratky jars with one plant each is the best experimental system: each jar is an independent experimental unit, nothing can break, water use per plant is measured simply by weighing the jar, and you can test EC, recipe, substrate or cultivar in a proper randomised block design. Choose DWC if your question is about oxygen or root temperature, and NFT only if you want to study flow and recirculation — and have a failure plan.

Build A: a Kratky system from food-grade containers

The Kratky method was developed at the University of Hawaii for leaf and semi-head lettuce: a covered, light-proof container is filled once with nutrient solution, the seedling sits in a net pot whose base dips into the solution, and no solution is added afterwards (Kratky, 1993, 2009). As the plant transpires, the level falls and a humid air space opens in which roots specialised for aeration grow. The single rule that makes it work: the level may stay the same or fall, but must never be raised, because refilling drowns the oxygen roots (Kratky, 2009).

Variant: a Kratky tote for six plants

A 55 × 35 × 20 cm storage tote with six holes in the lid holds about 36 L, enough for six lettuces (Lesson 5.1, Worked example 4). It is cheaper per plant, but the six plants share one solution: if the solution is your treatment, the tote is the experimental unit and you need several totes per treatment.

Build B: deep-water culture with an air pump

In DWC the plants float or hang above a deep volume of solution that is aerated with an aquarium air pump and an air stone. The large volume buffers temperature, EC and pH, and — as long as the pump runs — the roots never lack oxygen. When it stops, the roots' oxygen consumption can pull the dissolved oxygen from near saturation below 4 mg L⁻¹ within a few hours (Lesson 5.3, Worked example 3).

Build C: a mini NFT system

An NFT system pumps solution from a reservoir to the upper end of gently sloping channels; a film only a few millimetres deep flows past the roots and back to the reservoir. Roots get water, nutrients and oxygen at the same time, and the small volume makes the system light and cheap to heat or cool — but the crop has almost no buffer if the pump stops (Graves, 1983). The hydraulics are the subject of Lesson 5.4: design for about 1–2 L min⁻¹ per channel and a slope between 1:30 and 1:100; 1:30–1:40 is common because a bench true enough for 1:100 is hard to build.

Step by step

  1. Set the slope. Level the stand across its width, then raise the upper end: for 1:40 over 1.2 m the drop is 3 cm. Sight along each channel for sags, which cause ponding.
  2. Cut the planting holes in the channel lids every 18–20 cm and fit the end caps; the lower cap drains into the return pipe.
  3. Plumb the pump to the manifold, with one valve per channel and a bypass back into the reservoir; keep the reservoir below the channels so that everything drains back if the pump stops.
  4. Set the flow to 1–2 L min⁻¹ per channel: time how long a 1 L jug takes to fill at each outlet. Check that the whole channel floor is wet and that nothing overflows.
  5. Test 24 h with water, then with solution. Make the failure plan: who checks the pump each day, and what happens if it fails over a weekend?

Seedlings in stone wool

Uniform seedlings are the cheapest way to reduce the variation between your plants — and therefore to increase statistical power. Sow about twice as many seeds as you need plants, and select the most uniform seedlings at transplanting with a written rule.

  1. Condition the cubes. Wear gloves; wet stone wool does not release dust. Soak the cubes in water adjusted to pH ≈ 5.5–6.0 until saturated, let them drain, and check the pH of the run-off — fresh stone wool can raise the pH of the water it sits in.
  2. Sow one or two seeds in the small hole of each cube, no deeper than a few millimetres. Cover the tray with a clear lid to keep the humidity high.
  3. Germinate at 18–22 °C. Lettuce seeds of many cultivars germinate better in the light: the classic experiments that discovered phytochrome used lettuce seed (Borthwick et al., 1952). Remove the cover when the cotyledons have opened.
  4. Thin and feed. Cut (do not pull) the weaker seedling so that one remains per cube. Water from below with a dilute nutrient solution; never let the cubes dry out.
  5. Select and transplant at 2–3 true leaves, when white root tips appear at the bottom of the cube. Write your selection rule before you look at the seedlings (e.g. “discard the smallest 20 % and any damaged seedling”), photograph all selected seedlings from above for the day-0 leaf area, then allocate them to units at random (below).
Common misconception

“We picked the nicest seedlings for the treatment we expect to win.” Even without intending it, choosing which plant goes where is the most common way to bias an experiment. Select seedlings for uniformity first, then let random numbers decide which seedling goes to which unit.

Lighting: set-up and PPFD target

Start from the crop's daily light integral (DLI) and your photoperiod, and convert them to the photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) you need at canopy height (Lesson 4.2). Then check, by measuring, that the fixture delivers it evenly.

PPFD target from DLI, and what a fixture can deliver(P1.3)
\[ \text{PPFD}_{\text{target}} = \frac{\text{DLI}\times10^{6}}{3600\,t_{\text{photo}}}, \qquad \overline{\text{PPFD}} \approx \frac{f_{\text{c}}\;\eta_{\text{ph}}\;P_{\text{el}}}{A} \]

the PPFD needed is the daily light integral in moles per square metre per day multiplied by a million and divided by the number of seconds of light per day. The mean PPFD a fixture can deliver on a bench is its electrical power times its photon efficacy, times the fraction of its photons that land on the bench, divided by the bench area.

  2. A fixture emits PPF = \(\eta_{\text{ph}}P_{\text{el}}\) µmol s⁻¹. A fraction \(f_{\text{c}}\) lands on the bench of area \(A\); the mean flux density there is \(f_{\text{c}}\eta_{\text{ph}}P_{\text{el}}/A\).
  3. Example: 95 W × 2.5 µmol J⁻¹ = 238 µmol s⁻¹; with \(f_{\text{c}}\) = 0.6 on 0.6 m², the mean PPFD is ≈ 238 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ — exactly the target for DLI 13.7 at 16 h.

Map the PPFD — and repeat the map

  • Measure, don't trust the label. Use a quantum (PAR) sensor, levelled, at the height of the canopy — the top of the plugs on day 0, the top of the plants later. Switch off the room lights.
  • Grid. 9–12 points on the growing area, the same points every time; photograph the set-up. Report the mean, minimum, maximum and uniformity (min/mean).
  • Height and timer. Fix the lamp height (or keep the distance to the canopy constant) and set a mechanical or digital timer; check after a power cut that the timer still runs on time.
  • Surroundings. White or reflective walls around the bench raise the edge PPFD; curtains between compartments stop light treatments from contaminating each other.
  • Eyes. Never look into the fixture. Switch it off or dim it when you work close to it (safety).

Nutrient solution preparation

The simplest reliable approach for the project is a commercial two-part concentrate for leafy crops: part A typically contains calcium nitrate (often with potassium nitrate and the iron chelate), part B the phosphates, sulfates and most micronutrients. They are kept apart because calcium precipitates with sulfate and phosphate at stock concentrations (Lesson 5.2). In laboratory session 4 you can instead make your own A and B stocks from single salts with the nutrient mixer. For lettuce, the lessons use a start EC of about 1.2–1.6 mS cm⁻¹: the Cornell raft system runs at 1.2 (Brechner & Both, 2013) and Kratky (2009) starts at about 1.5.

How much concentrate? Dosing to a target EC(P1.4)
\[ v = V\;\frac{\text{EC}_{\text{t}} - \text{EC}_{\text{w}}}{k}, \qquad k = \frac{\Delta\text{EC}_{\text{test}}}{v_{\text{test}}/V_{\text{test}}} \]

the volume of each concentrate to add equals the volume of solution to be made, times the EC you still need to add (target minus the EC of the water), divided by the EC increase caused by one millilitre of concentrate per litre — a slope you measure once in a small test batch.

Assumptions and validity

EC rises almost linearly with the amount of fertiliser at the low concentrations of nutrient solutions (Lesson 5.2), so one test batch calibrates \(k\). Measure temperature-compensated EC (EC₂₅). Always add part A and part B in equal volumes, each to the diluting water — never to each other.

L × (mS cm⁻¹) ÷ (mS cm⁻¹ per mL L⁻¹) = L × mL L⁻¹ = mL ✓
  1. Measure the water. EC and pH of the tap water (or RO water). In a Kratky jar, unused background salts concentrate as the water is used — above about 0.5 mS cm⁻¹ use rain or RO water (Lesson 5.2).
  2. Calibrate first. pH meter with buffers 4.01 and 7.00, EC meter against a 1.413 mS cm⁻¹ standard (monitoring protocol).
  3. Mix. Fill the bucket with about 90 % of the water. Add part A with a syringe, stir; add part B, stir. Top up to the final volume, stir, measure EC and add more of both in equal steps if needed.
  4. Adjust pH last to 5.8 (target window 5.5–6.5) with pH-down, a few drops at a time, stirring and waiting a minute between additions.
  5. Record the batch: date, volume, mL of A and B, final EC and pH, water source — in the lab notebook and the grow log notes. For EC treatments, prepare each level as its own batch and label every jar.

Frontier — far-red and dynamic lighting

Adding far-red photons (700–750 nm) to a red–blue or white spectrum often increases lettuce leaf expansion and yield by improving light capture (Jin et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2019), and research farms are testing dynamic spectra and photoperiods that follow electricity prices. A far-red treatment is a realistic student project if you have two identical fixtures, one with a far-red channel — design it as the lighting compartments of Lesson 3.1, Section 3.1.10.

Experimental design: replication, randomisation, blocking

Three principles turn a growing set-up into an experiment that can answer a question (Lesson 3.1; Hurlbert, 1984):

  1. Replication. Apply each treatment to several independent experimental units. Plants that share a tank or a lamp are not independent of each other with respect to that tank or lamp; counting them as replicates is pseudo-replication. Four to six units per treatment is a practical minimum; a power analysis tells you what you can detect.
  2. Randomisation. Let random numbers decide which unit gets which treatment and where it stands. It protects you against gradients and biases you do not know about.
  3. Blocking. Group units that are alike — the same zone of the PPFD map, the same shelf, the same sowing batch — and put every treatment once in every block. The block-to-block differences are then removed from the error term by a randomised complete block ANOVA.

Worked example 1How many jars — and what can twelve jars detect?

Your group plans three EC levels in Kratky jars. From earlier courses you expect the shoot fresh mass of lettuce at harvest to vary with a standard deviation of about 17 g between jars at a mean of about 190 g (a coefficient of variation of 9 %). (a) How many jars per treatment are needed to detect a difference of 30 g between two treatments with 80 % power (α = 0.05, two-sided)? (b) What power does a design with four jars per treatment have for that difference? (c) How does blocking change the picture?

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Standardised effect size (Eq. 3.1.7): \(d = \Delta/\sigma = 30/17 = 1.76\).
  2. (a) Sample size. The noncentral-t calculation (the statistics workbench, power mode, or sampleSizeTwoSample in the course library) gives 7 jars per treatment for 80 % power — 21 jars for three treatments.
  3. (b) Power with 4 jars: ≈ 0.55 for d = 1.76: little better than a coin flip. With 6 jars: ≈ 0.78.
  4. (c) Blocking. If a quarter or more of the between-jar variation is due to position under the lamp, a block design removes it: in the illustrative dataset of this course the error standard deviation falls from 17.4 g (one-way ANOVA) to 12.5 g (block ANOVA). With σ = 12.5 g, d = 2.4 and four jars per treatment already give a power of about 0.8.
  5. Decision. Twelve jars in four blocks is a sensible minimum; eighteen or twenty-four jars (six or eight blocks) if space allows. Whatever you choose, state the minimum detectable difference in your plan.
≈ 7 jars per treatment without blocking; 4 per treatment in 4 blocks gives ≈ 80 % power only if blocking reduces σ to ≈ 12–13 g

Checklist for the experimental plan (D2) — the teacher approves the plan before you sow:

  • Our research question and hypotheses (H₀ and H₁) are written down, with one primary response variable (e.g., shoot fresh mass at DAT 35).
  • We have named the experimental unit, and every treatment level is applied to at least four independent units.
  • Our layout is randomised within blocks; we recorded the seed and kept the randomisation file.
  • We have a power estimate or a minimum detectable difference, and an exclusion rule for plants that die.
  • We have a measurement plan (what, how, how often, who — P2) and a risk assessment (below).

Electrical and water safety, hygiene and food safety

Most accidents in student growing projects are predictable: water meets mains electricity, a concentrate splashes, a lamp is stared into, a batch of solution is mixed wrongly. A written risk assessment turns each hazard into a control that the whole group knows.

Electricity and water

  • Every socket strip is protected by a 30 mA residual-current device (RCD, jordfelsbrytare) — test it with its test button at the start of the project.
  • All mains-powered equipment (drivers, pumps, timers, socket strips) sits above the water level and out of reach of spills; cables hang in drip loops. Prefer low-voltage (12/24 V) pumps.
  • Never handle plugs or equipment with wet hands; switch off before you put your hands in a tank with a pump. Never modify mains wiring or repair a fixture yourself.

Light

  • Horticultural LEDs are far brighter than room lighting and their blue component can harm the retina; fixtures are classified by the photobiological safety standard IEC 62471 into risk groups (exempt, RG1, RG2, RG3). Check the label, never look into the fixture, and switch it off or dim it to work close to it (IEC, 2006; Lesson 3.1).
  • Keep free air around drivers and fixtures; nothing flammable on or above them.

Chemicals

  • Read the safety data sheets of the concentrates and the pH-down before use; wear nitrile gloves and eye protection when handling concentrates and acids.
  • Never mix concentrates A and B with each other; add each to water. Add acid to water, never water to acid. Label every bottle with contents, concentration and date; store acids below eye level in a tray.
  • Spent nutrient solution is a fertiliser: dilute it and water plants with it, or dispose of it as your lab instructs — never pour concentrates or acids down the drain.

Hygiene and food safety

  • Wash containers, net pots and tools with warm water and detergent and rinse well before use; wash hands before and after work on the plants, and do not move tools between units without rinsing them — root pathogens such as Pythium spread with water and tools (Mattson, 2018).
  • Use food-grade containers, drinking-quality water and fertilisers intended for food crops. Nothing that is not food-safe (laboratory chemicals, uncoated metal, glue, aquarium sealants of unknown grade) may touch the solution.
  • Stone wool: handle it wet, wear gloves; avoid dust.
  • If you want to eat the harvest, do so only after all measurements, and only if the whole chain was food-safe; wash the leaves. Plants treated with anything other than food-grade inputs are not eaten. Your own produce may be served in the sensory study only with the teacher's approval (P5).

Commissioning checklist (D3)

Before day 0 — and before you put a single seedling in — go through the list together and photograph the set-up. Tick each item (your ticks are saved in this browser).

  • All units are labelled with permanent IDs on lid and body; the randomised layout is printed and taped to the bench.
  • 24-hour leak test passed; no light enters the root zone.
  • RCD tested; all mains equipment above the water level; drip loops in place.
  • Pump and air stones work (DWC/NFT); the failure plan and the care rota are written down.
  • Timer set and checked; lamp height fixed; PPFD map at canopy height recorded (mean, min, max, uniformity).
  • pH and EC meters calibrated today; calibration values in the lab notebook.
  • Nutrient solution made up, EC and pH recorded per unit; initial volume (or mass) of each unit recorded.
  • Energy meter installed on the lamp (and pump) circuit and its start reading recorded.
  • Grow log set up with the start date and the plant list; day-0 photographs taken with the scale marker.
