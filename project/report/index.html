Report, presentation and assessment

Eight weeks of work become a scientific argument: a report that a reader can check, a poster that a visitor understands in three minutes, and a review that helps another group to improve. This page gives you the templates, the checklists and the rubric that is used to assess the project — so that you know from week 1 what an excellent project looks like.

What you will be able to do

  • Write an IMRaD report on a growing experiment and a sensory study with reproducible methods and correctly reported statistics (LO4).
  • Design figures and tables that show the data and their uncertainty honestly (LO4, LO5).
  • Present the project on a poster or in a short talk and defend it in discussion (LO4, LO6, LO7).
  • Review another group's report constructively against explicit criteria (LO4).
  • Use sources and generative AI transparently and act according to the rules of research integrity (LO4).

What you hand in

In week 10 each group submits the report (D9), presents the poster or a talk (D10) and reviews another group's draft (D11); each student submits an individual contribution statement (D12). The data package (D8) — grow log, system log, anonymised sensory data and analysis files — is an appendix of the report (overview of deliverables).

Start writing in week 4

Write the Methods while you do them (weeks 3–5), draft figures as soon as data arrive (weeks 5–8), and keep the last two weeks for Results, Discussion and revision. A report written in the final week reads like one.

The report: an IMRaD template

The report has the structure of a scientific article: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion, each answering one question for the reader (Lesson 3.4). Because the project has two studies, Methods and Results each have a growing part and a sensory part, and the Discussion brings them together. Open each section for guidance; the word counts are indicative.

Paste into your word processor or a shared document.
Title, authors, abstract ≤ 250 words

An informative title states the finding or the question with the key variables: “Nutrient-solution EC of 1.6–2.4 mS cm⁻¹ increased lettuce yield in Kratky jars by a quarter, at 24 kWh per kilogram” rather than “Hydroponics project”. The abstract is a structured summary in one paragraph: background (one sentence), aim, methods (system, design, n, measurements, panel), results with numbers (effect sizes, CIs, p-values, key indicators), and the conclusion.

Typical mistakes

No numbers in the abstract; results that are not in the report; a title that could fit any group.

1 Introduction ≈ 600–800 words

Move from the general to the specific in three steps: context (why resource-efficient, acceptable food production matters — planetary boundaries, CEA, novel foods; Modules 1, 12–14), gap (what is not known for your factor, crop or product), and aim with the research questions and hypotheses of both studies, written as testable statements (H₀/H₁). Cite a handful of good sources you have read.

Typical mistakes

A textbook chapter on hydroponics without a gap; hypotheses that appear first in the Discussion.

2 Methods — growing experiment ≈ 900–1 200 words

Write for reproducibility: another group must be able to repeat the experiment from your text (Lesson 3.4).

  • System and conditions: containers and volumes, substrate, fixture (power at the plug, PPFD map with mean, range and uniformity), photoperiod, DLI, air temperature and humidity, solution recipe, EC and pH targets and how they were managed.
  • Plant material: species, cultivar, seed source, sowing and transplanting dates, selection rule for seedlings.
  • Design: factor and levels, experimental unit, number of units, blocks, randomisation method and seed, power or MDD.
  • Measurements: what, how, how often, instruments and calibration (refer to the calibration log in the appendix); image analysis; harvest and drying protocol.
  • Balances: boundary, period and product basis of the water, energy and light indicators.
  • Statistics: the primary response and planned test, software/tools, α, assumption checks, exploratory analyses.
Typical mistakes

Methods written as a diary; missing units and instrument details; the analysis described only in the Results.

2 Methods — sensory study ≈ 500–700 words

Samples (products, suppliers, legal status under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, preparation, portion, temperature, coding), panel (n, recruitment, age range, exclusion criteria, neophobia scale), procedure (method, exact questions, blind/informed, booth and light, serving design, palate cleansing), ethics (information, consent, allergens, data handling, teacher approval) and statistics (Lesson 13.4, Table 6).

3 Results ≈ 1 200–1 500 words

Report what you found, in the order of the questions, without interpretation. Every figure and table is cited in the text and has a self-contained caption.

  • Conditions achieved: EC, pH, temperature, DO and PPFD per treatment (a table of means and ranges), deviations and events.
  • Growth: growth curves of every plant and treatment means; RGR per interval; fitted curve parameters; harvest forecast vs actual harvest.
  • Treatment comparison: descriptive statistics, the planned test, effect sizes with CIs.
  • Resource use: water, energy and light indicators with uncertainty.
  • Sensory study: panel description, discrimination result (x/n, p, pd, d′), liking (means with CIs, block ANOVA, Tukey), neophobia relations.
Typical mistakes

Tables that repeat the figures; results without n; interpretation mixed into the Results.

4 Discussion and conclusions ≈ 1 200–1 500 words

Answer the questions in the first paragraph; then explain the results with mechanisms (e.g. why EC drifted in Kratky jars; why LUE was low early), compare them quantitatively with published values on the same basis, discuss the limitations and what they mean for the conclusions (power, pseudo-replication risks, convenience panel, simulated vs measured), and place the project in the course's big questions: sustainability and planetary boundaries, resilience and accessibility of the technology (LO1, LO7), and acceptance and regulation of novel foods (LO6). End with concrete conclusions and recommendations for the next group.

Typical mistakes

“Further research is needed” without saying which; comparing kWh kg⁻¹ with numbers that include HVAC without saying so; generalising from a student panel to “consumers”.

Back matter references · data · AI · contributions · appendices

References in APA 7, only sources you have read. Data availability: where the grow log, system log and anonymised sensory data are. Use of generative AI: tool, version, dates, what for — and what not (below). Author contributions (CRediT). Appendices: experimental plan (preregistration) and deviations, calibration log, data dictionary, ballots and consent text, additional figures.

Figures and tables

A good figure shows the data, not only their summary: individual plants or panellists, with means and uncertainty on top (Weissgerber et al., 2015; Lesson 3.2). Four figures carry most project reports: growth curves (every plant + treatment means), a dot plot of the harvest with means and 95 % CIs, a comparison of your indicators with published systems, and the sensory results (means with CIs, or the discrimination result against the binomial distribution) — all of which the grow log, the analysis guide and the sensory designer produce.

  • Every figure and table is numbered, cited in the text and has a caption that explains it without the text (what, n, what the error bars are).
  • Axes have quantity names and units (Shoot fresh mass (g); Days after transplanting (d)); ticks and labels are readable at the printed size.
  • Error bars are defined (SD, SE or 95 % CI) and the choice fits the message; individual data are visible where n is small.
  • Colours are consistent across figures (one colour per treatment) and distinguishable for colour-blind readers; no 3D effects.
  • Tables have units in the column headings, consistent decimals, and numbers aligned on the decimal point.
  • No figure repeats a table; every figure makes one point that the text states.

Reporting statistics

Report statistics so that readers can check them: the test, the statistic with its degrees of freedom, the exact p-value, the effect size with a confidence interval, and the descriptive statistics behind them (Lesson 3.4; American Psychological Association, 2020).

Table 2. Write this, not that.
Instead ofWrite
“EC had a significant effect (p < 0.05).”“Shoot fresh mass differed between EC levels (randomised-block ANOVA, F(2, 6) = 14.3, p = 0.005, partial η² = 0.83).”
“EC 1.6 was better than EC 0.8.”“Plants at 1.6 mS cm⁻¹ were 46 g heavier than at 0.8 mS cm⁻¹ (Tukey HSD, 95 % CI 18–73 g, p = 0.005).”
“There was no difference between 1.6 and 2.4.”“1.6 and 2.4 mS cm⁻¹ did not differ detectably (−12 g, 95 % CI −39 to 15 g): the data are compatible with anything from a 39 g loss to a 15 g gain.”
“p = 0.000”“p < 0.001”
“The mean was 186.0583 g.”“The mean was 186 g (SD 26 g, n = 12).”
“The panel could not taste the difference.”“13 of 24 panellists identified the odd sample (p = 0.028; pd = 0.31, 95 % CI 0.01–0.61).”
  • The unit of analysis is the experimental unit (one value per jar or panellist); repeated measurements are summarised per unit.
  • The planned (confirmatory) analyses are reported first and labelled; exploratory analyses are labelled as such.
  • Means are given with SD (or SE, stated) and n; differences with 95 % CIs; exact p-values to two significant digits.
  • Assumption checks are mentioned (residuals, variances) and the software or tool is named.
  • Non-significant results are reported with what the study could detect (MDD or power).

The poster

A poster is a conversation starter, not a report on a wall: a visitor should grasp the question, the design and the main answer in three minutes from two metres away. Use about 800 words, large figures, and one clear take-home message.

Oral presentation

If your course uses talks instead of posters: 8 minutes plus 4 minutes of questions, about one slide per minute. A proven structure:

  1. Hook and question (1 slide): why this matters — one number or image — and the question.
  2. What we did (2 slides): the system and the design as pictures, not bullet points.
  3. What we found (3 slides): one message per slide, stated in the slide title (“EC 1.6 and 2.4 gave 20–30 % more lettuce than 0.8”), the evidence below it.
  4. What it means (1 slide): answer, limitations, the link to sustainability or acceptance.
  5. Take-home (1 slide): one sentence.

Rehearse against the clock; everyone presents a part; everyone must be able to answer questions on any part of the project. Show uncertainty on the slides as you would in the report.

Peer review

Each group reviews another group's draft with the form below. A good review is specific, constructive and respectful: it identifies the strongest parts, the main problems (in order of importance) and concrete suggestions, and it distinguishes errors from matters of taste. Do not upload the other group's report to generative-AI tools — it is confidential, and the European guidelines ask researchers not to use generative AI substantially in peer review (European Commission, 2026).

Assessment rubric

The rubric describes what the project demonstrates at each grade level for ten criteria, each mapped to the learning outcomes (Table 1 of the overview). It is shown on the U/3/4/5 scale common in Swedish engineering programmes; if your syllabus uses another scale (e.g. A–F), the teacher will translate it. The formally adopted course syllabus decides the grading rules. A suggested rule: all criteria must reach at least 3 to pass; grade 4 requires at least six criteria at 4 or higher, including R1, R4 and R5; grade 5 requires at least six criteria at 5, including R1, R4 and R5, and none below 4. Individual grades take the contribution statements, the group contract and the discussion at the poster session into account.

Academic integrity and use of generative AI

The report is an examination. Plagiarism, fabricated or falsified data and undeclared help are handled under Chapter 10 of the Higher Education Ordinance (Högskoleförordningen, 1993:100); a university's disciplinary board can issue a warning or suspend a student for up to six months (Lesson 3.4). In 2025 unauthorised use of AI tools became the largest category of disciplinary cases at Swedish universities (UKÄ, 2026). The rules for the project follow the European Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research (European Commission, 2026), the European code of conduct for research integrity (ALLEA, 2023) and the Swedish Research Council's Good research practice (Swedish Research Council, 2025); your course's own rules always come first.

Three things that always fail a project report

Invented or “smoothed” data; text, figures or code copied without credit; references you have not read (including AI-invented ones). Honest reporting of a failed crop never does.
