Study guide

How to study Future Food Production

This course asks you to understand plants, photons, nutrients, microbes, data and law well enough to calculate with them and to judge new technologies. That is a lot for ten weeks. This guide shows how the course is built, how to spread the work over the weeks, which study habits research shows to work, how to get the most out of the laboratories, and where to refresh the mathematics you need.

The shape of the course

Future Food Production is a first-cycle course of 7.5 ECTS credits, which corresponds to about 200 hours of student work — roughly 20 hours a week over ten weeks at half-time study. Everything you need is on this site: modules grouping the lessons, the laboratories (interactive models of real systems), the practice generator, and the course project, in which your group grows a crop in a soilless system, measures it and runs a sensory study. Each important idea is met four times, in four different forms (Figure 1).

Key idea — plan the whole course backwards

The harvest (week 8) and the sensory study (week 9) cannot be moved once your plants are growing. Look at Figure 2 in week 1, then use the light weeks 3–4 to read ahead in Modules 10–12, so that the heavy weeks are for review and project work rather than first reading.

A typical week

Twenty hours a week is easier to manage as a routine than as a heroic effort before each deadline. Figure 3 shows one rhythm that works for many students; move the blocks to fit your timetable, but keep the principle — reading before the seminar, short practice sessions spread over the week, and project time every few days.

Learning strategies that work

Many popular study habits feel productive but do little for long-term learning. Dunlosky et al. (2013) reviewed the evidence for ten common techniques and rated their usefulness across students, materials and tests (Table 3). Two were rated of high utility, three of moderate utility and five of low utility. The course is designed around the first five; this section shows how to use them on purpose.

Active engagement matters in the classroom too: in a meta-analysis of 225 studies, average examination scores were about 6 % higher in active-learning sections of science, engineering and mathematics courses, and students in traditional lectures were 1.5 times more likely to fail (Freeman et al., 2014). The seminars and laboratory sessions of this course are designed for active work — come prepared.

Retrieval practice: test yourself to learn

Retrieving something from memory changes the memory: it becomes easier to retrieve again. Students who took recall tests on prose passages remembered more one week later than students who spent the same time restudying, even though restudying looked better five minutes after learning (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Retrieval practice also produced more learning than building concept maps with the text available (Karpicke & Blunt, 2011), and a meta-analysis of practice testing confirms the benefit across many settings (Adesope et al., 2017).

  • Take each lesson quiz before you feel ready; wrong first attempts are part of learning. Only the first attempt is scored, but you can retry.
  • Close the lesson and write down, from memory, the key equations of a section and what each symbol means. Then check against the equation box.
  • Use the practice generator in Smart review mode for ten minutes at the start of each study session.

Spacing: little and often

Memories fade, quickly at first and then more slowly — the forgetting curve that Ebbinghaus measured on himself in the 1880s and that Murre and Dros (2015) replicated. Each well-timed review slows the forgetting further, which is why the same total study time spread over several days beats one long session (Cepeda et al., 2006). Figure 4 shows the idea with a deliberately simple model.

Interleaving: mix problem types

Practising one type of problem at a time (“blocked” practice) feels smooth, but in the exam you must first decide which method a problem needs. Mixing problem types forces that decision. Students whose mathematics practice was shuffled did worse during practice but much better on a test a week later (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007), and learners who saw paintings by different artists interleaved learned the artists’ styles better, although most of them believed blocking had helped them more (Kornell & Bjork, 2008). Such “desirable difficulties” make learning feel slower while making it last (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). In the practice generator, select several modules at once.

Worked examples, then faded examples

For a beginner, studying a fully worked solution is more efficient than struggling alone with an equivalent problem (Sweller & Cooper, 1985). Every lesson therefore contains worked examples with the reasoning hidden behind a “Reveal” button. Use them in three steps: attempt the problem on paper, compare your approach with the solution step by step, then solve the next problem in the practice generator without help. As you gain skill, rely less on examples and more on independent problems.

Self-explanation: say why each step is true

Students who were prompted to explain a biology text to themselves, sentence by sentence, understood it better than students who read it twice (Chi et al., 1994). Each equation box helps you do this: it reads the equation aloud, lists every symbol with its unit and shows a unit check. Cover the “read aloud” line and try to produce it yourself; write your explanation in the notebook (press N).

Common misconception

“I have read it twice and it all makes sense, so I know it.” Familiarity is not recall. Rereading and highlighting create an illusion of fluency; a quiz or a blank sheet of paper tells you what you can actually do.

Using the laboratories

The laboratories are working models of real systems: a grow room with LED fixtures, a nutrient-film channel, an aquaponic loop, a bioreactor, a sensory booth. Each has the same five tabs, and they form a cycle (Figure 5). The cycle follows the predict–observe–explain strategy used in science teaching (White & Gunstone, 1992): committing to a prediction before you look makes you notice when the model surprises you — and surprise is where learning happens.

  • Predict before you move a slider. Write your prediction in the notebook (“if I double the mounting height, PPFD will fall to about a quarter”), then test it.
  • Use the presets to jump to realistic scenarios, and compare two of them rather than exploring at random.
  • Save investigations. The “Save investigation” button stores the settings, the results and a link in your notebook. Laboratory assignments are handed in this way.
  • Share a state. Every setting is stored in the address bar, so a link reproduces exactly what you see — useful for group work and questions to your teacher.
  • Mark the laboratory completed when you have done the investigation tasks; your progress page keeps track.
  • 3D stages respond to the mouse and to the keyboard: click the stage, then use the arrow keys to orbit and +/− to zoom. The toolbar resets the view, takes a screenshot or goes full screen. Everything in a 3D scene is also available as numbers, charts and text.

Reading scientific papers

Every lesson ends with required and recommended readings, and the frontiers radar links to the primary sources behind recent milestones. Research papers are not written to be read from start to finish. Keshav (2007) suggests reading in three passes, each with a different goal:

  1. First pass (5–10 minutes): title, abstract and introduction; the section and subsection headings; the conclusions; a glance at the references. Afterwards you should be able to answer the “five Cs”: category (what kind of paper: experiment, model, review, LCA?), context (which other work does it build on?), correctness (do the assumptions look valid?), contributions (what is new?) and clarity (is it well written?).
  2. Second pass (up to an hour): read with care but skip proofs and detailed methods; study every figure and table — axes, units, error bars, sample sizes — and mark references you might read.
  3. Third pass (several hours): reconstruct the work in your head, as if you were doing it yourself, and look for hidden assumptions. You will rarely need this pass in a first-cycle course, except for a paper at the centre of your project.

Worked example 1Reading a headline claim

A news article states: “Vertical farms use 95 % less water than field farming.” Using the questions in Table 5, decide what you need to know before you repeat the claim in your report.

Reveal the reasoning
  1. Find the primary source. Is the number from a measured farm, a model, or a company brochure? Follow the link to the paper or report.
  2. Check the functional unit. Water per kilogram of lettuce? Fresh or dry weight? A per-kilogram figure says nothing about water per unit of protein or energy.
  3. Check the system boundary. Does it include the water used to generate the farm’s electricity, and the water in building materials?
  4. Check the comparison. Field lettuce in a dry climate with irrigation, or rain-fed? The reference decides the percentage.
  5. Check the date and the source of funding. Is it a peer-reviewed study or an industry claim?
Report it as “a lettuce farm in … used … L per kg (source, year), compared with … for …” — never as a general truth.

Mathematics refresher

The course uses upper-secondary mathematics — but uses it constantly. The five tools below appear in almost every module. Each has an equation box and a live calculator: change the values and watch the result and the graph respond. The equation reference book collects every equation of the course.

Exponentials and logarithms

Whenever a quantity grows or decays in proportion to itself — microbes in a fermenter, a young crop, the light passing through a canopy — the exponential function appears. Its inverse, the logarithm, turns multiplication into addition and makes very large and very small numbers manageable.

Exponential growth and doubling time(SG.1)
\[ N(t) = N_0\,e^{r t}, \qquad t_{\text{d}} = \frac{\ln 2}{r} \]

the quantity at time t equals the initial quantity times e raised to the growth rate times time; it doubles every ln 2 divided by the growth rate.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(N(t),\ N_0\)amount at time t and at the start (e.g. biomass concentration)g L⁻¹
\(r\)specific growth rateh⁻¹
\(t,\ t_{\text{d}}\)time; doubling timeh
Follow the derivation
  1. Growth proportional to the amount present: \(\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = rN\).
  2. Separate the variables and integrate: \(\ln N = \ln N_0 + rt\), so \(N = N_0 e^{rt}\).
  3. Doubling: set \(N = 2N_0\), so \(e^{r t_{\text{d}}} = 2\) and \(r\,t_{\text{d}} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693\).
Assumptions and validity

Only while nothing limits growth. Real cultures and crops slow down as substrate, space or light run out — which is why the logistic model (SG.5) and the Monod model of Lesson 12.3 exist. Example: a fungus that doubles every 4 h has \(r = 0.693/4 = 0.17\ \text{h}^{-1}\).

\(r\,t\): h⁻¹ × h is dimensionless, as the exponent must be ✓
Logarithms: the pH scale(SG.2)
\[ \text{pH} = -\log_{10}\,[\text{H}^{+}] \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad [\text{H}^{+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}} \]

pH is minus the base-ten logarithm of the hydrogen-ion concentration; one pH unit is a factor of ten in concentration.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\([\text{H}^{+}]\)hydrogen-ion concentration (strictly, activity)mol L⁻¹
pHacidity on a logarithmic scale–
Assumptions and validity

Uses concentration instead of activity, which is accurate in dilute solutions such as hydroponic nutrient solutions (Lesson 5.2). Remember the rules \(\log(ab) = \log a + \log b\) and \(\log a^{n} = n\log a\): a solution at pH 5.5 is \(10^{0.5} \approx 3.2\) times more acidic than one at pH 6.0.

the argument of a logarithm must be dimensionless: pH uses the concentration divided by 1 mol L⁻¹ ✓

Units and conversions

Most calculation errors in this course are unit errors: micromoles versus moles, hours versus seconds, ppm versus milligrams per cubic metre. Write the units with every number, and convert by multiplying with factors equal to one, such as \(3600\ \text{s} / 1\ \text{h}\). For gases, the ideal-gas law links a mole fraction (ppm) to a mass concentration.

From ppm to mass concentration(SG.3)
\[ c = \frac{x\,P\,M}{R\,T} \]

the mass concentration of a gas equals its mole fraction times the pressure times its molar mass, divided by the gas constant times the absolute temperature.

SymbolMeaningUnitExample
\(c\)mass concentrationg m⁻³0.73 for 400 ppm CO₂
\(x\)mole fraction (ppm × 10⁻⁶)mol mol⁻¹400 × 10⁻⁶
\(P\)air pressurePa101 325
\(M\)molar mass (CO₂)g mol⁻¹44.01
\(R\)molar gas constantJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹8.314
\(T\)absolute temperatureK293.15
Follow the derivation
  1. Ideal gas: the molar concentration of all gases is \(n/V = P/(RT)\) mol m⁻³.
  2. The gas of interest is the fraction \(x\) of all molecules: \(n_{\text{CO}_2}/V = xP/(RT)\).
  3. Multiply by the molar mass to convert moles to grams: \(c = xPM/(RT)\).
  4. Worked number: \(400\times10^{-6}\times 101\,325 \times 44.01 / (8.314\times 293.15) = 0.73\) g m⁻³ = 730 mg m⁻³.
\(\dfrac{\text{Pa}\cdot\text{g mol}^{-1}}{\text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}} = \dfrac{\text{N m}^{-2}\,\text{g}}{\text{N m}} = \text{g m}^{-3}\) ✓

Dimensional analysis

Every term of an equation must have the same units, and the arguments of exponentials and logarithms must be dimensionless. Checking this catches most errors before you calculate — that is why every equation box ends with a unit check. Some of the most useful quantities in engineering are dimensionless groups, whose units cancel completely; the Reynolds number tells you whether water flows smoothly (laminar) or chaotically (turbulent) in a hydroponic pipe (Lesson 5.4).

A dimensionless group: the Reynolds number(SG.4)
\[ \text{Re} = \frac{\rho\,v\,D}{\mu} \]

the Reynolds number is the fluid density times its velocity times the pipe diameter, divided by the dynamic viscosity — the ratio of inertial to viscous forces.

SymbolMeaningUnitWater, 20 °C
\(\rho\)densitykg m⁻³998
\(v\)mean flow velocitym s⁻¹0.1–2
\(D\)inner pipe diameterm0.02
\(\mu\)dynamic viscosityPa s = kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹1.0 × 10⁻³
Assumptions and validity

For flow in a full circular pipe, flow is usually laminar below \(\text{Re} \approx 2300\) and turbulent above about 4000, with a transition in between. The thresholds differ for open channels and other geometries.

\(\dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-3}\cdot\text{m s}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}}{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}} = \dfrac{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{kg m}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}} = 1\) — dimensionless ✓

Derivatives as rates

A derivative is a rate of change: how fast biomass accumulates, how quickly a sensor signal drifts, how steeply photosynthesis rises with light. Graphically it is the slope of the tangent. The logistic growth curve of Lesson 10.1 shows why the derivative carries information the curve itself hides: the crop grows fastest exactly half-way to its final size.

Logistic growth and its growth rate(SG.5)
\[ W(t) = \frac{K}{1 + e^{-r\,(t - t_0)}}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} = r\,W\left(1 - \frac{W}{K}\right) \]

the biomass follows an S-shaped curve towards the maximum K; its growth rate is the relative rate r times the biomass times the fraction of capacity still unused.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(W\)biomass (e.g. fresh weight per plant)g
\(K\)asymptotic (final) biomassg
\(r\)relative growth rate at the startd⁻¹
\(t_0\)time of the inflection point, where \(W = K/2\)d
Follow the derivation
  1. Write \(u = e^{-r(t-t_0)}\), so \(W = K/(1+u)\) and \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = -r u\).
  2. Chain rule: \(\mathrm{d}W/\mathrm{d}t = -K(1+u)^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = rKu/(1+u)^{2}\).
  3. Since \(W/K = 1/(1+u)\) and \(1 - W/K = u/(1+u)\), this equals \(rW(1 - W/K)\).
  4. The rate is largest where \(W(1-W/K)\) is largest, at \(W = K/2\) (\(t = t_0\)): \((\mathrm{d}W/\mathrm{d}t)_{\max} = rK/4\).
\(\text{d}^{-1}\times\text{g}\times\) dimensionless = g d⁻¹, a rate ✓

Integrals as accumulation

An integral adds up a rate over time — the area under its curve. The daily light integral is literally that: the photon flux, summed over the hours of the day (Lesson 4.2). On an idealised clear day the photon flux rises and falls like half a sine wave, and the integral has a neat closed form.

Daily light integral of a sine-shaped day(SG.6)
\[ \text{DLI} = \frac{3600}{10^{6}}\int_{0}^{N} P_{\max}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{N}\right)\mathrm{d}t = \frac{2\,P_{\max}\,N\cdot 3600}{\pi\cdot 10^{6}} \]

the daily light integral is the area under the photon-flux curve over the day length, converted from micromoles per second times hours to moles; for a half-sine day it equals two over pi times the peak flux times the day length.

SymbolMeaningUnit
\(\text{DLI}\)daily light integralmol m⁻² d⁻¹
\(P_{\max}\)photon flux density at solar noonµmol m⁻² s⁻¹
\(N\)day lengthh
\(t\)hours since sunriseh
Follow the derivation
  1. Antiderivative: \(\int \sin(\pi t/N)\,\mathrm{d}t = -(N/\pi)\cos(\pi t/N)\).
  2. Evaluate from 0 to \(N\): \(-(N/\pi)(\cos\pi - \cos 0) = 2N/\pi\) hours.
  3. Multiply by \(P_{\max}\), by 3600 s h⁻¹ and divide by \(10^{6}\) µmol mol⁻¹.
  4. The average over the day is \(2/\pi \approx 0.64\) of the noon value. Worked number: \(P_{\max} = 1500\), \(N = 18\) h gives \(2\times1500\times18\times3600/(\pi\times10^{6}) = 61.9\) mol m⁻² d⁻¹.
Assumptions and validity

An idealised cloudless day with a sine-shaped course of irradiance. Real days are cloudier and the shape is flatter at high latitudes; the sun-path laboratory integrates the real solar geometry numerically.

µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ × h × s h⁻¹ ÷ µmol mol⁻¹ = mol m⁻² (per day) ✓

Accessibility and different ways of studying

The course is built to be usable with a keyboard, a screen reader, a magnifier or a printout. Every laboratory presents its results as numbers, charts and text as well as in 3D; lessons can be printed with all hidden solutions expanded (“Print lesson”); the site follows your operating system’s dark mode and reduced-motion settings. If something does not work for you, tell your teacher — the accessibility statement explains what is known, what alternatives exist and how to report a problem. If you have a disability or study-related needs, contact your university’s study-support service early: support such as extended exam time has to be arranged in advance.

Choose your learning path

Everyone studies all fifteen modules and is assessed on all seven learning outcomes. The three paths suggest where to go deeper — which optional laboratories to finish, which readings to add and which angle to take in your project — depending on what drives you. The bar under each path shows how many of its lessons you have marked as studied in this browser.

Paths and the project

Groups work best when their members follow different paths: the grower keeps the plants alive, the data engineer owns the sensors and the statistics, the systems analyst leads the sensory study and the sustainability discussion — and everyone understands all three.
